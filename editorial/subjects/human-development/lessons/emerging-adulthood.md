# Emerging Adulthood

## In 30 Seconds

Emerging adulthood is the developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties, when people are legally adults but have not yet settled into adult roles. Psychologist Jeffrey Arnett named the stage and argued that it is new: since the 1960s, longer education and later marriage and parenthood created years that earlier generations lacked. Its five features are identity explorations, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and possibilities. The honest catch: it is a stage of some societies, not all, and people experience it very differently.

## Why This Matters

The years between "old enough to vote" and "settled into adult life" now stretch across most of the twenties, and they shape real decisions: when to study, work, move, marry, and start a family. Arnett's five features give you a vocabulary for that stretch — why a twenty-two-year-old changes cities twice in one year (instability), why a friend says she feels "sort of adult" (feeling in-between), why the future still feels wide open (possibilities). The concept also keeps you honest: it is not a law of nature, it does not apply everywhere or to everyone equally, and no path through it is more legitimate than another. Emerging adulthood is the runway many people use before takeoff — and understanding it means understanding the decade most of your classmates are living through right now.

## The College Version

### The working definition

Emerging adulthood is the developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties, when people are legally adults but have not yet settled into adult roles. Psychologist Jeffrey Arnett gave the stage its name, and the working definition used in lifespan development follows his account: researchers commonly describe it as the years from 18 to 29. The OpenStax Lifespan Development textbook places it as the first phase of early adulthood, when individuals are in the process of becoming an adult and establishing a fully adult life. The legal markers of adulthood arrive early in this stretch — the right to vote, drive, drink alcohol, and marry. The settled markers — a stable career, a household of one's own, marriage and children, financial independence — typically arrive years later. Emerging adulthood is the gap between those two sets of markers, and the concept exists because that gap is now long and structured enough to count as a stage of its own, with its own tasks and its own feel.

### Why it is new

Arnett and colleagues describe emerging adulthood as a new life stage. Since about 1960, demographic trends — longer time spent in education and later entry into marriage and parenthood — created a stretch of years that earlier generations did not have as a distinct phase of life. The numbers make the point concrete. In the United States, the median age at first marriage was about 21 for women and 23 for men in 1970; by the 2020s it had risen to the late twenties for women and about 30 for men (U.S. Census Bureau). Original example: in 1970, a twenty-four-year-old woman named Ruth was married, had a toddler, and had been working since high school; her path from school to family was short. Today her granddaughter Nina is twenty-four, finishing a master's degree, living with two roommates, and single. Neither path is better or worse — the point is that Nina's decade of becoming, with its explorations, moves, and do-overs, is the new stage the concept names. Many countries recognize these years as a buffer period before full adulthood, during which young people may study, train, work, or travel before adult roles lock in.

### The five features

Arnett's theory gives emerging adulthood five defining features. Identity explorations: emerging adults keep working out who they are in work and love, refining their beliefs and values. Instability: the years are full of change — new jobs, new homes, new relationships — partly because young adults are often the first hired and the first let go. Self-focus: with fewer obligations to a spouse or children, emerging adults can concentrate on becoming self-sufficient. Feeling in-between: though legally adults, many do not yet feel fully adult and consult family and friends before big decisions. Possibilities: the future still feels open, and many emerging adults report optimism about what lies ahead, with room to leave behind parts of their early lives. Each feature is one thread of the same stage: exploration feeds change, change keeps life self-focused, and the whole experience sits between adolescence and settled adulthood, full of possibility.

### The honest note

The five features are a description, not a promise. Research shows that people experience emerging adulthood regardless of their circumstances, but differently: young adults with fewer economic resources often have less room for identity explorations and less reason for optimism. A young person who is the family's main earner may move between short-term jobs rather than sample careers, and may delay or skip milestones such as moving out on their own. The features are typical patterns, not requirements — no one who misses one of them is failing the stage, and the stage itself looks different depending on who is living it.

### The cultural caveat and the honest framing

Emerging adulthood is a stage of some societies, not all. It took shape in post-industrial, high-income societies, where extended education and delayed family formation are common, and the textbook is direct that some transitions associated with early adulthood may not apply to all cultures, and that the experiences of people in this age range vary significantly. The honest framing: think of emerging adulthood as the runway before takeoff. A runway is not the flight — it is where speed and readiness are built — and it is not the same length for every plane. Some people get a long, smooth runway; others get a short one, or none at all. The concept helps us see the runway; it does not grade who uses it best.

## Key Vocabulary

- **emerging adulthood** — The developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties, when people hold legal adult status but have not yet settled into adult roles.
- **new life stage** — A recognized developmental phase that did not exist for earlier generations; Arnett uses the term for emerging adulthood.
- **identity explorations** — Arnett's feature describing young adults' continued efforts to learn who they are in work and relationships and to firm up their beliefs and values.
- **instability** — Arnett's feature describing the frequent change in jobs, homes, and relationships that marks emerging adulthood.
- **self-focus** — Arnett's feature describing the period when, with few obligations to others, young adults concentrate on becoming self-sufficient.
- **feeling in-between** — Arnett's feature describing the sense of being legally but not fully adult, often shown by consulting family and friends on major decisions.
- **age of possibilities** — Arnett's feature describing the optimism of emerging adulthood, when the future feels open and life can be redirected.
- **adult roles** — The settled social positions of full adulthood, such as stable work, marriage, parenthood, and an independent household, whose timing defines emerging adulthood.

## Eli-10

Ask a room of twenty-three-year-olds whether they feel like adults, and you will get a lot of "sort of." That is the heart of emerging adulthood: the years from about 18 to the late twenties, when you can legally do almost everything but do not yet have the settled life most people picture for adulthood — a stable career, your own place, a family. Psychologist Jeffrey Arnett named this stage and gave it five features: exploring who you are in work and love; a great deal of change in jobs, homes, and relationships; being focused mainly on your own path; feeling in-between, adult and not adult; and feeling that possibilities are still open. It is historically new, and it belongs to some societies rather than all — in many places, young people move straight from school into adult roles.

## Eli's Analogy

Emerging adulthood is the runway before takeoff. The plane sits at the gate, fully built and cleared for flight — that is legal adulthood, every right and responsibility in place. But a plane on the runway is not yet flying: it is accelerating, adjusting, and gathering what it needs to lift off. That is the emerging adult — legally airborne on paper, actually still gaining speed. The five features are the preflight checklist: exploring routes (identity explorations), changing runways (instability), watching your own instruments (self-focus), not quite feeling airborne (feeling in-between), and knowing the sky is open (possibilities). Takeoff is the arrival of settled adult roles — and it happens at a different time for every flight.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** Runways are standardized and every plane uses one the same way; emerging adulthood is neither. Not everyone gets a runway: in many societies and circumstances, young people move straight from school into work, marriage, and parenthood with little room for exploration. A runway also has a fixed length, while the stage's duration varies enormously, and a plane that lingers on the runway is wasting fuel — extra years of exploration are not wasted. Finally, the analogy suggests one destination, but adult life has many routes.

## Worked Example

Leo is twenty-three and could check off Arnett's five features in a single week. On Monday he interviews for a job in a city he would have to move to (instability); on Wednesday he tells his sister he is "not really an adult yet" (feeling in-between); on Friday he declines a group trip because he is saving for his own apartment (self-focus); all month he keeps testing whether to stay in event planning or retrain as a nurse (identity explorations); and he tells his roommate he still believes he can end up somewhere great (possibilities). Not every emerging adult ticks all five boxes at once — Leo's cousin Maya, who supports her parents and works nights, has far less room for exploration — but the features give us a language for the decade between legal adulthood and a settled life.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Treating emerging adulthood as a fancy word for 'being young and irresponsible.'"** — It is a structural description, not a verdict: a distinct developmental stage between adolescence and settled adulthood, created by later education, marriage, and parenthood. It describes a period of life, not anyone's character.
- **"Assuming the stage runs from 18 to 25 and ends on a fixed birthday."** — The working definition is about age 18 to the late twenties, and researchers commonly write ages 18 to 29. There is no exact cutoff; the stage ends when adult roles actually arrive.
- **"Believing everyone between 18 and 29 experiences the five features equally."** — Research shows experience varies with circumstances: people with fewer economic resources often have less room for exploration and less optimism about possibilities. The features are typical patterns, not universal requirements.
- **"Thinking emerging adulthood is a universal stage that happens everywhere."** — It is a stage of some societies — those with extended education and delayed adult roles. In many societies young people move directly from adolescence into adult roles, and even within post-industrial countries experience varies by culture and situation.
- **"Confusing emerging adulthood with adolescence."** — Adolescence ends in the teenage years and is tied to puberty and schooling; emerging adulthood follows, spans the late teens through the twenties, and is marked by legal adulthood without settled adult roles.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Emerging adulthood vs. Adolescence:** Both involve identity explorations and change, but adolescence is anchored in puberty and secondary school and typically ends in the late teens, while emerging adulthood follows with full legal status but unsettled adult roles.
- **Emerging adulthood vs. Early adulthood:** Emerging adulthood is the first phase of early adulthood — the years of becoming; early adulthood as a whole extends through the twenties and thirties and includes settled careers, relationships, and households, which the early-adulthood topic owns in depth.
- **Feeling in-between vs. Being legally adult:** The two can sit side by side: legal adulthood grants rights such as voting, drinking, and marrying, while feeling in-between describes the subjective sense of not yet being a full adult — the stage is defined by the gap between them.

## Key Takeaway

Emerging adulthood is the developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties — a historically recent stage of some societies, mapped by Arnett's five features, experienced differently by different people, and best understood as the runway before takeoff.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Researchers describe emerging adulthood as which of the following?**
   - A. The developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties, when people are legally adults but have not yet settled into adult roles
   - B. The teenage years between puberty and the eighteenth birthday
   - C. The years after retirement, when adults review their lives and achievements
   - D. Any stage of life in which a person changes jobs or homes

   **Answer: A.** The working definition, following Arnett, is the developmental period from about age 18 to the late twenties (researchers commonly write 18 to 29), when legal adulthood is achieved but adult roles are not yet settled. Option B describes adolescence, option C describes late adulthood, and option D is far too broad to be a definition.

2. **Twenty-three-year-old Marcus finished his degree in May, moved back in with his parents to pay down student loans, and tells his cousin that he can vote and rent a car but still does not feel like a real adult. Which of Arnett's five features does Marcus's feeling best illustrate?**
   - A. Identity explorations
   - B. Instability
   - C. Feeling in-between
   - D. Self-focus

   **Answer: C.** Marcus is legally adult yet does not feel fully adult — exactly Arnett's feature of feeling in-between, described in the textbook as the state of emerging adults who, though legally adults, are not yet comfortable making some decisions on their own. Identity explorations would involve trying out careers or values, instability would involve frequent change, and self-focus would involve concentrating on self-sufficiency.

3. **Arnett and colleagues describe emerging adulthood as a new life stage. Which statement best explains what makes it new?**
   - A. Young people today reach physical maturity several years earlier than past generations did
   - B. Since about 1960, longer time in education and later marriage and parenthood created a stretch of years between adolescence and settled adult life
   - C. The voting age in many countries was lowered, making eighteen-year-olds full citizens
   - D. Modern technology lets young people study and work from anywhere, so they leave home earlier

   **Answer: B.** Arnett and colleagues trace the stage to demographic trends since about 1960: longer time in education and later entry into marriage and parenthood, which created a distinct stretch of years that earlier generations did not have. Physical maturation timing, voting-age law, and remote work do not explain why the stage is historically new.

4. **Which list correctly names all five of Arnett's features of emerging adulthood?**
   - A. Identity explorations, stability, other-focus, feeling grown-up, limitations
   - B. Career choice, marriage, parenting, home ownership, retirement planning
   - C. Trust, autonomy, initiative, industry, identity
   - D. Identity explorations, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, possibilities

   **Answer: D.** Arnett's five features are identity explorations, instability, self-focus, feeling in-between, and possibilities (the textbook calls the last one being in an age of possibilities). Option A reverses or swaps the features, option B lists adult milestones rather than features, and option C lists Erikson's first five psychosocial stages.

5. **Rosa, age 22, works full time and supports her younger siblings, with little time to sample different careers; her cousin Daniel, also 22, spends two years trying jobs and volunteer roles before choosing a path. Which conclusion is best supported by research on emerging adulthood?**
   - A. Both are within the emerging-adulthood age range, but their experiences of the stage differ with their circumstances, such as financial and family responsibilities
   - B. Daniel is in emerging adulthood but Rosa is not, because her responsibilities make her fully adult
   - C. Rosa skipped emerging adulthood, so she will never experience identity explorations
   - D. Emerging adulthood is a universal stage that everyone from 18 to 29 experiences in the same way

   **Answer: A.** The textbook reports that people seem to experience emerging adulthood regardless of socioeconomic status, but differently: fewer resources and heavier responsibilities can mean less room for identity explorations and less optimism about possibilities. Options B and C wrongly treat responsibility as removing a person from the age range, and option D contradicts the documented variation in how the stage is experienced.
