# Language Development

## In 30 Seconds

Language development is the process by which children acquire their first language — learning the sounds, words, and rules of the language spoken around them. Most children follow a familiar sequence: cooing, babbling, first words around the first birthday, then two-word "telegraphic" phrases. These are typical ranges with plenty of variation, not deadlines. Language comes easiest in the early years — a window, not a cliff. Chomsky saw an inborn capacity; learning accounts stress experience. And growing up with two languages is common and normal worldwide.

## Why This Matters

Language is the mind's first great achievement: within about three years, a child who cannot say a word becomes a speaker who asks questions, tells stories, and argues about bedtimes. Understanding how that happens helps parents, teachers, and health workers tell ordinary variation from something worth checking, and it reveals a central puzzle of development — how so much skill arrives so fast. The nature-nurture debate in language also models how scientists argue productively about human development, and knowing that bilingualism is normal worldwide keeps the conversation honest.

## Learning Objectives

- Define language development as the process by which children acquire their first language.
- Describe the typical milestone sequence — cooing, babbling, first words, telegraphic speech — as typical ranges with variation, not deadlines.
- Explain the critical-period idea and why it is a window of ease rather than a hard cutoff.
- Distinguish Chomsky's innate-capacity theory from learning accounts of language acquisition, as theories.
- Apply knowledge of typical language milestones and typical bilingual development to a concrete child's situation.

## The College Version

### What Language Development Is

In developmental terms, language development is the process by which children acquire their first language: learning the sounds, the words, and the rules of the language spoken around them. The National Institute on Deafness and Other Communication Disorders (NIDCD) describes language as a set of shared rules that let people express ideas, and the acquiring of it as a natural progression that begins at birth. OpenStax's Lifespan Development text calls language development a complex process influenced by both nature and nurture. The first signs of communication arrive weeks after birth: a cry that brings food and comfort teaches an infant that sounds move other people. From that start, the road to full sentences is remarkably fast — and remarkably consistent across children, even as the timing varies.

### The Milestone Sequence: Cooing to Telegraphic Speech

Researchers and health agencies describe early language as a sequence of typical milestones, each a range rather than a fixed date. NIDCD's checklist of speech and language milestones runs from birth to age five; OpenStax summarizes the first two years. Cooing comes first: by about two months, most infants make vowel-like sounds — "oooo," "ah" — as practice vocalizations, often in back-and-forth exchanges with a caregiver. Babbling follows around four to six months: strings of consonant-vowel syllables such as "mama" and "baba." Early babbling is sound play, not yet meaning — caregivers often mistake it for real words, but the infant has not tied sounds to meaning. Around the first birthday, most children have their first recognizable words, often names for familiar people and things: "mama," "dada," "dog." Some say a first word as early as nine months, and NIDCD counts one or two words by the first birthday as typical. Between about eighteen and twenty-four months, children start combining words into short, telegram-like phrases — "more cookie," "go papa house" — called telegraphic speech, which carries meaning without full grammar. Vocabulary then expands rapidly: roughly fifty to two hundred words by age two, and up to about a thousand words by age three.

### The Critical-Period Idea: A Window, Not a Cliff

One of the most striking facts about language is how early it comes and how quickly the window of ease begins to close. NIDCD states that the first three years of life — when the brain is developing and maturing — are the most intensive period for acquiring speech and language skills, and that there appear to be critical periods when the brain is best able to absorb language. If those periods pass without exposure, NIDCD says, language becomes more difficult to learn. Infants begin as universal listeners, able to distinguish the sounds of any language; by ten to twelve months, their brains specialize in the sounds they actually hear. The honest note: this is a window, not a cliff. Adults learn new languages all the time — it takes more effort and rarely ends in a perfect accent, but it plainly happens. Early exposure makes acquisition easier and more natural; it does not make later learning impossible.

### The Nature-Nurture Debate: Chomsky vs. Learning Accounts

Why does every child, in every culture, become a fluent speaker within a few years — with no formal grammar lessons? The classic debate has two poles. Noam Chomsky's nativist theory proposed that humans are born with an innate capacity for language, which he called a Language Acquisition Device (LAD): a language-ready brain that lets children analyze the speech around them and extract its rules quickly. B.F. Skinner's learning account argued the opposite: language is acquired the way other behaviors are, through operant conditioning — caregivers reinforce an infant's random sounds with attention and smiles, so rewarded sounds get repeated — along with imitation of others. Both are theories, and neither alone explains the evidence. Contemporary interactionist accounts combine them: children arrive with biological readiness for language, and that readiness is activated by rich, responsive social interaction — which is why OpenStax notes that toddlers learn vocabulary better from face-to-face conversation than from screens.

### Bilingualism: Two Languages, Perfectly Ordinary

Many children grow up hearing two or more languages, and OpenStax's Lifespan Development is direct about it: children in multilingual environments learn and use multiple languages naturally. The process differs a little from single-language learning — bilingual children may mix languages within a sentence, and they may temporarily have a smaller vocabulary in each language — and none of that signals confusion. With continued exposure and practice, children become fluent in both, and early exposure is the greatest advantage; what matters is regular exposure and support for each language. The honest note: bilingualism is common and normal worldwide. In many countries, children routinely grow up with two languages as the ordinary family experience rather than an unusual achievement, and no evidence shows that learning two languages at once holds a child back.

### The Reality Check: Every Child's Path

Every child's path through language is a little different. One toddler speaks in three-word sentences at twenty months while a playmate says single words — and both can be typical. Milestone lists are descriptions of what most children do by a given age, not exams with deadlines; NIDCD stresses that children vary in their development while following a natural timetable, and a single missed checkmark is not a diagnosis. When a child seems clearly behind in several areas, the right step is a conversation with a pediatrician, because early help works best. The honest framing: language is the mind's first great achievement. In about three years, a newborn who communicates by crying becomes a person who asks questions, tells stories, and negotiates — a transformation so universal we rarely stop to marvel at it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Language development** — The process by which children acquire their first language — learning the sounds, words, and rules of the language spoken around them (working definition synthesized from NIDCD and OpenStax).
- **Cooing** — The vowel-like sounds, such as "oooo" and "ah," that infants begin making by about 2 months as early practice vocalizations, often in back-and-forth exchanges with caregivers.
- **Babbling** — Repeated consonant-vowel strings such as "mama" and "baba," typically beginning around 4 to 6 months; early babbling is sound play without meaning, not yet real words.
- **First words** — The first meaningful single words, often names for familiar people and things such as "mama," "dada," or "dog," which typically appear around the first birthday (some children as early as 9 months).
- **Telegraphic speech** — Short two-word combinations such as "more cookie" that carry meaning without full grammar, typically appearing between about 18 and 24 months.
- **Critical period** — A developmental window in early childhood when the brain is best able to absorb language, making acquisition easier and more natural; in this lesson, a window of ease rather than a hard cutoff (per NIDCD).
- **Language Acquisition Device (LAD)** — Noam Chomsky's proposed innate capacity for language — a language-ready brain that lets children extract the rules of the language around them quickly; a theory, not a discovered organ.
- **Bilingualism** — The ability to use two languages, common and normal worldwide; children in multilingual environments learn both naturally, with early mixing and uneven per-language vocabulary as typical patterns.

## Eli-10

Language development is how a baby turns into a talker. Nobody sits a one-year-old down with a grammar book; children just absorb the language around them, in a predictable order — cooing, babbling, first words, then two-word phrases — each step a typical range, not a due date. The early years are the easiest time to do this: the young brain is built for absorbing language, which is why babies learn faster than most adults. There is a long-running argument about why: some researchers say we are born with a language instinct, others that we learn by watching and being rewarded. Both sides are partly right, and most modern researchers combine them. And a child hearing two languages from birth simply learns two — one of the most ordinary things a human brain does.

## Eli's Analogy

Learning a first language is like a sponge dropped into a bucket of water: the sponge soaks up whatever liquid it sits in — Spanish, English, or both at once — without choosing, straining, or needing the water poured in drop by drop. It just needs to stay in the water regularly and long enough.

Where the analogy breaks down: a sponge soaks passively, but a child listens actively — babies sort sounds, test syllables, and practice words on purpose, and they learn better from a real, responsive person than from a screen. A sponge absorbs everything equally, while a child gradually narrows to the sounds of her own languages. And a sponge left out of water forever stays dry, but a person who misses the early window can still learn a language later in life — it is harder, not impossible.

## Worked Example

Noor is twenty months old and the center of a small family debate. She says about forty words, has just started combining them — "more milk," "go park" — and calls every small furry animal "kitty," including the neighbor's cat-sized dog. Her grandmother, who speaks Urdu with Noor at home, worries that the English she hears at daycare is confusing her; her aunt points out that Noor's cousin said a hundred words at this age. Reading Noor against the typical ranges: two-word combinations at eighteen to twenty-four months are exactly the telegraphic-speech milestone, forty words sits inside the roughly fifty-to-two-hundred-word range for age two, and "kitty" for all furry animals is overextension — a normal word-learning error, not a mistake. The mixing is not confusion; it is a bilingual child's ordinary pattern, and continued exposure to both Urdu and English is all she needs.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating milestone ages as deadlines** — a child who has no words at twelve months is automatically behind. Correction: first words are typical around twelve months, but NIDCD stresses that children vary in their development while following a natural timetable, and milestones describe what most children do, not every child. A single missing checkmark is not a diagnosis; a conversation with a pediatrician is the right step when several areas lag.
- **Hearing early babbling as real words** — "baba" at six months must mean "bottle." Correction: early babbling is sound play without meaning; infants typically tie sounds to meaning only near the first birthday, when first words like "mama" or "dada" name specific people and things (OpenStax 3.5).
- **Believing bilingualism confuses children** — learning two languages at once will slow or scramble a child's speech. Correction: children in multilingual environments learn both languages naturally; early mixing and a temporarily smaller vocabulary in each language are normal, and fluency follows continued exposure (OpenStax 5.4).
- **Treating the critical period as a hard cutoff** — if a child misses early exposure, language can never be learned. Correction: NIDCD says language becomes more difficult to learn after the early critical periods pass — harder, not impossible. Adults learn languages throughout life; the early window is a period of ease, not the only chance.
- **Treating the Language Acquisition Device as a proven organ** — "Chomsky discovered the language organ in the brain." Correction: the LAD is a hypothesis — Chomsky's proposed innate capacity for language — not a discovered structure; modern accounts combine biological readiness with social experience (OpenStax 3.5).

## Compare / Contrast

- **Cooing vs. babbling** — Cooing (around 2 months) is vowel-like practice sound such as "oooo"; babbling (around 4 to 6 months) adds consonant-vowel strings like "mama" and "baba." Neither carries meaning yet.
- **First words vs. telegraphic speech** — First words (around 12 months) are single meaningful names for people and things; telegraphic speech (around 18 to 24 months) strings words into short phrases like "more cookie" without full grammar.
- **Chomsky's nativist account vs. Skinner's learning account** — One proposes an innate, language-ready brain (the LAD); the other holds that language is acquired through reinforcement of sounds and imitation of others. Both are theories, and modern accounts combine them.
- **A critical period vs. a hard deadline** — A critical period is a window when language comes most easily; a deadline is a cutoff after which learning is impossible. NIDCD's position is a window: language is harder to learn later, never impossible.

## Key Takeaway

Language development is the process by which children acquire their first language, following a typical sequence — cooing, babbling, first words, telegraphic speech — best understood as ranges with variation: a window of ease rather than a cliff, shaped by both innate capacity and experience, and perfectly capable of handling two languages at once.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In developmental psychology, language development is best described as which of the following?**
   - A. The process by which children acquire their first language — its sounds, words, and rules
   - B. The study of how adults memorize vocabulary for a second language
   - C. The change in a child's vocal cords during the preschool years
   - D. The set of grammar rules printed in a dictionary
   - **Answer: A.** The working definition for this lesson — synthesized from NIDCD (language as shared rules for expressing ideas) and OpenStax (language development as a complex process influenced by nature and nurture) — is the process by which children acquire their first language. The other options describe adult second-language study, physical voice change, and dictionary content, none of which captures first-language acquisition in childhood.

2. **A toddler who says "more cookie" and "go papa house" is using what kind of speech?**
   - A. Overextended grammar
   - B. Holophrastic babbling
   - C. Telegraphic speech
   - D. Child-directed speech
   - **Answer: C.** OpenStax defines telegraphic speech as short two-word combinations — "more cookie," "Go papa house" — that carry meaning without relying on grammar, typically appearing between about 18 and 24 months. Overextension is a word-naming error (calling a tiger "kitty"), holophrases are single words standing for whole thoughts, and child-directed speech is the simplified talk caregivers use with infants.

3. **Amara is thirteen months old. She babbles, understands "cup" and "shoe," and said her first word, "Dada," two weeks ago. A neighbor's baby said three words at eleven months. Based on typical milestone ranges, which reading is accurate?**
   - A. Amara is behind, because most children say three words by eleven months
   - B. Amara is on track — first words typically appear around twelve months, and thirteen months is within the typical range
   - C. Amara should already be combining words like "more cookie" by thirteen months
   - D. Amara's babbling shows her first word was not a real word
   - **Answer: B.** NIDCD counts one or two words by the first birthday as typical, and OpenStax puts first words around twelve months (some children as early as nine). Understanding words like "cup" and "shoe" is also a listed 7-months-to-1-year skill, so Amara sits inside the typical ranges. Two-word combinations arrive later, around 18 to 24 months, and early babbling does not cancel out a real first word.

4. **Noam Chomsky proposed that humans are born with a Language Acquisition Device, while B.F. Skinner argued that language is learned through reinforcement and imitation. Which statement best captures the difference between the two theories?**
   - A. Chomsky and Skinner both held that language is entirely learned from the environment
   - B. Skinner proposed the innate device, while Chomsky emphasized reinforcement
   - C. Chomsky studied signed languages only, while Skinner studied spoken languages only
   - D. Chomsky emphasized an innate capacity for language, while Skinner emphasized learning from experience
   - **Answer: D.** OpenStax presents Chomsky's nativist theory — an innate, uniquely human Language Acquisition Device — against Skinner's learning account, in which caregivers reinforce infants' sounds and children imitate others. Option D captures that contrast; the others reverse the positions or invent claims the sources do not make. Both are theories, and modern interactionist accounts combine biology with social experience.

5. **Diego's family speaks Spanish at home and his preschool uses English. At age three, Diego sometimes mixes the two languages in one sentence and knows fewer English words than his monolingual classmates. What is the accurate assessment?**
   - A. The two languages are confusing Diego, and he should focus on just one
   - B. Diego's pattern is typical of bilingual children — mixing and uneven per-language vocabularies early are normal, and fluency grows with continued exposure
   - C. Diego has missed the critical period for learning two languages
   - D. Bilingual children permanently know fewer words than monolingual children
   - **Answer: B.** OpenStax's Lifespan Development notes that children in multilingual environments learn multiple languages naturally; they may mix languages or have a smaller vocabulary in each at first, and with continued exposure and practice they become fluent. The critical period concerns ease of early learning, not a cutoff, and it applies to acquiring languages young — Diego is not "past" anything at age three.
