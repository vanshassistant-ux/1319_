# Motivation and Learning

## In 30 Seconds

Motivation is not one dial that turns students up or down. Research treats it as several separable judgments: whether you expect to succeed, whether the task is worth what it costs, whose reason you are acting on, what you think caused your last result, and what you are counting as success. Self-determination theory, situated expectancy-value theory, attribution theory, and achievement goal theory each formalize one of those judgments, and each points at a different classroom move.

## Why This Matters

Every teaching decision - how much choice to offer, how to word feedback, whether to grade on a curve, whether to pay students for reading - is a bet about what drives effort. Motivation research turns those bets into testable claims, and not all of the claims survive testing. Two of the field's most repeated ideas, that rewards poison interest and that a short mindset lesson lifts achievement, are contested in ways popular summaries rarely report. Reading the actual effect sizes, including the null results, is what separates a teacher who designs incentives deliberately from one who inherits a classroom ritual and never asks what it is doing. The vocabulary here also underwrites later work on assessment, engagement, and self-regulated learning.

## Learning Objectives

- Define motivation in achievement settings in terms of direction, intensity, and persistence rather than as a single quantity.
- Explain why the intrinsic/extrinsic binary is too crude, using self-determination theory's continuum of regulation and its three basic psychological needs.
- Apply situated expectancy-value theory and attribution theory to diagnose why a specific student has disengaged.
- Distinguish mastery from performance goals, including approach and avoidance forms, and explain why measurement choices complicate the comparison.
- Evaluate what the evidence actually shows about extrinsic rewards and about growth mindset interventions, using reported effect sizes rather than slogans.

## The College Version

### Motivation is a set of judgments, not a quantity

Calling a student unmotivated rarely survives contact with the student. The ninth grader who will not start a lab report may spend four hours on a drum part or a fantasy league roster, so the useful question is not how much motivation exists but where it points and why. Achievement motivation research therefore separates three things a theory has to predict: direction, meaning which task gets chosen; intensity, meaning how much effort goes in; and persistence, meaning what happens after the first bad result. A theory earns its place by predicting all three from something you can observe or change.

Four families of theory dominate the field, and they are complements rather than rivals. Self-determination theory asks why the student is doing the task. Expectancy-value theory asks whether the student expects to succeed and what the task is worth against what it costs. Attribution theory asks what the student believes caused the last outcome. Achievement goal theory asks what the student is treating as success in the first place. Self-efficacy - a person's judgment of their own capability on a specific task - runs underneath all four; it is developed in the social learning theory lesson, and here it matters mainly as the engine behind expectancy for success.

### Why intrinsic versus extrinsic is too crude

Intrinsic motivation means doing something because the activity itself is interesting or satisfying. Extrinsic motivation means doing it for some outcome separable from the activity. Ryan and Deci's objection to stopping there is that extrinsic reasons differ enormously in how much they feel like the student's own, so a single label hides the variable that actually predicts behavior.

Self-determination theory arranges extrinsic reasons on a continuum of internalization. External regulation is compliance to get a reward or dodge a penalty; the reason lives outside the student. Introjected regulation is a rule swallowed but not digested: the student works to avoid guilt or shame or to protect self-worth, so the pressure is now internal without being freely chosen. Identified regulation is endorsement; the student may still find the task dull but genuinely values what it leads to. Integrated regulation is identification that sits consistently with the person's other values and sense of self.

The continuum is not decoration. A meta-analysis of 344 samples covering 223,209 students found that these types behave differently. Intrinsic motivation related to success and well-being. Identified regulation was especially strongly related to persistence. Introjected regulation predicted persistence and performance goals but also indicators of ill-being. External regulation was not associated with performance or persistence at all, and was associated with reduced well-being. One word, extrinsic, collapses at least three distinct patterns.

### The three basic needs

Self-determination theory explains the continuum with three basic psychological needs. Autonomy is experiencing your own action as self-endorsed rather than coerced. It is not permissiveness, and it is not a menu: a requirement whose rationale a student accepts can support autonomy, while a choice among three trivial options usually does not. Competence is the sense that you can meet the demand, supported by work pitched near the edge of current ability and by feedback that says what to change. Relatedness is being connected to and mattering to the people in the room.

Meeting those needs is how a reason moves along the continuum from external toward identified. The standard classroom translation, as the open educational psychology literature states it, is to offer genuine choices, calibrate difficulty, give timely feedback, and build cooperative work. The competence-support half of this has federal backing of a modest kind: the 2007 IES practice guide on encouraging girls in math and science recommends prescriptive, informational feedback - telling a student specifically what to do differently rather than only how they did - and rates that recommendation as supported by moderate evidence.

### Expectancy, value, and cost

Eccles, Wigfield, and colleagues have spent forty years on two questions: can I do this, and do I want to? Expectancy for success is a student's belief about how well they will do on an upcoming task; ability beliefs are the related judgment about current competence. Subjective task value breaks into attainment value, whether doing this well matters to who the student is; intrinsic value, whether they enjoy it; utility value, whether it gets them something they want; and cost. Cost has done increasing work in recent versions of the theory: time surrendered, effort required, anxiety incurred, and the other things not done instead. A student can rate a course as genuinely valuable and still walk away because the cost column is heavy.

In 2020 Eccles and Wigfield renamed the framework situated expectancy-value theory. The rename added no new machinery. Its point was to stop readers treating the familiar box-and-arrow diagram as static, linear, and monolithic, and to foreground how expectancies and values develop inside a particular situation and cultural background, shaped by the messages students receive from teachers, families, and peers, and by how students interpret their own past performance. Read that way, the claim that a student does not value mathematics turns into a question about what her setting has been telling her, and for how long.

### Attributions: what the student thinks caused it

After a result arrives, students explain it. Weiner's attributional theory sorts those explanations by three dimensions rather than by content. Locus asks whether the cause is inside the person - ability, effort, strategy - or outside, such as task difficulty, luck, or a grader. Stability asks whether the cause will still be there next time. Controllability asks whether the person can influence it.

The dimensions predict what happens next better than the label does. I failed because I am not a math person is internal, stable, and uncontrollable, the combination that most reliably ends persistence, because nothing about the next attempt looks any different. I failed because I started the night before is internal, unstable, and controllable, which leaves a move on the board. Roughly, stability governs expectancy for the next attempt while controllability governs emotional responses and how others react.

Attribution retraining follows from this and is easy to do badly. Redirecting a student toward effort helps only when effort actually would have changed the outcome. If the real cause was a missing prerequisite or a poor strategy, try harder is both false and demoralizing, which is why the open literature pairs attribution work with appropriately difficult tasks, individual help, and the message that ability itself grows incrementally.

### Goal orientation, and a measurement problem

Achievement goal theory asks what a student is treating as success. Mastery goals define it as understanding the material or improving on your own past performance. Performance goals define it relative to other people. Elliot and McGregor crossed that distinction with approach and avoidance to give four types: mastery-approach, pursuing understanding; mastery-avoidance, trying not to fail to learn or not to lose a skill; performance-approach, trying to outdo others; and performance-avoidance, trying not to look incompetent. Performance-avoidance is the one nearly everyone agrees is bad news, and it breeds self-handicapping, where a student procrastinates or visibly does not try so that a poor result implicates effort rather than ability.

The textbook summary is that mastery goals produce deeper, more durable learning while performance goals may still buy grades. Treat that as a first approximation, because the measures disagree with each other. A 2010 meta-analysis of 243 correlational studies with 91,087 participants found that goal-outcome correlations depended on which scale was used and how its items were worded: performance-approach scales built mostly from normatively referenced items correlated positively with performance outcomes, r = .14, while scales built mostly from appearance and evaluative items correlated negatively, r = -.14. The authors concluded that researchers are putting one label on conceptually different constructs. When you read that performance goals help or hurt, the first question is what the items actually said.

### Two contested claims, reported honestly

Rewards. Pooling 128 experiments in 1999, Deci, Koestner, and Ryan reported that when students expected a tangible reward, their later free-choice engagement with the task fell - most sharply where the reward hung on merely taking part, d = -0.40 - while positive feedback pushed that same free-choice engagement up, d = 0.33. Critics replied that these detrimental effects appear only under restricted and easily avoidable conditions. The most usable synthesis is a 2014 meta-analysis of 183 samples and 212,468 participants: intrinsic motivation forecast performance both with incentives present and without them; that forecast weakened where the incentive hung directly on performance and held where it hung on performance only indirectly; and incentives tracked how much work got done while intrinsic motivation tracked how good it was. In the vocabulary of this lesson, a directly contingent payment installs external regulation in the place where you wanted identified regulation. The behaviorism lesson works through the same evidence from the contingency-design side.

Mindset. Dweck's implicit theories research holds that students who believe ability is malleable handle difficulty better than students who believe it is fixed, and short interventions have been claimed to raise achievement. The strongest test is the National Study of Learning Mindsets, a preregistered randomized trial with 12,490 ninth graders across 65 nationally representative U.S. schools. A single online session under an hour raised lower-achieving students' core-course GPA by 0.10 grade points and lifted enrollment in advanced tenth-grade mathematics from 33 percent to 36 percent, with effects concentrated in schools whose peer norms supported the message. That is real, targeted, and small. Meta-analyses find less. Sisk and colleagues, across a correlational analysis of 273 effects from 365,915 people and an intervention analysis of 43 effects from 57,155, reported weak overall effects with some support for benefits among low-socioeconomic-status or academically at-risk students. Macnamara and Burgoyne's 2023 review of 63 studies covering 97,672 students found an overall effect of d = 0.05 that was not significant after correcting for publication bias, d = 0.02 among the six highest-quality studies, and larger published effects from authors with a financial incentive to report them. The 2007 IES guide's recommendation to teach that academic abilities are expandable and improvable was rated moderate at the time; that rating predates every trial just described, which is a good reason to check the date on any recommendation.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Motivation** — The processes that determine which activities a person chooses, how much effort they invest, and how long they keep going after difficulty.
- **Internalization** — The process by which a reason for acting that started outside the person becomes something they endorse and treat as their own.
- **Introjected regulation** — Acting under internal pressure such as guilt, shame, or the need to protect self-worth, rather than from a freely chosen reason.
- **Identified regulation** — Acting because you personally value what the activity leads to, even if the activity itself holds no interest.
- **Basic psychological needs** — In self-determination theory, the three conditions - autonomy, competence, and relatedness - whose satisfaction supports interest and the internalization of external reasons.
- **Expectancy for success** — A learner's belief about how well they will perform on an upcoming task, distinct from how much they want to do it.
- **Subjective task value** — How worthwhile an activity seems to a learner, made up of importance to identity, enjoyment, usefulness for other goals, and the costs incurred.
- **Cost** — In situated expectancy-value theory, what pursuing a task takes away: time, effort, emotional strain, and the opportunities given up.
- **Causal attribution** — The explanation a person gives for why an outcome happened, classified by whether the cause is internal, lasting, and under their control.
- **Achievement goal orientation** — What a learner is treating as success in a setting - improving on their own past, or comparing favorably with other people - and whether they are pursuing or avoiding it.

## Eli-10

Instead of asking whether someone is motivated, ask four smaller questions. Do I think I can do this? People rarely start things they expect to fail at. Is it worth what it costs? Worth can mean fun, or useful, or important to who I am, and cost is the time, effort, and stress it takes. Whose reason is it? Doing homework because you would feel guilty otherwise is a different engine from doing it because you actually want what it leads to, even though neither one is fun. And what am I trying to prove? Trying to understand something and trying not to look stupid produce very different behavior once the work gets hard.

## Eli's Analogy

Deciding to cook a real dinner instead of ordering takeout works the same way. You need to believe the recipe is within your skill, you need to want the result enough - because it tastes good, because it saves money, or because you think of yourself as someone who cooks - and you weigh all that against the cost: the shopping, the hour, the sink full of pans. Change any one of those and the decision flips.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The analogy makes motivation look like a conscious calculation made once, alone, with full information. It is usually none of those. A student's sense of what a subject is worth is built over years out of grades, offhand comments, and what their friends treat as normal, and it can shift inside one class period. A kitchen also has no audience deciding whether you belong in it, while relatedness - feeling connected to the people around you - is one of the three needs the research says has to be met.

## Worked Example

Hulleman and Harackiewicz ran a randomized field experiment in high school science classes. One randomly assigned group wrote short pieces connecting the unit's content to their own lives; the comparison group did not. The relevance writing raised interest in science and course grades, but the gain concentrated among students who had low expectations of success. Read that through situated expectancy-value theory. The intervention targeted utility value and left expectancy alone, so it paid off precisely for the students whose value was the binding constraint; students who already expected to do well had no shortage of value to add. That is the general lesson of diagnosis before intervention: find out whether the missing piece is expectancy, value, cost, or an unmet need, because the wrong repair does nothing at all.

## Common Mistakes

- **Sorting students into two boxes, intrinsically and extrinsically motivated.**  
  Extrinsic reasons run along a continuum of internalization. Identified regulation is extrinsic and still autonomous, and in a meta-analysis of 344 samples it was the type most strongly related to persistence, while external regulation was unrelated to performance or persistence.
- **Treating unmotivated as a stable description of a person.**  
  Ask which judgment failed: does the student expect to fail, find the task worthless, find the cost too high, or lack autonomy, competence, or relatedness in that room? Each diagnosis implies a different move, and the label implies none.
- **Telling every struggling student to try harder because effort attributions are the good kind.**  
  Effort attributions help only when effort would actually have changed the result. If the barrier was a missing prerequisite or a bad strategy, the accurate and more useful response is prescriptive, informational feedback about what to do differently.
- **Taking mastery goals good, performance goals bad as a settled empirical result.**  
  It is a reasonable first approximation, but a 2010 meta-analysis found performance-approach scales correlated with performance at r = .14 or r = -.14 depending only on how the items were worded. Ask what the measure said before generalizing.
- **Presenting growth mindset interventions as a reliable way to raise achievement.**  
  The best-powered national trial found a 0.10 grade-point gain limited to lower-achieving students in schools with supportive peer norms, and meta-analyses report weak to non-significant average effects with evidence of publication bias. Report it as contested.

## Compare / Contrast

| Idea | Contrasted with | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| **Intrinsic motivation** | **Identified regulation** | Intrinsic motivation comes from the activity being interesting in itself; identified regulation comes from valuing what the activity leads to. Both are autonomous, which is why lumping identified regulation in with rewards and punishments as extrinsic loses the distinction that predicts persistence. |
| **Expectancy for success** | **Subjective task value** | Expectancy is a prediction about performance; value is a judgment about worth. Neither one substitutes for the other: a student confident of an easy A can still skip a course they consider pointless, and a student who loves a subject can avoid it if they expect to fail. |
| **Performance-approach goals** | **Performance-avoidance goals** | Both define success by comparison with others, but approach goals aim at outperforming while avoidance goals aim only at not looking incompetent. Avoidance goals are the ones consistently tied to poor outcomes and to self-handicapping such as deliberate under-effort. |
| **Attributing failure to low ability** | **Attributing failure to a weak strategy** | Both are internal, but ability is usually construed as stable and uncontrollable while strategy is unstable and controllable. The second leaves expectancy for the next attempt intact and names a specific thing to change; the first does neither. |

## Key Takeaway

Motivation is not one quantity to raise but a set of separable judgments - expectancy, value, cost, whose reason is driving the effort, and what counts as success - and the honest picture of the research includes contested findings, small effect sizes, and measures that disagree with one another.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. In self-determination theory's continuum of extrinsic motivation, which form best describes a student who completes assignments mainly to avoid feeling guilty or ashamed?**

   A. External regulation
   B. Identified regulation
   C. Introjected regulation
   D. Integrated regulation

   *Answer: C.* Introjected regulation is the partially internalized form in which the pressure has moved inside the person - guilt, shame, or protecting self-worth - without being freely chosen. External regulation would mean working for a reward or to avoid a penalty imposed from outside; identified regulation would mean valuing what the work leads to; integrated regulation would mean that valuing sits consistently with the student's other values and sense of self.
   *Difficulty: recall · Skill: Naming the forms of regulation on the internalization continuum · Sources: ryan-deci-2000-intrinsic-extrinsic-definitions, howard-2021-sdt-student-motivation-meta-analysis*

**2. Why did Eccles and Wigfield rename their framework situated expectancy-value theory in 2020?**

   A. They replaced expectancy and value with a single combined motivation score.
   B. They wanted to stress that expectancies and values develop within particular situations and cultural contexts rather than being static, linear, and universal.
   C. Later research showed that subjective task value does not predict which activities students choose.
   D. They restricted the theory to laboratory studies of short-term task choice.

   *Answer: B.* The 2020 article argues that the standard diagram makes the model look static, linear, and monolithic, and uses the rename to foreground the situative and cultural formation of expectancies and values along with the elaborated cost component. The constructs were not merged or discarded, task value remains a central predictor of choice, and the framework is developmental and field-based rather than restricted to the laboratory.
   *Difficulty: understanding · Skill: Interpreting the current framing of expectancy-value theory · Sources: eccles-wigfield-2020-situated-expectancy-value, wigfield-eccles-2000-expectancy-value-theory*

**3. A student says, "I bombed the chemistry test because I'm just not a science person." Using Weiner's dimensions, which reading of that attribution is accurate?**

   A. External, stable, and controllable, so the student is likely to blame the teacher and change strategy.
   B. Internal, unstable, and controllable, so the student has left an obvious move available for next time.
   C. External, unstable, and uncontrollable, so expectancy for the next test should be unaffected.
   D. Internal, stable, and uncontrollable, the combination that most undermines expectancy for the next attempt.

   *Answer: D.* Not being a science person locates the cause in the person, treats it as permanent, and treats it as beyond influence. Because stability drives expectancy for the next attempt, that combination leaves the student with no reason to expect a different result and no specific thing to change. The other options misclassify at least one dimension: the cause is neither external nor unstable as the student has framed it.
   *Difficulty: application · Skill: Classifying a real attribution and predicting its motivational consequence · Sources: seifert-sutton-educational-psychology-motivation, weiner-1985-attributional-theory*

**4. Which statement best represents the current state of the evidence on growth mindset interventions?**

   A. Large randomized trials show that short mindset interventions produce large achievement gains for most students.
   B. A nationally representative randomized trial found small, targeted gains for lower-achieving students in schools with supportive norms, while meta-analyses report weak or non-significant average effects.
   C. Meta-analyses show that mindset interventions reduce achievement, so schools should abandon them.
   D. No large-scale randomized evidence about mindset interventions has been collected.

   *Answer: B.* The National Study of Learning Mindsets found a 0.10 grade-point gain in core-course GPA for lower-achieving students and a three-percentage-point rise in advanced mathematics enrollment, concentrated where peer norms supported the message; Sisk and colleagues reported weak overall effects, and Macnamara and Burgoyne found d = 0.05 overall, non-significant after correcting for publication bias. So the effects are neither large, nor negative, nor absent from the record - they are small and conditional.
   *Difficulty: analysis · Skill: Weighing a large trial against meta-analytic evidence on a contested claim · Sources: yeager-2019-national-study-learning-mindsets, sisk-2018-growth-mindset-meta-analyses, macnamara-burgoyne-2023-growth-mindset-meta-analysis*

**5. A science department wants better lab reports, not more of them, and proposes paying students a fixed amount for each report submitted. What does the meta-analytic evidence most support about this design?**

   A. Incentives tend to predict the quantity of work while intrinsic motivation better predicts its quality, so paying per report targets the wrong outcome and risks displacing the more autonomous reasons for writing well.
   B. Because the payment is tangible and expected, the students' interest in science will be permanently destroyed.
   C. Rewards have no measurable relationship to intrinsic motivation, so the design is motivationally neutral.
   D. Praise and cash payment have the same measured effect on free-choice engagement, so the choice between them does not matter.

   *Answer: A.* The 2014 meta-analysis of 183 samples found that incentives better predict quantity of performance while intrinsic motivation better predicts quality, and that intrinsic motivation's contribution shrinks when incentives are tied directly to performance. The remaining options overstate the 1999 undermining result as permanent destruction, deny a large measured literature, or ignore that the 1999 meta-analysis found positive feedback raised free-choice engagement while expected tangible rewards lowered it.
   *Difficulty: application · Skill: Using contested reward evidence to evaluate an incentive design · Sources: cerasoli-2014-intrinsic-extrinsic-meta-analysis, deci-koestner-ryan-1999-meta-analysis, eisenberger-cameron-1996-detrimental-effects*
