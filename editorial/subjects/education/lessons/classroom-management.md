# Classroom Management

## In 30 Seconds

Classroom management is mostly what happens before anything goes wrong. Jacob Kounin's observation studies found that effective managers were not better at stopping misbehavior; they prevented it, by communicating awareness, keeping activities moving, and teaching routines until the routines ran themselves. Current federal guidance keeps that logic and adds two things worth learning early: behavior serves a purpose, so the wrong response can strengthen it, and removing students from class carries documented costs that fall unequally.

## Why This Matters

New teachers rate management as their first worry, and the research literature grew partly in response to that. It is also the part of teaching that observation rubrics, mentors and licensure assessments watch most closely, because everything else depends on it: instruction that cannot be delivered cannot be learned. The stakes are not only instructional. Federal civil rights data show that exclusionary discipline falls far more heavily on Black students and students with disabilities than their enrollment would predict, and the research literature links suspension to worse academic outcomes rather than better behavior. How a teacher handles the first ten seconds of a small problem is, in aggregate, an equity issue.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain Kounin's central finding that effective classroom management is characterized by prevention rather than by skill at stopping misbehavior, and define withitness, overlapping, momentum, smoothness and group alerting.
- Distinguish a rule from a procedure, and explain why effective managers teach procedures at the start of the year instead of announcing them.
- Analyze how instructional design - task clarity, pacing, activity format, transitions and success rate - changes the amount of off-task behavior a lesson produces.
- Apply the functional view of behavior to explain how a well-intentioned teacher response can strengthen the behavior it was meant to stop.
- Evaluate claims about behavior-specific praise, positive-to-corrective ratios and exclusionary discipline against what the evidence actually supports.

## The College Version

### Kounin's inversion

Until Kounin's work, advice about classroom order was mostly advice about what to do after something went wrong; the ERIC record for his own later monograph observes that anyone trying to help teachers with discipline in the early 1960s had few resources at hand. Jacob Kounin's research inverted that. Working from videotaped classroom observation - he was among the first to use the medium as a systematic data source - he asked what distinguished teachers whose classes showed high work involvement and low misbehavior, and found that the answer was not what those teachers did to stop misbehavior. It was what they did so that less of it started.

Five constructs from that work are still taught. Withitness is the communication of awareness: the teacher's attention is visibly distributed across the whole room, so students experience being known rather than unobserved. Overlapping is handling two things at once - taking a question from one student while a signal keeps the rest of the class working. Momentum is the absence of drag inside an activity, and smoothness is the absence of jerks between activities: no false starts, no lesson interrupted for an announcement that could have waited. Group alerting is the set of moves that keep everyone accountable during a whole-class segment rather than only the student currently speaking.

Two cautions belong with the list. The research is correlational observation, so it identifies what effective managers did, not a proven cause of order. And the list itself is not fixed: the ERIC record for Kounin's later monograph names four dimensions plus properties of the lesson's signal system, while Evertson and Harris's research synthesis names five. Learn the constructs, not the numbering.

### Routines are content, so teach them

If prevention matters most, the highest-leverage window is the start of the year. Emmer, Evertson and Anderson observed teachers across a school year and looked hard at their first days. Their conclusion was blunt: how well a classroom runs in April can be predicted from the first several weeks. The teachers whose students later showed high on-task rates and higher achievement had planned rules and procedures before students arrived, communicated expectations clearly, taught routines and procedures along with the standard of performance expected, monitored work and behavior systematically, and gave feedback on both.

The distinction underneath that finding is between a rule and a procedure. A rule is a standard of conduct - treat others with respect - and there should be few of them; federal classroom guidance suggests three to five positively stated expectations, posted, defined and explicitly taught. A procedure is the sequence of physical steps for a recurring event: entering the room, getting a pencil, submitting work, moving from desks to lab benches, what to do when you finish early. Rules are announced. Procedures have to be taught the way anything else is taught - explained, modeled, practiced, given feedback, and retaught after a break or a schedule change.

This is not merely descriptive. Later field studies trained teachers in beginning-of-year management and compared them with untrained colleagues: the trained teachers used significantly more management procedures, and their students showed higher task engagement, less inappropriate behavior and higher academic success. That moves the finding from what good managers happen to do to something that can be learned deliberately.

### The lesson is the management plan

A large share of off-task behavior is manufactured by the task. Kounin's own data made the point early: student engagement in his observations ran highest during recitation, at about 85 percent, and lowest during seatwork, at about 65 percent, and later work found that frequent seatwork lowered on-task rates while alternating shorter segments of content development and practice held engagement higher than one long stretch of either.

Three design variables do most of the work. The first is clarity. Students have to interpret two things about any assignment - what they are supposed to learn and how they are supposed to participate - and an unclear task leaves them guessing about both. More cognitively and procedurally complex work, and work in which students produce something or face a novel problem, makes larger management demands, which is an argument for specifying participation structures in advance, not for avoiding demanding work. The second is difficulty. Rosenshine reports that the optimal success rate during practice appears to be about 80 percent; work that a student cannot get right is work that student will stop doing, and the stopping usually looks like a behavior problem. The third is transitions, which is where smoothness cashes out. Six transitions a period at ninety wasted seconds each is nine minutes a day, or twenty-seven hours across a hundred and eighty days.

The federal practice guide on reducing behavior problems in elementary classrooms points the same way: modifying the classroom learning environment, and teaching and reinforcing new skills, are its two recommendations rated as supported by strong evidence. Planning and pacing as design belong to the lesson planning topic; what matters here is that they are management decisions, made before the bell.

### Respond small, and know the escalation trap

Prevention is not perfection, so teachers still need a response repertoire, and its defining feature should be low intensity. Effective managers in the observational studies handled most minor misbehavior unobtrusively - proximity, eye contact, a redirection that did not stop the lesson. Federal guidance describes an error correction as brief, contingent, specific about what to do instead, calm, respectful, delivered privately where possible, paired with specific praise once the student does the right thing, and - the part teachers skip - ended by disengaging. The stated purposes of a consequence include preempting escalation and minimizing inadvertent reward of the problem behavior.

The escalation trap is what happens when the correction becomes a transaction. A public reprimand invites a reply, the reply invites a firmer reprimand, and within thirty seconds a teacher who wanted a student to open a book is having an argument in front of thirty witnesses that neither party can now exit without losing something. The exits are all early ones: state the expectation once, give the student a moment to comply, and turn back to the lesson rather than standing there to watch.

The positive side of the ratio deserves a careful reading. The same federal guidance recommends a minimum of five praise statements for every correction and describes praise that names the behavior - not 'great job' but a statement of what the student actually did. But be honest about the evidence. An analysis of three years of data from 151 classrooms in 19 elementary schools found a positive linear relationship between a teacher's praise-to-reprimand ratio and student on-task behavior, with no threshold at 3:1 or 4:1 or anywhere else at which behavior suddenly improved. And a systematic review of thirty studies of teacher praise, eleven of which met Council for Exceptional Children and What Works Clearinghouse standards, concluded there was insufficient evidence to call teacher praise an evidence-based practice for K-12 students without severe disabilities. Direction supported; specific numeral, a rule of thumb.

### Behavior is doing something for the student

The single most useful analytic idea in this topic is that behavior has a function. Functional analysis research classifies what maintains a behavior into social positive reinforcement - adult or peer attention, or access to an item or activity - social negative reinforcement, meaning escape from or avoidance of a demand, and automatic or sensory reinforcement, where the behavior produces its own consequence. Teacher preparation usually presents this as four functions: attention, items or activities, escape, and automatic.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable. A response that delivers the function will strengthen the behavior it was meant to suppress, regardless of how the adult intended it. Sending a student into the hallway looks like a penalty; if the behavior was maintained by escape from a task that is too hard to read, the hallway is exactly what the behavior was for, and it will happen again tomorrow. The same logic explains why federal guidance limits planned ignoring to minor behavior maintained by teacher attention and rules it out where the behavior draws peer attention instead - ignoring only withholds the payoff if you have correctly identified the payoff.

The diagnostic move is to look at what happens immediately before and after: the antecedent-behavior-consequence pattern, which federal practice guidance recommends identifying, and which classroom data systems support with frequency counts, duration records, interval sampling and simple ABC cards. This lesson teaches the functional view as a way of thinking, not a procedure. Formal functional behavioral assessment and any individual student's behavior plan belong to that student's team, its data and its specialists. The mechanics of reinforcement itself - the four contingencies, and why negative does not mean bad - belong to the behaviorism topic.

### The structure the teacher sits inside

The field's current structural answer is multi-tiered. Positive Behavioral Interventions and Supports, supported by a technical assistance center funded by the U.S. Department of Education, organizes support in three tiers: Tier 1 is the schoolwide layer, including defining, teaching and acknowledging expected behavior for everyone; Tier 2 adds targeted support for students who need more than the universal layer provides; Tier 3 provides intensive individualized support. A 2023 brief from that center reviews the literature and designates Tier 1 PBIS an evidence-based practice for reducing exclusionary discipline and improving social, emotional and behavioral outcomes - read that conclusion knowing the center that promotes the framework wrote it.

The framework also has a statutory foothold. Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act, when a child's behavior impedes that child's learning or the learning of others, the IEP team must consider the use of positive behavioral interventions and supports and other strategies to address that behavior. That is a requirement to consider, addressed to a team, and the rest of IDEA procedure belongs to the special education topic.

For a classroom teacher, the tiers mainly settle a question of scope. Universal practices are yours: the routines, the expectations, the ratio, the design of the task. Beyond that you are a participant in a team process, not a solo practitioner, and knowing where that line falls is part of the professional skill.

### What removal costs, and who it falls on

Exclusionary discipline - office referral, in-school suspension, out-of-school suspension, expulsion - is the response whose effects are best documented and least reassuring. Two things should be stated precisely, because they are often blurred together.

First, the disparities. The 2021-22 Civil Rights Data Collection, a mandatory federal survey covering 17,704 districts and 98,010 schools and released in January 2025, recorded about 2.3 million K-12 students receiving at least one in-school suspension and about 2.4 million receiving at least one out-of-school suspension - roughly 5 percent of enrollment each. Black boys were 8 percent of K-12 enrollment but 18 percent of students given in-school suspension, 22 percent of those given out-of-school suspension and 21 percent of those expelled. Black girls were 7 percent of enrollment and 11, 13 and 13 percent of the same three categories. Students with disabilities were 17 percent of enrollment but 27, 29 and 24 percent. The pattern starts before kindergarten: Black children were 18 percent of preschool enrollment and 38 percent of preschool children suspended out of school. GAO's earlier analysis of 2013-14 data found the same shape and added that it persisted regardless of the type of discipline, the poverty level of the school, or the type of school attended.

Second, what that does and does not establish. GAO says so itself: disparities like these may support a finding of discrimination, but taken alone they do not establish that unlawful discrimination occurred. The measurement is solid; the explanation is contested, and this lesson does not resolve it. The outcome literature is similar in structure. A meta-analysis of 53 cases from 34 studies found a significant inverse relationship between suspension and achievement and a significant positive relationship between suspension and dropout - associations from observational research, not a demonstrated causal effect, since suspended and non-suspended students differ in many ways. What the available research documents is association with lower achievement and higher dropout, not improved subsequent behavior. That is the reason the whole field has moved toward the prevention-and-instruction model this lesson describes.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Withitness** — A teacher's visible, continuously communicated awareness of what is happening across the whole room, which Kounin found among the behaviors distinguishing effective classroom managers.
- **Overlapping** — The capacity to attend to two or more events at the same time, such as answering one student while a signal keeps the remaining students working.
- **Momentum** — The pace at which an activity keeps moving forward without drag, stalls, or unnecessary interruptions once it has begun.
- **Group alerting** — Moves that keep every student in a whole-class segment accountable and attentive rather than only the one currently answering.
- **Procedure** — The taught sequence of steps for a recurring classroom event, such as entering, submitting work or transitioning, as distinct from a standard of conduct.
- **Precorrection** — A reminder of what is expected delivered before the moment of difficulty arrives, rather than after a student has already got it wrong.
- **Behavior-specific praise** — Acknowledgment that names exactly what the student did, in contrast to a general expression of approval such as 'great job'.
- **Function of behavior** — The purpose a behavior serves for the student: obtaining attention, obtaining an item or activity, escaping a demand, or producing sensory consequences.
- **Exclusionary discipline** — Responses that remove a student from instruction, including office referral, in-school suspension, out-of-school suspension and expulsion.
- **Tier 1** — The universal layer of a multi-tiered support framework, applied to every student in the school, including defining, teaching and acknowledging expected behavior.

## Eli-10

Most people think classroom management means what a teacher does when someone acts up. Researchers who watched hundreds of hours of classroom video found something different. The teachers with the calmest rooms were not better at telling people off. They were better at the hour before that, when nothing had gone wrong yet. They showed the class that they noticed everything. They kept the lesson moving so there were no dead spots. And on the very first days of school they taught the boring stuff - how you come in, how you hand things in, what you do when you finish early - the same careful way you would teach fractions, with modeling and practice. There is one more idea worth knowing: every annoying behavior is getting the student something. Attention, or a break from hard work, or a thing they wanted. If the teacher's reaction hands over exactly that, the behavior gets stronger, even though it looked like a punishment.

## Eli's Analogy

Think about how a city cuts down on car crashes. You could hire more police to write tickets after crashes happen. Or you could paint clear lane lines, put up signs before the turn instead of at it, time the lights so traffic keeps flowing, and design the junction so the confusing move is hard to make. The second approach prevents far more crashes than the first, and a classroom works the same way: the design does most of the job, and the ticket is what is left over.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** Roads do not care whether anyone notices them, and students do. Withitness only works because a person registers being seen, which has no equivalent in traffic engineering. Cars also do not have reasons; a student's behavior is doing something for them, and until you work out what, you cannot tell whether your response is a fix or a reward. And a road is the same on Monday and Friday, while a class of twenty-eight brings twenty-eight different days into the room.

## Worked Example

A ninth-grade biology teacher plans the first week around procedures rather than rules. She lists the routines her room actually runs on - entering and starting the warm-up, moving from desks to lab benches and back, collecting goggles, handing in work, what to do when finished early, leaving at the bell - and for each one she writes the steps, models it, has the class practice it, and gives feedback, the same cycle she would use for a lab technique. She posts four expectations, not fifteen rules. In February the room gets noisy again after a schedule change, so instead of adding consequences she counts: six transitions a period, roughly ninety seconds lost at each, which is nine minutes a day, and across a hundred and eighty days, twenty-seven hours - about thirty-two class periods of instruction. She reteaches two transitions and adds a precorrection before each. She also tallies her own talk for one period: four praise statements and eleven corrections, a ratio of about 0.36 to 1. Meeting the recommended minimum of five to one from that starting point would take fifty-five praise statements, which tells her the real problem is the eleven corrections, and that the fix is upstream in the transitions, not in manufacturing compliments.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating classroom management as a discipline system - a ladder of consequences for what to do after a student misbehaves.**
  Build the prevention layer first. Kounin's finding was that effective managers were not distinguished by how they stopped misbehavior but by how much of it never started, and the beginning-of-year research shows the prevention layer is mostly built in the first few weeks.
- **Posting rules and assuming procedures are obvious - announcing 'be responsible' and expecting students to know how to enter, submit work and transition.**
  Separate the two. Keep three to five posted expectations, then teach each recurring procedure explicitly - explain, model, practice, give feedback - and reteach after any break or schedule change.
- **Meeting a small off-task behavior with a large public response, then staying in the conversation until it becomes a contest.**
  Keep the response low-intensity and brief: state what to do instead, calmly and privately where possible, then disengage and return to teaching. Federal guidance names preempting escalation as one of the purposes of a consequence.
- **Assuming that anything unpleasant for the student will reduce the behavior, so a removal must be working.**
  Check the function first. If the behavior was maintained by escape from a task, removal delivers exactly what it was for, and the behavior will strengthen. Whether something functioned as a penalty is settled by what happens to the behavior next, not by how it felt to administer.
- **Quoting a specific praise-to-correction ratio, or behavior-specific praise generally, as an established finding.**
  State it as guidance with a limit. The large multi-classroom analysis found a positive linear relationship but no threshold ratio, and a systematic review found insufficient evidence to call teacher praise an evidence-based practice for K-12 students without severe disabilities.

## Compare / Contrast

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A rule** | **A procedure** | A rule states a standard of conduct and there should be very few of them, posted and defined. A procedure is the physical sequence for a recurring event and it has to be taught, practiced and retaught. Most classrooms that feel out of control have enough rules and too few taught procedures. |
| **Withitness** | **Surveillance** | Both involve monitoring, but withitness is defined by what reaches the students: awareness that is communicated, distributed across the room, and used to intervene early and quietly. Surveillance is watching for infractions to catch, which produces the same eyes with the opposite effect on the room. |
| **Behavior-specific praise** | **General praise** | Behavior-specific praise names the action, so it tells the student and everyone listening exactly what met the expectation. General praise - 'great job' - carries approval without information. Federal guidance recommends the specific form, while a systematic review reports the evidence base for teacher praise overall is thinner than its popularity. |
| **Punishment as intended by the adult** | **Function as experienced by the student** | The adult's intention does not decide the outcome. A hallway, an office referral or a suspension may function as escape from an aversive task, in which case it reinforces the behavior. The test is empirical: did the behavior become less frequent afterwards? |
| **A documented disparity** | **A demonstrated cause** | Federal data measure who receives discipline, and the overrepresentation of Black students and students with disabilities is a measurement, not an inference. Why it occurs, and whether suspension causes the worse outcomes associated with it, are separate questions the available observational evidence does not settle - a point GAO makes about its own findings. |

## Key Takeaway

Classroom management is prevention and instruction, not a consequence ladder: teach the routines, design the task, communicate awareness, keep responses small, and ask what a behavior is doing for the student before deciding how to answer it - because removal is the response with the best-documented costs and the least evidence of working.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. What did Kounin's classroom observation research identify as the characteristic of effective classroom managers?**

A. They responded to misbehavior more firmly and more consistently than other teachers.
B. They prevented problems from starting, rather than being better at stopping misbehavior once it occurred.
C. They used a published discipline program with a defined sequence of consequences.
D. They removed disruptive students from the room earlier than other teachers did.

*Answer: B.* Kounin's finding, as summarized in Evertson and Harris's research synthesis, is that what characterizes effective group management is not what teachers do to stop misbehavior but how they prevent problems in the first place. The dimensions he identified - withitness, overlapping, smoothness and momentum, group alerting - are all preventive. Firmness, published programs and removal are not what the observational data distinguished.
*Difficulty: recall. Skill: Recall the central finding of the observational classroom management literature. Sources: eric-kounin-1983-classrooms-behavior-settings, evertson-harris-1992-managing-classrooms-synthesis.*

**2. A teacher posts twelve rules on the first day, reads them aloud, and moves straight into content. Judged against the beginning-of-year management research, what is missing?**

A. A consequence attached to each of the twelve rules.
B. A signed acknowledgment of the rules from families.
C. Few enough expectations to be usable, plus explicit teaching and practice of the procedures for recurring events.
D. A schoolwide rather than classroom-level list of rules.

*Answer: C.* The teachers whose classes later showed high on-task rates planned rules and procedures in advance, communicated expectations clearly, and taught routines and procedures together with the standard of performance expected. Federal classroom guidance points the same way with three to five posted, defined and explicitly taught expectations. Announcing twelve rules supplies neither a usable set of expectations nor any taught procedure; consequences, family signatures and schoolwide lists do not fill that gap.
*Difficulty: understanding. Skill: Distinguish rules from taught procedures and apply the beginning-of-year findings. Sources: emmer-evertson-1979-beginning-of-school-year-report, eric-emmer-1980-beginning-of-school-year, evertson-harris-1992-managing-classrooms-synthesis, osep-2015-supporting-and-responding-to-behavior.*

**3. During a difficult reading task, a pattern recurs in a class: off-task behavior begins, the student is sent to sit in the hallway, and over several weeks the behavior becomes more frequent. What does the functional view of behavior suggest about this pattern?**

A. The removal is likely functioning as escape from the task, delivering what the behavior was for and strengthening it.
B. The removal was not applied quickly enough to work as a punishment.
C. The behavior must be maintained by sensory consequences, since removal did not reduce it.
D. The increase shows the student is testing limits and the removal should be lengthened.

*Answer: A.* Functional analysis classifies maintaining variables as social positive reinforcement, social negative reinforcement (escape or avoidance), or automatic reinforcement. If the behavior is escape-maintained, removal from the demand supplies the very consequence that maintains it, which is why the frequency rises. Nothing in the pattern points to a sensory function, and lengthening or speeding up a response that delivers escape makes the contingency stronger, not weaker.
*Difficulty: application. Skill: Apply the functional view of behavior to diagnose an unintentionally reinforcing response. Sources: hanley-2003-functional-analysis-review, iris-center-behavioral-form-and-function, osep-2015-supporting-and-responding-to-behavior.*

**4. How should a teacher-preparation course describe the evidence for the recommended positive-to-corrective interaction ratio?**

A. A randomized trial identified 5:1 as the threshold above which classroom behavior improves, which is why federal guidance names that specific number.
B. Federal guidance recommends a minimum of five praise statements per correction; a large multi-classroom analysis found a positive linear relationship but no threshold ratio, and a systematic review found the evidence insufficient to call teacher praise an evidence-based practice.
C. The ratio has been tested and disproven, and the reviews conclude that behavior-specific praise should be avoided in K-12 classrooms.
D. The evidence is entirely anecdotal: no systematic studies of teacher praise and reprimand rates have been conducted in real classrooms.

*Answer: B.* Three findings have to be held together. Federal OSEP guidance does recommend a minimum five-to-one ratio. The Caldarella analysis of 151 classrooms across 19 schools found a positive linear relationship with on-task behavior but explicitly no threshold at 3:1, 4:1 or elsewhere. The Moore review of thirty studies, eleven meeting CEC and WWC standards, found insufficient evidence to identify teacher praise as an evidence-based practice for K-12 students without severe disabilities. No trial established a threshold, insufficient evidence is not disproof, and the literature is systematic rather than anecdotal.
*Difficulty: analysis. Skill: Evaluate the evidential standing of a widely repeated classroom management rule. Sources: osep-2015-supporting-and-responding-to-behavior, caldarella-2020-praise-to-reprimand-ratio, moore-2019-teacher-praise-evidence-review.*

**5. Federal civil rights data show Black boys at 8 percent of K-12 enrollment but 22 percent of students receiving out-of-school suspension, and students with disabilities at 17 percent of enrollment but 29 percent. What is the most accurate way to state what these data establish?**

A. They prove that the disparities are caused by intentional discrimination by teachers.
B. They are estimates from a voluntary sample and should be treated as provisional.
C. They document a measured overrepresentation in exclusionary discipline; GAO notes that such disparities may support a finding of discrimination but alone do not establish that unlawful discrimination occurred.
D. They show the disparities disappear once school poverty level is taken into account.

*Answer: C.* The Civil Rights Data Collection is a mandatory survey of all public districts and schools, not a voluntary sample, so the shares are measurements rather than estimates from a sample. GAO states explicitly that disparities of this kind may support a finding of discrimination but taken alone do not establish that unlawful discrimination occurred, and GAO also found the pattern persisted regardless of school poverty level, discipline type or school type.
*Difficulty: analysis. Skill: State precisely what federal discipline disparity data do and do not establish. Sources: crdc-2021-22-first-look, gao-2018-discipline-disparities.*
