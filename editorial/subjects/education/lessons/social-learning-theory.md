# Social Learning Theory

## In 30 Seconds

Albert Bandura showed that people learn by watching. A child, a student, or an adult can acquire a whole pattern of behavior from a model without performing it first and without being reinforced for it. Whether that learning ever shows up depends on attention, retention, production, and motivation, including consequences seen happening to somebody else. Bandura later renamed the theory social cognitive theory, because what the observer notices, expects, and believes about their own capability turned out to be doing most of the work.

## Why This Matters

Modeling is the mechanism underneath a large share of what teachers actually do: demonstrating a procedure, thinking aloud through a hard problem, handing out a worked example, seating a struggling student beside a peer who has just figured it out. Naming the mechanism lets you diagnose why a demonstration failed, because attention, memory, production, and motivation break for different reasons and need different repairs. Self-efficacy gives you a second lever, one that explains why two students with the same skill attempt very different tasks. And the Bobo doll studies are among the most misquoted experiments in education coursework, so learning to state exactly what they showed is useful practice for reading any contested evidence claim.

## Learning Objectives

- Define observational learning and distinguish it from simple imitation and from behaviorist accounts that require the learner to perform first.
- Explain Bandura's four subprocesses and identify which one has failed in a described classroom breakdown.
- Distinguish vicarious reinforcement and vicarious punishment from the acquisition-performance distinction they support.
- Describe the 1961 and 1963 Bobo doll designs accurately and evaluate what they do and do not license anyone to claim about media.
- Apply self-efficacy and its four sources to choose feedback and models for a specific learner.
- Explain why Bandura relabelled social learning theory as social cognitive theory.

## The College Version

### The problem Bandura was solving

By the late 1950s two accounts of how children pick up aggression were in circulation, and Bandura thought both were wrong. Psychoanalytic theory treated aggression as pressure building inside the child, discharged cathartically, which implied that watching a violent performance should drain the impulse rather than feed it. Behaviorist accounts, covered in this unit's behaviorism lesson, treated new behavior as something the learner has to emit before consequences can select it. Bandura's objection to the second was practical. Trial and error is slow, and for driving, surgery, or swimming it is dangerous. If a behavior could be acquired only by performing it and having that performance reinforced, most of what a culture transmits could not be transmitted at all. His alternative claim was blunt: watching what someone else does can produce responses whose prior probability in the observer is effectively zero. That is a claim about acquisition, and it was testable.

### The Bobo doll studies, described accurately

The 1961 experiment used 72 children, 36 boys and 36 girls, from the Stanford University Nursery School, aged 37 to 69 months, matched beforehand on rated aggressiveness. Twenty-four saw an adult model attack a five-foot inflatable doll with distinctive, novel acts, including mallet blows to its head and scripted lines; 24 saw an adult assemble tinker toys and ignore the doll; 24 saw no model at all. Model sex and child sex were counterbalanced. Every child, controls included, was then mildly frustrated: shown attractive toys, then told those were being saved for other children. Children who had watched the aggressive model reproduced its specific physical and verbal acts far more than the other groups, while about 70 percent of the nonaggressive-model and control children scored zero on the imitative measures. Boys produced more imitative physical aggression than girls, and the male model exerted more influence overall. The result usually left out of textbook summaries is that the nonaggressive model inhibited: those children, particularly the ones who watched the male nonaggressive model, were less aggressive than the no-model controls on several measures. The 1963 follow-up changed the delivery rather than the logic, comparing a live model, a filmed human model, and a filmed cartoon model against a control. Children in the film conditions produced close to twice as much aggression as controls.

### Observational learning and its four subprocesses

Bandura's explanatory machinery is a set of four component subfunctions, and the useful thing about them is that they fail separately. Attentional processes determine what an observer actually extracts from a demonstration; a learner watching the teacher's face rather than the burette has not attended to the feature that matters. Retention processes convert what was seen into a form that survives the demonstration, which Bandura describes as an active transforming and restructuring of what the model did into rules and conceptions, not a recording. Production processes cover turning that representation back into performance, which requires component skills the observer may not have; Bandura originally called this motor reproduction, and the later term production also covers performances that are not motor acts, such as strategies, judgments, and ways of talking through a problem. Motivational processes determine whether a learned behavior is ever performed. Models come in three forms: live models, verbal instructional models who describe rather than demonstrate, and symbolic models met through film, text, or screen. The diagnostic payoff is that a failed demonstration has four possible causes and four different repairs, and repeating the demonstration only fixes one of them.

### Vicarious consequences, and learning that never shows

The motivational subprocess is where consequences re-enter, but at one remove. Vicarious reinforcement is what happens to an observer who sees a model reinforced: the observer's own production of that behavior becomes more likely. Vicarious punishment works the other way, damping the observer's willingness to perform something. Notice the wording. What changes is willingness to perform, not whether learning occurred. Bandura insisted on the distinction between acquisition and performance precisely because people do not perform everything they learn, and he distinguished direct incentives, vicarious incentives, and self-produced incentives arising from a person's own standards. A student who has watched a classmate get sent out for calling out has learned the behavior and learned something about its price. Remove the price and the behavior is available. This is why a quiet classroom is not proof that nothing was learned, and why suppression is a fragile achievement.

### Reciprocal determinism and the renaming of the theory

Bandura rejected the picture of a person as either driven from within or pushed from without. Reciprocal determinism, or triadic reciprocal causation, holds that personal factors including cognition, affect, and biological events, behavior itself, and environmental influences all act on one another bidirectionally. A student who believes she is bad at mathematics avoids the harder set, which produces a weaker performance, which confirms the belief and changes how her teacher groups her. People are producers of their environments as well as products of them. In 1986, with Social Foundations of Thought and Action: A Social Cognitive Theory, Bandura changed the label. He did so for two stated reasons: to distance his account from the other social learning theories then in circulation, including Miller and Dollard's 1941 drive-reduction version, and to mark that cognition is doing the causal work, in constructing reality, self-regulating, encoding information, and performing. The word learning had come to promise less than the theory delivered.

### Self-efficacy and its four sources

The construct Bandura added in 1977 and built on for the rest of his career is self-efficacy: a person's judgment of their capability to organize and execute the courses of action a designated performance requires. Three features matter. It is a judgment about capability, not about worth, so it is not self-esteem. It is task-specific and domain-specific, so a single global score is nearly useless. And it is distinct from an outcome expectation, which is a judgment about what a behavior will produce; a student can be confident she can write the essay and still expect the grading to be unfair. Efficacy beliefs are built by interpreting four sources of information. Mastery experience, the interpreted result of one's own previous performance, is the most influential, because it is the most authentic evidence of capability. Vicarious experience, watching others perform, is weaker but gains force when the observer is uncertain or inexperienced and sees the model as similar. Social persuasion, what credible others say, is weaker still and is not the same thing as praise: it works only when the success it envisions is actually attainable, and negative appraisals damage efficacy more easily than positive ones build it. Physiological and affective states supply the fourth source, since a racing heart before a presentation gets read as evidence about capability. Across many studies academic self-efficacy is positively associated with academic performance, and longitudinal work indicates the two feed each other rather than running one way, which is reason to distrust any method that raises stated confidence without producing real accomplishment.

### What this looks like in a classroom

Four applications follow directly. Teacher modeling of thinking, not just of answers, targets attention and retention: the reasoning is the thing being demonstrated, and it is invisible unless spoken. The WWC practice guide on secondary writing rates a Model-Practice-Reflect instructional cycle at strong evidence, the highest rating in that guide. Worked examples are symbolic models in print, and the IES guide Organizing Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning recommends interleaving worked example solutions with problem-solving exercises, at a moderate level of evidence. Peer models exploit similarity, and the research is specific about which peer: coping models, who make errors, say what they are doing to recover, and gradually succeed, raise self-efficacy and performance more than flawless mastery models, especially for students with little familiarity or a history of failure. Efficacy-building feedback means engineering a genuine mastery experience, a task just past the current edge, plus feedback naming the specific move that made the success work, so the student can repeat it deliberately.

### What the Bobo studies did not settle

The 1961 and 1963 experiments demonstrated that preschoolers reproduce specific novel acts modelled by an adult, against an object built to be struck, in an unfamiliar room, minutes after exposure, after deliberate frustration. That is a real and important finding about imitation. It is not a finding about whether media cause violence, and the studies have drawn methodological, interpretive, and ethical criticism on exactly those grounds. The wider media-effects literature remains contested. The APA's 2020 resolution states an association between violent video game use and increases in aggressive behavior, affect, and cognition and decreases in prosocial behavior and empathy, but it also records that the meta-analytic effect sizes at issue, 0.19, 0.15, 0.08, and 0.16, have been interpreted dramatically differently by the researchers who produced them, that insufficient research has examined lethal violence, and that attributing events like mass shootings to game use is not scientifically sound. Reading the same literature, the Supreme Court majority in Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association endorsed a lower court's assessment that it is nearly all correlational rather than causal and that many of the studies carry admitted methodological flaws. A careful student holds both: modeling is well established, and the inference from laboratory imitation to societal violence is not.

## Key Vocabulary

- **observational learning** - Acquiring a behavior, strategy, or rule by watching another person, without having performed it and without any consequence being delivered to the watcher.
- **model** - A person or symbolic depiction whose performance an observer can pattern their own behavior, thinking, or beliefs after.
- **symbolic model** - A demonstration met through media rather than in person, such as a filmed procedure, a character in a story, or a worked example printed in a textbook.
- **vicarious reinforcement** - An increase in an observer's willingness to perform a behavior after seeing someone else rewarded for it.
- **vicarious punishment** - A decrease in an observer's willingness to perform a behavior after seeing someone else suffer a cost for it, which suppresses performance without erasing what was learned.
- **acquisition-performance distinction** - Bandura's insistence that having learned something and choosing to do it are separate, so an unperformed behavior is not evidence of an unlearned one.
- **reciprocal determinism** - The claim that personal factors, behavior, and environment each influence the other two, so a learner both shapes and is shaped by the setting.
- **self-efficacy** - A person's judgment of their own capability to organize and carry out the actions a specific performance requires.
- **mastery experience** - An interpreted result of one's own past performance, which Bandura treats as the strongest evidence a person uses when judging what they can do.
- **coping model** - A demonstrator who visibly struggles, narrates the strategies used to recover, and only gradually succeeds, rather than performing flawlessly from the start.

## Eli-10

Learning theories before Bandura mostly asked what happened to you. Bandura asked what you saw happen to someone else. Watch a classmate get the whole room's attention by shouting, and you have learned something about shouting without shouting once. Watch that classmate get sent into the hallway, and you have learned something too, though what you learned is about the price, not about the move. His second idea is that watching alone is not enough. You have to notice the part that matters, hold on to it, actually be able to do it, and have some reason to bother. Miss any one of those four and nothing visible happens, even though the watching was completely real.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like riding in the passenger seat along the route to a new school. You follow every turn, you can name every landmark, and it genuinely feels like you know the way. Then you take the wheel and find out that knowing the route and driving the route are two different skills, and that whether you set off at all depends on whether you believe you can.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** A passenger is passive, and Bandura's observer is not: retention in his account means rebuilding what you saw into rules and conceptions you can work from, not replaying a recording. The analogy also gives you one driver and one fixed road, while real observers choose their models, act on their surroundings, and change what they will see next, which is the point of reciprocal determinism. And a passenger learns nothing from watching the driver get a ticket, whereas consequences landing on somebody else are central to the theory.

## Worked Example

A biology teacher demonstrates a titration at the front bench, narrating each step, then sets the class to work. Three students fail in three different ways. Devi watched the teacher's hands but never the burette, so she never encoded the endpoint criterion: an attention failure, repaired by directing where to look, not by repeating the demonstration. Marcus can recite the whole procedure the next morning but fumbles the stopcock and overshoots: retention worked, production did not, and he needs guided practice with feedback rather than another explanation. Tomas can do it and does not start, because his last three labs went badly and he expects this one to as well: that is motivation resting on low self-efficacy, and the repair is a short task he can genuinely finish, followed by feedback naming the specific move that made it work. One demonstration, three diagnoses, three different fixes.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating the theory as the claim that children copy what they see.
  **Correction:** Copying is the narrowest case. Observation can also inhibit a behavior, as the nonaggressive model in the 1961 study did, teach abstract rules and standards rather than specific acts, and produce learning that is never performed at all.
- **Mistake:** Citing the Bobo doll studies as proof that violent media cause violence.
  **Correction:** They showed preschoolers imitating specific modelled acts on a strike-me doll in a laboratory, minutes after exposure, following deliberate frustration. The media-effects question is a separate literature with small, contested effect sizes and, by the APA's own statement, insufficient research on lethal violence.
- **Mistake:** Describing vicarious reinforcement as a reward given to the observer.
  **Correction:** The consequence lands on the model. What reaches the observer is information that changes expectations, and it mainly regulates whether a behavior is performed rather than whether it was learned.
- **Mistake:** Using self-efficacy as another word for confidence or self-esteem.
  **Correction:** Self-efficacy is a judgment about capability on a specific task in a specific domain, not a global feeling about oneself, and it is built mainly by genuine mastery experience rather than by encouragement.
- **Mistake:** Assuming the switch to social cognitive theory introduced a different theory.
  **Correction:** It was the same research programme relabelled in 1986, to separate it from other social learning theories of the period and to mark that cognitive processes, not associations, were carrying the explanation.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Behaviorist account of acquiring a new behavior | Bandura's observational learning | The behaviorist account requires the learner to emit the behavior so consequences can act on it; Bandura's requires only that the learner observe a model, with consequences entering later to govern performance. |
| Vicarious reinforcement | Vicarious punishment | Both act on the observer through what happens to the model, but one raises and the other lowers the likelihood that the observer performs the behavior; neither determines whether the observer learned it. |
| Acquisition | Performance | Acquisition is whether the behavior entered the learner's repertoire; performance is whether it appears. Incentives, direct or vicarious, mostly move the second. |
| Self-efficacy | Outcome expectation | Self-efficacy is a judgment about whether you can execute the actions; an outcome expectation is a judgment about what those actions will get you. A student can be high on one and low on the other. |
| Mastery model | Coping model | A mastery model performs correctly throughout; a coping model errs, verbalizes recovery strategies, and improves. The coping model raises observers' self-efficacy more when the observers lack task familiarity or have struggled before. |

## Key Takeaway

People acquire whole patterns of behavior by watching others, but whether that learning ever appears depends on attention, retention, production, and motivation, and on what the learner believes they are capable of. Bandura's studies proved imitation under laboratory conditions; they did not settle what media do to societies.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. In Bandura's account, which four subprocesses govern observational learning?**

   A. Attention, rehearsal, imitation, and reward.
   B. Encoding, storage, retrieval, and transfer.
   C. Attention, retention, production, and motivation.
   D. Modeling, shaping, chaining, and fading.

   *Answer: C.* Bandura named four component subfunctions: attentional, retention, production (originally motor reproduction), and motivational processes. The second option is the vocabulary of information-processing memory models, and the fourth is operant vocabulary from the behaviorism lesson. The first swaps in terms Bandura did not use and drops motivation's role as a separate gate.

**2. A student watches a classmate get sent into the hallway for calling out answers, and she stops calling out herself. What has happened, in Bandura's terms?**

   A. Vicarious punishment, which suppressed her performance of something she may well have already learned.
   B. Negative punishment, because something she valued was taken away from her.
   C. Extinction, because calling out no longer produces any consequence at all.
   D. Vicarious reinforcement, because seeing the consequence taught her what the teacher expects.

   *Answer: A.* The consequence landed on the model, not on her, which makes it vicarious, and it lowered her willingness to perform, which makes it punishment rather than reinforcement. Nothing was removed from her, so negative punishment is wrong, and calling out is still producing a consequence for the classmate, so this is not extinction. The key point is that her learning is untouched; only her performance changed.
   *Difficulty:* understanding · *Skill:* Distinguishing vicarious consequences from direct contingencies and from effects on learning · *Sources:* openstax-psychology-2e-observational-learning, artino-2007-bandura-and-the-bobo-doll

**3. A teacher demonstrates long division carefully. The next day a student can narrate every step of the procedure aloud but stalls repeatedly when working a problem on paper. Which subprocess is the bottleneck, and what follows?**

   A. Attention, so the demonstration should be repeated with the critical features highlighted.
   B. Retention, so the student needs a mnemonic and more rehearsal of the sequence.
   C. Motivation, so the student needs an incentive tied to finishing the work.
   D. Production, so the student needs guided practice with feedback rather than another explanation.

   *Answer: D.* Narrating every step is direct evidence that attention and retention succeeded: the representation is there and can be reported. What has not been built is the capacity to turn that representation back into performance, which is the production subprocess, and production is repaired by supported practice, not by re-explaining. Motivation is not implicated because the student is attempting the work.
   *Difficulty:* application · *Skill:* Diagnosing which modeling subprocess has failed from evidence about a learner's behavior · *Sources:* pajares-overview-social-cognitive-theory-self-efficacy, artino-2007-bandura-and-the-bobo-doll, schunk-zimmerman-1995-peer-modeling

**4. Which conclusion does the 1961 Bobo doll experiment actually license?**

   A. Exposure to violent media causes children to commit violent acts later in life.
   B. Preschoolers who watched an adult perform novel aggressive acts reproduced those specific acts more than children who saw a nonaggressive model or no model.
   C. Aggression is discharged cathartically, so watching an aggressive model lowers a child's own later aggression.
   D. Reinforcement delivered to the child is necessary before a genuinely new behavior can be acquired.

   *Answer: B.* The study measured imitation of specific modelled acts, in a laboratory, minutes after exposure, following a deliberate frustration procedure. That supports the second statement and nothing stronger. The first overreaches from lab imitation to real-world violence, an inference the APA itself warns against; the third is the catharsis prediction the study contradicted; the fourth is the behaviorist assumption the study was designed to challenge.
   *Difficulty:* analysis · *Skill:* Separating what an experiment demonstrated from what is commonly claimed about it · *Sources:* bandura-ross-ross-1961-transmission-of-aggression, apa-resolution-violent-video-games-2020, galanaki-malafantis-2022-bobo-doll-critique

**5. A student doubts she can handle stoichiometry. Which teacher move works through the strongest source of self-efficacy?**

   A. Telling her repeatedly and warmly that she is a natural at chemistry.
   B. Having her watch the highest-scoring student in the class solve a problem flawlessly.
   C. Giving her a problem just past her current edge, then feedback naming the specific step that made her solution work.
   D. Reminding her that the unit test counts heavily toward her final grade.

   *Answer: C.* Mastery experience is the most influential source, and the third option engineers one: a real success she produced, plus information about the move that produced it so she can repeat it deliberately. The first is social persuasion untethered from attainable success, the weakest and most fragile route. The second is vicarious experience through a model she is unlikely to see as similar, which blunts the effect. The fourth raises the stakes without touching her judgment of capability.
   *Difficulty:* application · *Skill:* Selecting an instructional move that targets the strongest source of self-efficacy · *Sources:* pfitzner-eden-2016-sources-of-teacher-self-efficacy, pajares-overview-social-cognitive-theory-self-efficacy, schunk-zimmerman-1995-peer-modeling
