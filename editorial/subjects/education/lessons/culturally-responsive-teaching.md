# Culturally Responsive Teaching

## In 30 Seconds

Culturally responsive teaching is a family of instructional approaches built on one claim: the knowledge students bring from their households and communities is material to teach with, not a deficit to correct. Gloria Ladson-Billings named three criteria in 1995 - academic success, cultural competence, and critical consciousness. Geneva Gay described the classroom practices, and Django Paris later argued the field should aim to sustain community practices rather than merely respond to them. The theoretical literature is large; the causal evidence for the package is thin.

## Why This Matters

You will meet this term in almost every American teacher preparation program, and you will usually meet it misquoted - as classroom decoration, as a food-and-festival unit, or as permission to expect less. Reading the primary scholarship fixes that, because the criteria the founding scholars actually wrote are about rigor and instruction. The topic also gives you practice at a skill this subject demands constantly: separating what a theory claims from what evidence has established, and keeping both separate from what people want the theory to mean. Teachers, curriculum designers, and school boards argue about this approach, and the argument goes better when everyone can state the criteria correctly.

## Learning Objectives

1. Define culturally responsive teaching and distinguish it from culturally relevant pedagogy and culturally sustaining pedagogy, attributing each term to the scholar who proposed it.
2. State Ladson-Billings' three criteria accurately and explain what each one asks of instruction.
3. Explain funds of knowledge and describe how the concept positions household and community knowledge as an instructional resource.
4. Distinguish culture treated as a repertoire of practice from culture treated as a fixed group trait, and explain why inferring an individual student's needs from group membership is stereotyping.
5. Evaluate the empirical record honestly, separating the packaged approach from the component practices that have independent support.
6. Analyze a classroom scenario and judge whether it meets the criteria the scholarship actually states.

## The College Version

### Three names, three scholars, one family

Three closely related terms circulate, and they are not synonyms. Gloria Ladson-Billings proposed culturally relevant pedagogy in a 1995 American Educational Research Journal article drawn from a three-year study she began in 1988. She worked with eight teachers in a small, predominantly African American, low-income elementary district in Northern California. She did not select them herself: African American mothers at local churches nominated teachers they judged outstanding, principals generated an independent list, and the nine names appearing on both lists were invited, of whom eight agreed. She interviewed each teacher, visited classrooms unannounced roughly three days a week for almost two years, videotaped lessons, and then convened the teachers to analyze one another's tapes across ten long meetings. The theory came out of that record, not out of a program design. Geneva Gay developed culturally responsive teaching along a parallel track, with the emphasis on what a teacher does. On her definition, a teacher draws on what ethnically diverse students already know, what they have already experienced, the frames of reference they reason with, and the styles in which they perform, and uses all of it to make learning encounters land - more relevant, and more effective. Her account of such a teacher begins with academic empowerment: high expectations held alongside a commitment to every student's success. Aronson and Laughter, synthesizing both traditions, draw a useful line - Gay's strand is about teaching and targets a teacher's competence and methods, while Ladson-Billings' strand is about pedagogy and targets a teacher's stance and dispositions. Both strands descend from earlier language and literacy research of the 1970s and 1980s that argued against deficit models, which treated the language and cultural practices of students of color as deficiencies to be replaced.

### The three criteria, stated correctly

Ladson-Billings wrote that the theory had to accomplish three things at once: students who achieve academically, students who demonstrate cultural competence, and students able to understand the prevailing social order and to criticize it. These are the three criteria, and they are frequently misquoted. Academic success comes first and is not decorative. In her study the teachers held that every student was capable of high-level work, refused to let students choose failure, and did not use what she called the language of lacking about them; the students in those classrooms outperformed their district peers on standardized tests, though they still lagged behind students in middle-class communities, and she reported classroom evidence of sophisticated reading, writing, computation, and problem-posing alongside the test data. She later clarified that the criterion is student learning - what students actually know and can do after working with a skilled teacher - rather than a test score, because later adopters had collapsed the two. Cultural competence means students maintain fluency in their own community's practices while gaining access to the wider culture, rather than trading one for the other; her concern was the documented pattern in which academically successful African American students became socially isolated. Critical consciousness, sometimes called sociopolitical consciousness, means students learn to recognize, understand, and critique social inequities using what they are being taught - in her fieldwork, a class researched an abandoned shopping center in their own community and presented a land-use proposal to the City Council. Ladson-Billings also described three broad propositions running underneath the criteria: culturally relevant teachers' conceptions of self and others, the way they structure social relations in the classroom, and their conceptions of knowledge.

### Funds of knowledge, and why the mechanism is prior knowledge

The most concrete piece of machinery in this tradition comes from Luis Moll, Cathy Amanti, Deborah Neff, and Norma Gonzalez, writing in Theory Into Practice in 1992. Working with Mexican and Mexican American households in Tucson, Arizona, they trained teachers to act as ethnographic researchers: to visit homes as learners rather than as evaluators, build relationships with parents, and then design instruction informed by what they found. Funds of knowledge is their term for the bodies of knowledge and skill a household accumulates over time and develops culturally, and that it needs in order to function and to do well. In practice that means the things a family actually runs on: a trade, a repair skill, a small business, budgeting, caregiving, medicine and remedies, the daily work of operating in more than one language. The claim is not sentimental. It is that these are real intellectual resources that ordinary classroom instruction discards. Notice what this makes the mechanism. If instruction connects new material to knowledge a learner already has, the new material has somewhere to attach. That is not a claim unique to this literature; it is the first key finding of the National Research Council's How People Learn synthesis, which reports that students arrive with preconceptions about how the world works and that instruction failing to engage those understandings leaves learners unable to grasp new concepts or liable to revert to their prior beliefs after a test. Read this way, funds of knowledge is a proposal about where to look for prior knowledge - in households and communities the school has usually not asked about - rather than a separate theory of learning.

### Culture is a repertoire, not a trait

The single most important rigor point in this topic is that culture is dynamic and internally varied, and that it is not a property you can read off a student's group membership. Kris Gutierrez and Barbara Rogoff made the argument directly in 2003. They accept that there are regularities in how communities organize activity, and they reject the assumption that those regularities are static traits carried inside individuals and attributable categorically to ethnic group membership. Their alternative is repertoires of practice: the ways of engaging in activities that a person builds up by observing and participating in the practices of the communities they have actually lived in. Repertoires vary within any group, change over time, and expand - part of what schooling should do is add new ones and help students judge which approach fits which situation. Treating cultural difference as a trait, they argue, encourages overgeneralization, assumes every group member shares the same experiences and interests, and has produced a kind of tracking in which instruction gets adjusted purely on the basis of a group category. Paris makes the same warning inside his own proposal, cautioning against essentializing and over-determined links between language, race, ethnicity, and cultural practice. Two practical consequences follow. First, inferring an individual student's instructional needs from that student's demographic category is stereotyping; it is the opposite of what these scholars are asking for, and no amount of good intent converts it back. Second, this approach is not learning styles under a new name. Culture-specific learning-style claims - that members of one group learn holistically and another analytically, that a given group needs a particular seating arrangement - lack research support, and the broader meshing hypothesis has never met its own evidential test. The cognitivism lesson covers that evidence in detail.

### What the evidence actually supports

Be honest here, in both directions. The literature on culturally relevant education is large, but it is dominated by qualitative, conceptual, and small-scale work. Aronson and Laughter's synthesis in the Review of Educational Research surveyed more than forty studies across mathematics, science, social studies, English language arts, and English as a second language, and found real positive examples - mostly for engagement, motivation, and participation - while stating plainly that the available studies are small-scale and that the construct is not defined consistently across them. They cite an earlier review of forty-five classroom studies that identified twelve kinds of culturally relevant action, of which no single study displayed all twelve. Sleeter had already named too little research connecting the approach to student achievement as one of three reasons it stays marginal. Measurement is a live problem: a 2024 systematic review of twenty-seven quantitative measures of culturally responsive practice in U.S. PK-12 classrooms found that most were teacher self-report and concluded that the validity of their interpretation and use is not adequately supported by evidence, with some exceptions. Australia's national education research agency reached a parallel conclusion in 2024 for First Nations education, reporting limited causal evidence, no standardized measures, and too many competing definitions to certify specific practices as evidence-based, while noting convergence in the literature on trust, care, and teacher reflexivity. The strongest quantitative evidence is narrower than the framework. Dee and Penner used a fuzzy regression discontinuity design on a San Francisco ninth-grade ethnic studies course assigned to students with eighth-grade GPAs below a threshold, and found increases of 21 percentage points in attendance, 1.4 grade points in GPA, and 23 credits earned; a preregistered follow-up on 1,405 students found high school graduation up by roughly 16 to 19 percentage points, with postsecondary enrollment effects suggestive but not statistically significant in the full sample. A 2026 study of the same district's expansion to 24,246 students reported a GPA gain of 0.17 points and a 5.6 percentage point drop in course failure. Those are strong results for a specific course, delivered with high fidelity, mostly for students near an at-risk threshold; the authors themselves flag that regression discontinuity estimates are local and say nothing about higher-performing students. They do not establish culturally responsive teaching as a general instructional package. Several components, however, have independent support: raising teacher expectations has been shown in a meta-analysis of nineteen interventions to move expectations and achievement (Hedges' g of 0.38 and 0.30), affective teacher-student relationships show medium-to-large associations with engagement and small-to-medium associations with achievement across ninety-nine studies, and connecting new material to prior knowledge is a settled finding.

### How the field argues about it

The disagreements inside the scholarship are worth knowing, because they are more interesting than the caricature. Paris argued in 2012 that relevant and responsive do not go far enough: it is possible to be responsive to a community's language and practices without committing to their continuing presence in a student's repertoire, so he proposed culturally sustaining pedagogy, whose stated goal is to keep linguistic, literate, and cultural pluralism alive as part of the democratic work of schooling while still giving students access to dominant competence. Ladson-Billings accepted the revision in 2014, treating culturally sustaining pedagogy as a remix of her own theory and emphasizing a more fluid account of culture than a purely racial or ethnic framing allows. Sleeter argued that the approach gets flattened in practice into cultural celebration, trivialization, essentializing culture, and substituting cultural analysis for political analysis of inequality. Separately, the approach and the curricula associated with it are contested in policy; Paris' own 2012 essay was written in part against the closure of a Mexican American studies program under an Arizona statute. This lesson describes what the scholars claimed and what the studies found; it takes no position on any policy, statute, or party. Keeping that line clear is itself part of reading education research well: the empirical question of whether a practice raises attendance is a different question from the normative question of what schools should be for, and neither answer settles the other.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Culturally relevant pedagogy** — Gloria Ladson-Billings' 1995 theory, derived from a study of eight teachers, holding that teaching should produce academic success, cultural competence, and the ability to critique the existing social order.
- **Culturally responsive teaching** — Geneva Gay's account of instruction that draws on what ethnically diverse students already know and have experienced, the frames of reference they reason with, and the styles in which they perform, so that learning encounters are more relevant and more effective; it begins with high expectations for every student.
- **Culturally sustaining pedagogy** — Django Paris' 2012 revision of the goal, which asks schooling to keep the linguistic and cultural pluralism of students' communities alive rather than merely acknowledge it, while still giving students access to dominant language and cultural competence.
- **Funds of knowledge** — Bodies of knowledge and skill that a household builds up over generations and adapts culturally, and that it depends on to function and thrive; teachers identify them through home visits and use them as raw material for instruction.
- **Cultural competence** — In Ladson-Billings' sense, a student's ability to maintain fluency in the practices of their own community while gaining access to the wider culture, instead of trading one for the other.
- **Sociopolitical consciousness** — The third criterion, in which students use what they are learning to recognize, understand, and critique inequities in their own communities and in society; also called critical consciousness.
- **Repertoires of practice** — Gutierrez and Rogoff's term for the ways of engaging in activity that a person builds up through observing and participating in the practices of communities they have actually lived in.
- **Essentializing** — Treating a variable, changing set of community practices as a fixed characteristic that every member of a group shares, which licenses predictions about individuals from category membership alone.
- **Deficit approach** — An older stance, dominant before the 1970s, that treated the language and cultural practices of students of color as deficiencies to be eradicated and replaced with dominant school practices.

## Eli-10

Every student walks into class already knowing a pile of things - how their family runs a small business, two languages, how to fix a bike, what their grandmother's stories are about. Most of that never gets used at school, so it just sits there. Culturally responsive teaching says: use it. Build the hard lessons on top of what a student already knows, because new ideas stick better when they have something to stick to. Two rules make the difference between doing this well and doing it badly. First, the work has to stay just as hard. This is not about making school easier or putting up posters; the teachers Ladson-Billings studied refused to let anyone fail and expected excellent work from everyone. Second, you have to actually ask. You cannot look at a student and guess what they know because of where their family is from. That is a stereotype, and it is exactly the opposite of the idea.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like a piano teacher who starts with the songs a student already plays by ear at home, then uses those songs to teach how to read written music. The student ends up able to do both - not one instead of the other.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The analogy breaks in two places. A song is a thing a student clearly has, while culture is a set of practices a person participates in, varies within, and keeps changing - not a possession you can list. And the piano teacher can hear what the student plays in about ten seconds, while finding out what a student's household actually knows takes real work, which is why the original research sent teachers into homes as learners.

## Worked Example

A seventh-grade teacher is planning a three-week unit on ratio and proportional reasoning. Rather than guessing, she spends part of a parent evening asking families what they do that involves numbers, and hears about scaling recipes for a catering business, converting currency for money sent abroad, calculating fabric for tailoring, and pricing by the pound at a market stall. She builds four problem sets from those contexts, and here is the part that matters: the standard, the difficulty, and the end-of-unit assessment are unchanged. Students still have to set up and solve proportions symbolically, not just estimate. She invites two parents to pose an authentic pricing problem the class then models, which touches the cultural competence criterion, and one task asks students to compare unit prices at the market stall to the nearest supermarket and explain who pays more per pound and why, which touches critical consciousness. What she does not do is assign contexts by student demographics. Every student works every task, because the point is a shared bank of real contexts, not a prediction about individuals from their surnames.

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake.** Treating the approach as decoration or a celebration unit - posters, a heritage month display, a food-and-festival day - and counting that as culturally responsive teaching.

**Correction.** Sleeter identified cultural celebration and trivialization as two of the four ways the approach gets flattened in practice. None of the three criteria is satisfied by decor. Academic success is about what students know and can do, cultural competence is about maintaining community fluency while gaining access to the wider culture, and critical consciousness requires students to analyze real inequities with the content they are learning. A display changes the walls, not the instruction.

**Mistake.** Inferring an individual student's instructional needs from that student's racial, ethnic, or national group - assigning a task, a grouping, or a communication style because of what the group is said to prefer.

**Correction.** This is stereotyping, and Gutierrez and Rogoff argue it is precisely the failure mode the field has to escape. Regularities in community practice are not static traits carried inside individuals, repertoires vary widely within any group, and treating a category as predictive has produced a form of tracking in which instruction is adjusted on the basis of group membership alone. Find out what a particular student and family actually know and do; do not deduce it.

**Mistake.** Treating it as learning styles by another name, including culture-specific learning-style claims such as one group learning holistically and another analytically.

**Correction.** These are different claims with different evidence. Culture-specific learning-style typologies are frequently offered without research linking them to the groups they characterize, and the general meshing hypothesis - that instruction works best when its format matches a diagnosed preference - has never produced the crossover interaction it predicts under adequate designs. The cognitivism lesson covers that record; nothing in the culturally responsive tradition requires or supports it.

**Mistake.** Reading relevance as permission to lower the standard - easier texts, fewer problems, softer grading - for students whose backgrounds the curriculum has not represented.

**Correction.** Ladson-Billings' first criterion is academic success, and Gay's first dimension is academic empowerment through high expectations. The teachers in the 1995 study pressed students relentlessly toward high-level intellectual work, refused to let anyone opt into failure, and used no language of deficiency about them. Relevance changes what a task is about; it does not change how demanding the task is. If the difficulty dropped, the approach was not implemented.

**Mistake.** Overstating the evidence in either direction - calling the approach a proven, evidence-based practice, or dismissing it as having no empirical support at all.

**Correction.** Neither claim survives contact with the literature. The research base is largely qualitative, conceptual, and small-scale, definitions vary across studies, and a 2024 review found that most quantitative measures of the practice lack adequate validity evidence. At the same time, a well-identified regression discontinuity study of a San Francisco ethnic studies course found large gains in attendance, GPA, and credits, replicated for graduation and at district scale. Say what is true: strong results for a specific high-fidelity course, weak evidence for the package, and better independent evidence for several of its components.

## Compare / Contrast

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Culturally relevant pedagogy (Ladson-Billings)** | **Culturally responsive teaching (Gay)** | Both aim at the same students and share a commitment to rigor, but Ladson-Billings' strand describes a teacher's stance, dispositions, and conceptions of knowledge, while Gay's strand describes teaching practice - the methods and competencies a teacher enacts. Aronson and Laughter treat them as complementary, one affecting attitude and one affecting practice. |
| **Culturally responsive** | **Culturally sustaining** | Paris' argument is that a teacher can be responsive to a community's language and practices without any commitment to their surviving in the student's repertoire. Sustaining names that commitment explicitly: maintain community competence and add access to dominant competence, rather than trading the first for the second. |
| **Repertoires of practice** | **Cultural traits** | A repertoire is built from what a person has actually participated in, varies within any group, changes over time, and can be extended by schooling. A trait is treated as fixed, shared by all group members, and predictive of an individual from category membership - which is the inference this literature warns against. |
| **Culturally responsive teaching** | **Differentiated instruction** | Differentiation adapts content, process, product, and environment to each student's assessed readiness for a specific objective, so its engine is pre-assessment. Culturally responsive teaching adapts the content, relationships, and framing of instruction to community knowledge and to a critical purpose, so its engine is finding out what households and communities know. They can be used together and answer different questions. |

## Key Takeaway

Culturally responsive teaching is a claim about instruction and rigor - build demanding work on the knowledge students and their communities already hold - and its founding criteria are academic success, cultural competence, and critical consciousness. Treat culture as a varying repertoire rather than a group trait, and report the evidence honestly: strong for one well-studied ethnic studies course, thin for the approach as a package, better for several of its components.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. In Ladson-Billings' 1995 formulation, what are the three criteria a culturally relevant pedagogy must meet?**

- A. Academic success, cultural competence, and critical consciousness
- B. Cultural celebration, family engagement, and high expectations
- C. Relevance, representation, and rigor
- D. Cultural competence, differentiated content, and student choice

*Answer: A.* Ladson-Billings named academic achievement, demonstrated cultural competence, and the capacity to understand and criticize the prevailing social order as the three things the theory must accomplish. The other options mix real ideas from the wider literature with invented triads: celebration and representation are the very simplifications Sleeter identified, and differentiated content and student choice belong to a different framework.

*Difficulty: recall. Skill: Recalling the three criteria of culturally relevant pedagogy as their author stated them. Sources: ladson-billings-1995-culturally-relevant-pedagogy, aronson-laughter-2016-culturally-relevant-education-synthesis.*

**2. Why did Django Paris propose replacing 'relevant' and 'responsive' with 'sustaining'?**

- A. Because the earlier terms had been shown ineffective in randomized trials
- B. Because he wanted to remove critical consciousness from the framework
- C. Because a teacher can be responsive to a community's practices without committing to their continuing presence in students' repertoires
- D. Because the earlier terms applied only to language instruction and not to other subjects

*Answer: C.* Paris argued that responsiveness and relevance carry no commitment to maintaining heritage and community practices, and proposed sustaining pedagogy to make that commitment explicit alongside access to dominant competence. No randomized trials drove the change, he strengthened rather than removed the critical dimension, and the argument was never limited to language instruction.

*Difficulty: understanding. Skill: Explaining the reasoning behind the terminological shift to culturally sustaining pedagogy. Sources: paris-2012-culturally-sustaining-pedagogy, ladson-billings-2014-culturally-relevant-pedagogy-remix.*

**3. A teacher is preparing a unit and wants to work from her students' funds of knowledge as Moll and colleagues described the idea. Which action best fits that concept?**

- A. Looking up her school's demographic report and choosing contexts typically associated with those groups
- B. Visiting households as a learner to find out what skills and knowledge families use, then building tasks from what she learns
- C. Adding a section on cultural heritage to the end of the unit so it does not disturb the pacing guide
- D. Asking students to complete a learning-style inventory and grouping the unit's activities by the results

*Answer: B.* The original project trained teachers to act as ethnographic researchers, visiting homes as learners and designing instruction from what they observed. Choosing contexts from a demographic report predicts individuals from category membership, which is the inference Gutierrez and Rogoff warn against; appending a heritage section leaves the instruction untouched; and learning-style inventories are an unrelated and unsupported practice.

*Difficulty: application. Skill: Applying the funds of knowledge concept to an instructional planning decision. Sources: moll-1992-funds-of-knowledge-for-teaching, gutierrez-rogoff-2003-repertoires-of-practice, pashler-2008-learning-styles.*

**4. A district handbook states that students from a particular ethnic group are cooperative learners and should therefore always be placed in group tasks. On the scholarship in this lesson, what is the central problem with that instruction?**

- A. It treats a variable repertoire of practice as a fixed trait predicted by group membership, which the literature identifies as stereotyping and as a form of tracking
- B. It is correct in principle but applies the finding to the wrong age range
- C. It is acceptable so long as the teacher also offers individual tasks occasionally
- D. It fails because cooperative learning has been shown to be ineffective for all students

*Answer: A.* Gutierrez and Rogoff argue against locating cultural regularities as static traits inside individuals attributable categorically to ethnic group membership, and note that such reasoning has produced a kind of tracking in which instruction is adjusted purely on a group category. The problem is not the age range, and it is not cured by occasional individual work. Cooperative learning itself is not the issue here; the inference from category to individual is.

*Difficulty: analysis. Skill: Diagnosing the essentializing inference that the literature explicitly rejects. Sources: gutierrez-rogoff-2003-repertoires-of-practice, paris-2012-culturally-sustaining-pedagogy.*

**5. Which statement most accurately characterizes the empirical evidence for culturally responsive teaching?**

- A. Meta-analyses of randomized trials establish a moderate average effect on achievement across grade levels
- B. There is no empirical research on the approach, only theory
- C. The literature is largely qualitative and small-scale with inconsistent definitions and weak measures, while a well-identified study of one ethnic studies course found large effects for students near an at-risk threshold
- D. The approach has been tested only outside the United States, so no claims can be made about American classrooms

*Answer: C.* Aronson and Laughter report a body of small-scale studies with inconsistent definitions; a 2024 review of twenty-seven measures found most were self-report with inadequate validity evidence; and a national research agency concluded the causal evidence remains limited. Against that, Dee and Penner's regression discontinuity study of a San Francisco ethnic studies course found gains of 21 percentage points in attendance, 1.4 grade points, and 23 credits, with the authors noting the estimates are local to students near the assignment cutoff. No meta-analysis of randomized trials supports the first option, empirical work plainly exists, and much of it is American.

*Difficulty: analysis. Skill: Judging the strength and scope of an educational evidence base. Sources: aronson-laughter-2016-culturally-relevant-education-synthesis, franco-2024-culturally-responsive-practice-measures, aero-2024-cultural-responsiveness-in-education, dee-penner-2017-ethnic-studies-causal-effects.*
