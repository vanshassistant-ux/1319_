# Harmony

## In 30 Seconds

Harmony concerns pitches that sound together and the relationships they create. In a song, a vocal melody may unfold one pitch at a time while a guitar or keyboard supplies simultaneous pitches as accompaniment. Those vertical combinations can form chords, and a series of changing chords can form a chord progression. Harmony is not another word for melody, rhythm, tempo, or texture. Introductory terms such as consonance and dissonance can help describe stability or tension in particular musical contexts, but listeners should avoid treating them as universal judgments.

## Why This Matters

Harmony gives listeners a way to describe the support and change underneath or around a melody. It can clarify why a repeated tune feels newly colored, why an arrival seems settled or unsettled, or how several performers coordinate in an accompaniment. Harmony vocabulary also makes listening more precise: it separates successive melodic notes from simultaneous pitch combinations and separates a chord sequence from the rhythm used to play it. Because harmony has many traditions and practices, the most responsible basic analysis stays close to audible evidence and identifies the framework it is using.

## Learning Objectives

- Define harmony as simultaneous pitch combination and relationship.
- Distinguish harmony from melody, rhythm, tempo, and texture.
- Identify a chord as a simultaneous pitch grouping in the introductory Western framework used here.
- Describe a chord progression as a changing sequence of chords over time.
- Use consonance and dissonance as context-sensitive listening descriptions rather than universal value judgments.

## The College Version

### Harmony is the vertical dimension of pitch

Harmony concerns pitches that occur together and the relationships among them. This is why introductory texts often call harmony the vertical aspect of music: if a score is read from bottom to top at one moment, the pitches sounding at that moment form a vertical combination. A melody, by contrast, is a succession of pitches through time. The distinction is not a competition between two kinds of music; it is a way of separating two features that often work together.

Imagine a singer carrying a tune while a keyboard plays several notes underneath. The singer’s sequence can be described melodically: it rises, falls, repeats, or leaps. The keyboard’s notes can be described harmonically when they sound together as a support for that tune. If the keyboard changes its simultaneous pitch combination while the singer holds a single pitch, the harmony has changed even though the melody has not moved. If the singer adds a new pitch while the keyboard combination remains stable, the melody has changed while the accompaniment may be harmonically stable.

Harmony should also be kept separate from rhythm and tempo. A guitarist can strum the same chord in several rhythmic patterns without changing the chord itself. The tempo can speed up while the chord sequence remains the same. Dynamics can make an accompaniment louder or softer without changing its pitches. Texture describes the number and relationship of musical layers; a homophonic texture, for example, often places one melody over chordal accompaniment, but texture is the arrangement of layers rather than the pitch relationship called harmony.

Not every musical practice organizes pitch in the same way or gives harmony the same role. The basic vocabulary in this lesson comes from common Western introductory music theory, where chords and progressions are frequent analytical tools. It is useful within that scope, but it should not be imposed as the only way to hear or value music. The first task is to listen for what actually occurs: simultaneous tones, a drone, an accompaniment pattern, independent lines, or no clear vertical support at all.

### Chords and progressions give harmony changing shapes

In the cited introductory text, a chord is a group of three or more pitches played or sung together. A three-note chord built from stacked thirds is called a triad in common Western terminology. This lesson does not require you to identify every chord quality or spell notes on a staff. Instead, begin by recognizing the listening event: several pitches arrive together, continue together, or are replaced by a new simultaneous grouping.

A series of chords is called a chord progression. Progressions let harmony unfold through time even though each individual chord is vertical. A listener may hear a repeated four-chord pattern, a lingering chord beneath a phrase, or faster chord changes near a transition. The pattern of how often chords change is sometimes called harmonic rhythm. It is different from the rhythm of individual instruments: an arpeggiated accompaniment may play many notes while outlining one chord, whereas a band may strike a new chord only once each measure.

This difference is important for analysis. Suppose a pianist plays the notes of one chord one at a time, from low to high, while a singer sustains a melody. The individual attacks are sequential, but the pitches can still outline a single harmony. The cited text calls a chord played separately rather than simultaneously a broken chord or arpeggio. A listener should therefore ask both what pitches are implied together and how the performance distributes them in time. That question prevents the mistake of equating a busy accompaniment with many harmonic changes.

A chord progression can supply orientation for a melody without becoming the melody itself. In a simple song, the tune may repeat while the chord progression changes under it, or the progression may repeat while a performer varies the tune. Either relationship gives evidence about musical form and variation. For a beginning report, avoid naming a theoretical label that the recording cannot support. It is enough to say, for example, that a stable accompaniment changes to a new simultaneous pitch grouping at the start of each phrase.

### Consonance and dissonance describe relationships in context

Introductory Western theory often describes some intervals and chords as relatively consonant and others as relatively dissonant. In that framework, consonance can be associated with stability or rest, while dissonance can be associated with tension, friction, or a pull toward change. The LOUIS text explains these tendencies while also making an important qualification: hearing consonance as sweet and dissonance as harsh is shaped not only by physical sound properties but also by musical socialization.

That qualification changes how we use the terms. Consonance and dissonance should not become universal rankings of good and bad sound, nor should they be used to assume that every listener experiences the same response. They are more useful as relative descriptions inside a particular musical setting. A pitch combination that creates tension in one passage may be familiar, expected, or stable in another style or performance context. A later chord may sound more settled relative to what came before without requiring a claim about all music or all listeners.

The word resolution is often used when a tension-bearing musical event moves to one that listeners in a given style hear as more settled. This can be a useful observation, but it needs a reference point. Rather than saying, “This chord resolves because it is supposed to,” describe the evidence: “The first combination is held briefly, then changes to a more stable-sounding chord at the phrase ending in this tonal passage.” The first statement assumes a rule; the second tells another listener what to check.

Harmony can also be dense or sparse, stable or changing, but those are not interchangeable labels. A dense chord may stay in place for a long time; a simple chord may change rapidly. A loud dissonance is not automatically a more important harmonic event than a soft consonance. Keep pitch relationship, duration, dynamics, and texture in separate columns of your mental listening notes.

### Listen for support, change, and uncertainty

A useful first harmony analysis has four steps. First, identify whether more than one pitch is sounding at once or whether a sequence seems to outline a simultaneous grouping. Second, notice whether that grouping remains stable or changes. Third, compare those changes with a melody or phrase: does an accompaniment change when the melody begins, pauses, or returns? Fourth, describe how confidently you can hear the result. A short or noisy excerpt may not reveal enough to name a chord, but it can still support a smaller observation such as “low and middle pitches remain together beneath the voice, then move together at the next phrase.”

This method also works when harmony is not supplied by a familiar piano or guitar part. A vocal group may create vertical combinations; a string ensemble may move several lines at once; a bass and drone may establish a pitch background. In polyphonic music, multiple melodic lines can create harmonic results even when no performer is simply “playing chords.” The analysis should name the texture as needed and avoid pretending that every passage has one obvious accompaniment.

Harmony becomes clearer when the listener reports an audible relationship rather than merely listing labels. Explain what sounded together, what changed, what remained, and how the vertical pitch combination related to the line you followed. That is the bridge from a first impression to a responsible musical description.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Harmony:** The simultaneous combination of pitches and the relationships they form in a musical passage.
- **Vertical aspect:** A way of describing pitches heard together at one musical moment, rather than one after another.
- **Chord:** In the introductory Western framework used here, a group of three or more pitches sounding together.
- **Triad:** A three-note chord commonly built by stacking thirds above a root pitch in Western notation.
- **Chord progression:** A series of chords that changes over time in a musical passage.
- **Arpeggio (broken chord):** A chord whose pitches are performed separately in succession rather than struck together.
- **Consonance:** A relative description for a pitch relationship heard as comparatively stable in a particular musical context.
- **Dissonance:** A relative description for a pitch relationship heard as comparatively tense or unstable in a particular musical context.
- **Resolution:** A contextual movement from a tension-bearing musical event toward one heard as more settled.

## Eli-10

Melody is a line of notes that comes one after another. Harmony is what happens when notes are heard together. If one person sings a tune while a friend plays several piano notes underneath, the singing gives you a melody and the piano notes give you harmony.

Those notes together can make a chord. When one chord changes to another, you can hear the harmony move even if the tune stays similar. Some combinations may sound settled and others may sound more tense, but that depends on the musical setting and on what people have learned to hear. Start by noticing what sounds at the same time and when that group changes.

## Eli's Analogy

Harmony is like colors layered in one picture, while melody is like a line drawn across the picture. The line can be easy to follow, but the colors behind it change how the whole image feels. A chord is one combination of colors; a progression is a sequence of changing combinations.

**Where the analogy stops:** Musical pitches are not colors, and harmony unfolds in time even though it concerns simultaneous sound. The analogy helps separate a line from its background combinations but cannot explain exact pitch relationships or every musical tradition.

## Worked Example

A singer repeats a four-note tune while a keyboard accompanies. During the first repetition, the keyboard holds three pitches together beneath the singer. During the second, the keyboard changes to a different three-pitch grouping halfway through the tune; during the third, it returns to the first grouping. The listener can say that the melody repeats while the harmony changes and then returns. If the keyboard breaks the three pitches into individual notes, the listener should not assume that three separate chords have appeared. The notes may be an arpeggio outlining one harmony. If the middle grouping sounds more tense before the return, describe that as a contextual contrast, not as a universal reaction every listener must share.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using harmony as another word for a melody.** Describe a melody as a successive pitch line and harmony as pitches sounding together or their implied vertical relationship.
- **Assuming every fast note in an arpeggio creates a new chord.** Listen for whether the sequential notes outline one pitch grouping or whether the underlying combination actually changes.
- **Treating consonance as universally good and dissonance as universally bad.** Use the terms as context-sensitive descriptions of relative stability or tension in a particular musical framework.
- **Confusing a louder accompaniment with a harmonic change.** Separate dynamics from pitch content. A chord can grow louder without changing, and a quiet chord can introduce a new harmony.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Harmony | Melody | Harmony concerns simultaneous pitch relationships, while melody is a successive line of pitches through time. |
| Chord | Chord progression | A chord is one simultaneous pitch grouping; a chord progression is a series of those groupings changing over time. |
| Consonance | Dissonance | Within a given musical context, consonance is relatively stable and dissonance relatively tension-bearing; neither is a universal value judgment. |

## Key Takeaway

Harmony concerns pitch relationships that sound together. Listen for simultaneous groupings, their changes over time, and their relationship to melody, while using consonance and dissonance as context-sensitive descriptions.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What does harmony describe most directly?**
   - A. The speed of a musical pulse.
   - B. Pitches sounding together and the relationships they create.
   - C. The succession of one melodic line’s pitches only.
   - D. The loudness of a performance.
   - **Answer:** B. Harmony concerns simultaneous pitch combinations.

2. **Which situation is the clearest example of harmony rather than melody?**
   - A. A flutist plays four pitches one after another.
   - B. A drummer repeats a steady beat.
   - C. Three singers sustain different pitches at the same time.
   - D. A violinist increases the loudness of one note.
   - **Answer:** C. Three different pitches sounding simultaneously create a vertical pitch combination.

3. **A singer repeats the same tune while a guitarist changes the simultaneous pitch combination underneath each phrase. What has changed most clearly?**
   - A. The singer’s melody must have changed because the guitar changed.
   - B. The meter must have changed to triple.
   - C. The guitar has necessarily become louder.
   - D. The harmony has changed while the melody can remain the same.
   - **Answer:** D. Changing simultaneous pitch combinations changes the harmonic support.

4. **Which statement uses consonance and dissonance most carefully?**
   - A. They describe relative stability or tension within a musical context, not universal rankings of good and bad sound.
   - B. Consonance always makes every listener happy, while dissonance always makes every listener uncomfortable.
   - C. Dissonance means a performer has played the wrong note.
   - D. Consonance and dissonance describe tempo rather than pitch relationships.
   - **Answer:** A. The terms are context-sensitive descriptions of pitch relationships.

5. **A pianist plays the notes of one three-pitch grouping one after another from low to high while the underlying grouping remains unchanged. What is the best description?**
   - A. The pianist has created a different tempo for every note.
   - B. The passage has no harmony because the notes are not struck together.
   - C. The pianist may be using an arpeggio or broken chord to outline one harmony.
   - D. Each note must be a separate chord progression.
   - **Answer:** C. Sequential notes can outline one chord as a broken chord or arpeggio.
