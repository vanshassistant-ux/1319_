# Chords

## In 30 Seconds

In the introductory Western-theory framework used here, a chord combines three or more pitches sounding together. A triad is a common three-note chord whose pitches can be arranged in thirds; in compact root position, its notes are called root, third, and fifth. Chords can be played at once or outlined one note at a time as arpeggios. Their arrangement, bass note, and quality can vary, so a listener should distinguish a new chord from a new way of presenting the same underlying chord.

## Why This Matters

Chords give concrete names to the simultaneous pitch groupings that support melodies and shape harmonic change. They help listeners explain why an accompaniment remains stable, shifts under a repeated tune, or returns to a familiar sound. A basic understanding of triads also prepares students to read chord symbols, discuss progressions, and compare arrangements without confusing pitch order with chord identity. The concepts in this lesson come from a particular Western notation and theory tradition; they are useful analytical tools, not universal rules for every musical practice.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a chord and a triad in the introductory Western framework used in this lesson.
- Identify the root, third, and fifth of a compact root-position triad.
- Distinguish chord identity from voicing, spacing, and bass placement.
- Explain how an arpeggio can outline a chord without creating a new chord for every note.
- Distinguish a single chord from a chord progression.

## The College Version

### A chord is a simultaneous pitch grouping

A chord is a way of organizing pitches that sound together. In the Open Music Theory and LOUIS introductory framework used here, a chord consists of three or more pitch classes sounding simultaneously. This definition keeps chords connected to harmony: harmony concerns vertical pitch relationships, and chords are particular vertical groupings a listener or performer can identify. A chord is not simply any loud moment, any note on a staff, or a pattern of beats. It is a relationship among pitches.

Chords can appear in many settings. A pianist may strike several keys together, a guitar may strum several strings, or a vocal ensemble may sustain different pitches at once. In each case, the listener can attend to the combined pitch result. The sound source, volume, and rhythm may differ, but the basic question remains the same: which pitches are operating as a simultaneous group? A chord may support a melody, arrive by itself, or arise from several independent lines that happen to create vertical combinations.

This lesson uses common Western theory vocabulary, so its definitions should be scoped accordingly. Other musical practices may use drones, heterophony, layered lines, pitch collections, or other frameworks that are not best understood as a sequence of Western-style chords. The responsible first step is to describe the sound you hear before applying a label. If a passage clearly features a recurring simultaneous pitch grouping, chord vocabulary may help. If it does not, forcing the label can obscure rather than clarify the music.

A chord must also be separated from a chord progression. One chord is one pitch grouping at a moment; a progression is a series of chords that changes over time. A guitarist might hold one chord through an entire verse, or change chords at the beginning of each phrase. The rhythm of the strumming can change many times while the chord remains the same. That distinction is the foundation for recognizing harmonic change rather than merely counting attacks.

### Triads organize three notes around a root

A triad is a common kind of chord containing three notes that can be arranged in thirds. When a triad is stacked in compact root position, its lowest note is called the root, the middle note is called the third, and the upper note is called the fifth. These names describe the notes’ positions relative to the root in the stacked arrangement. They are not a claim that the root must always be the lowest note in every performance.

For example, a C-major triad in compact root position uses C as root, E as third, and G as fifth. A performer may place those pitch classes in different octaves, double one of them, or put a different chord tone in the bass. The resulting sound can have a different spacing or color while remaining an instance of the same triad. This is why listeners should be cautious about assuming that a changed lowest pitch means a wholly new chord. In common Western theory, when a chord tone other than the root is in the bass, the chord is said to be inverted.

Triads are commonly described by quality. The Open Music Theory source lists major, minor, diminished, and augmented triads. These are structural labels based on the intervals above the root, not simple emotion labels. A major triad is not automatically cheerful, a minor triad is not automatically sad, and a diminished triad is not automatically an error. Musical effect depends on surrounding harmony, rhythm, instrumentation, performance, lyrics, style, and context. Use quality words first to identify a pitch structure in the appropriate framework, then make any expressive claim separately and with evidence.

At this level, you do not need to memorize every spelling or symbol. The more important habit is to recognize the three roles in a root-position triad, understand that rearrangement does not necessarily change identity, and hear a triad as a simultaneous collection rather than a three-note melody. Later study can explore inversion, chord symbols, seventh chords, and key-specific harmonic function in more detail.

### Presentation can change without changing the chord

The same chord can be presented in several ways. When its notes are struck together, its vertical organization is immediately apparent. When the notes are sounded separately in succession, the result is an arpeggio or broken chord. An arpeggio can make a single harmony feel active and flowing because the individual notes have rhythm and contour, but it does not automatically produce a new chord at every note. The listener should ask whether the notes continue to outline the same underlying group or whether the pitch content actually changes.

Spacing matters too. A triad may be compact, with its notes near each other, or open, with more distance between notes. Octave doublings can add weight or color. These choices affect texture and timbre as well as the listener’s experience of the chord, but they need not change the root and quality by which the triad is identified. The Open Music Theory source notes that doubling or spacing does not alter triad identification. This is a useful reminder that a chord is more than its exact surface arrangement.

Bass placement is another source of variation. In root position, the root is in the bass. In an inversion, another chord tone is the lowest sounding chord tone. The chord’s root remains the reference for its identity even though the bass changes. A beginner can observe this without drawing a full analysis: “The accompaniment keeps the same three pitch classes but places a different one at the bottom.” This is stronger than claiming a new chord merely because the low note changed.

Written chord symbols are a shorthand system for communicating harmony. In many lead-sheet contexts, a symbol indicates a chord’s root and quality, with additional markings for extensions or a specified non-root bass note. Symbols are useful, but they do not replace listening. A chart may indicate the intended harmony while performers choose rhythm, spacing, register, and articulation. Conversely, an audio excerpt may support the observation of changing groups without supplying enough information to name exact symbols.

### Listen for pitch grouping before assigning a label

A practical chord-listening method has four stages. First, identify moments when several pitches sound together or when a sequence clearly outlines a recurring group. Second, notice whether that group holds, repeats, or changes. Third, separate those changes from the accompaniment’s rhythm, tempo, and dynamics. Fourth, state uncertainty when the recording does not make exact notes clear. For example: “The keyboard repeatedly outlines one three-pitch group, then shifts to a new group at the phrase boundary. The low pitch changes, but I would check a score before naming the exact chord.”

This kind of report is more useful than saying a passage has “lots of chords” merely because many notes are played. It identifies vertical pitch organization, its timing, and the limits of the evidence. It also avoids a different error: calling a simple accompaniment unimportant. A two- or three-chord pattern can organize a song’s phrases and help a listener anticipate returns, even when the melodic line receives more attention.

Chords are a doorway into harmony, not the whole of music. Use them to clarify simultaneous pitch relationships and changing support, then leave room for melody, rhythm, texture, form, timbre, and performance to contribute their own information.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Chord:** In the introductory Western framework used here, a simultaneous combination of three or more pitch classes.
- **Triad:** A three-note chord whose pitch classes can be arranged in thirds.
- **Root:** The reference pitch of a triad; in compact root position, it is the lowest note.
- **Third:** The middle note of a compact root-position triad, a generic third above its root.
- **Fifth:** The upper note of a compact root-position triad, a generic fifth above its root.
- **Quality:** A structural classification of a triad, such as major, minor, diminished, or augmented.
- **Root position:** An arrangement in which a chord’s root is the bass note.
- **Inversion:** An arrangement in which a chord tone other than the root is in the bass.
- **Arpeggio (broken chord):** A presentation in which chord tones are sounded separately in succession.

## Eli-10

A chord is a group of notes that belong together and can sound at the same time. A triad is a very common chord with three notes. In one neat arrangement, the bottom note is called the root, the next is the third, and the top is the fifth.

You can play the notes of a chord together, or spread them out one after another. Spreading them out is called an arpeggio; it can still be one chord, just shown in a different way. You can also move a chord note to the bottom without changing the chord’s main identity. Listen for the whole group of notes, not only the fastest or lowest note.

## Eli's Analogy

A chord is like a three-person team. The root is the team’s reference point, while the other members have particular relationships to it. The team can stand in a different order or arrive one at a time, but it may still be the same team.

**Where the analogy stops:** Pitches do not have jobs or personalities, and a chord can contain more than three notes. The analogy only helps explain why changing the order or timing of notes does not always create a new chord.

## Worked Example

A keyboard player accompanies a singer with the pitch classes C, E, and G. First the player strikes the three notes together. Next, the player sounds C, then E, then G in a repeating pattern. Finally, the player places E in the bass and sounds G and C above it. Across all three versions, the listener can hear the same underlying C-major triad presented with different timing, spacing, and bass placement. The second version is an arpeggio; the third uses an inversion. A new rhythmic pattern or a new lowest note does not, by itself, prove a new chord. The listener should look for a change in the underlying pitch collection before claiming a harmonic change.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling each note of an arpeggio a separate chord.** Check whether the sequential notes continue to outline one underlying pitch grouping before deciding that the harmony has changed.
- **Assuming that a different bass note always means a different chord.** A triad can be inverted, putting a chord tone other than the root in the bass while preserving its root and quality.
- **Treating major and minor as fixed emotion labels.** Use major, minor, diminished, and augmented as structural labels; make expressive claims only with supporting musical and contextual evidence.
- **Confusing a chord with a chord progression.** A chord is one pitch grouping; a progression is a series of chords changing over time.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Chord | Chord progression | A chord is one simultaneous pitch grouping, while a chord progression is a changing series of chords. |
| Root position | Inversion | Root position puts the root in the bass; inversion puts another chord tone in the bass without necessarily changing the chord’s identity. |
| Chord | Arpeggio | A chord identifies an underlying pitch grouping; an arpeggio is one way of presenting its tones separately in time. |

## Key Takeaway

A chord is a simultaneous pitch grouping in the introductory Western framework used here. Listen for its underlying pitch collection, then separate chord identity from its timing, spacing, bass placement, and role in a progression.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In the introductory Western framework used in this lesson, what is a chord?**
   - A. Any sequence of pitches played by one instrument.
   - B. A simultaneous combination of three or more pitch classes.
   - C. A single pitch that lasts for one measure.
   - D. A pattern of strong and weak beats.
   - **Answer:** B. A chord is a simultaneous pitch grouping.

2. **When a triad is stacked in compact root position, what are its three notes called from low to high?**
   - A. Bass, melody, tempo.
   - B. Downbeat, upbeat, measure.
   - C. Root, third, fifth.
   - D. Verse, chorus, bridge.
   - **Answer:** C. A compact root-position triad has root, third, and fifth.

3. **A performer uses the same three pitch classes as before but moves a chord tone other than the root into the bass. What is the best conclusion?**
   - A. The music must have changed tempo.
   - B. Every pitch has become a separate chord.
   - C. The chord has no root once a different note is lowest.
   - D. The triad may be inverted while keeping the same root and quality.
   - **Answer:** D. An inversion changes bass placement without necessarily changing the triad’s root and quality.

4. **Which statement best distinguishes a chord from a chord progression?**
   - A. A chord is one simultaneous pitch grouping; a chord progression is a series of changing chords over time.
   - B. A chord is only sung, while a chord progression is only played on instruments.
   - C. A chord names a tempo, while a chord progression names a dynamic level.
   - D. A chord has rhythm, but a chord progression cannot have rhythm.
   - **Answer:** A. A progression traces harmonic change through a series of chords.

5. **A guitarist plays the notes of one triad one at a time in a repeated pattern while the underlying pitch collection does not change. What is the most careful description?**
   - A. The guitarist has created a new chord for every attack.
   - B. The passage has no harmony because the notes are sequential.
   - C. The guitarist may be outlining one chord as an arpeggio or broken chord.
   - D. The meter has necessarily changed.
   - **Answer:** C. A chord may be presented as an arpeggio with tones sounded separately.
