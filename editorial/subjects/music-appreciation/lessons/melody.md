# Melody

## In 30 Seconds

A melody is an organized succession of pitches through time: the line a listener may hum, sing, or recognize as a tune. It is more than a list of notes. A melody has direction, range, register, rhythmic shape, and often smaller phrase units. It may move mostly by nearby steps or include larger leaps. Melody differs from harmony, which concerns pitches sounding together, and from rhythm, which concerns durations and placements. Listening for a line’s rise, fall, repetition, and boundaries gives you evidence for describing it.

## Why This Matters

Melody helps listeners follow musical identity and motion. It offers a practical entry point for comparing two performances, identifying a recurring tune, or explaining why a new section seems to begin. The vocabulary of range, register, contour, steps, leaps, motives, and phrases turns a vague reaction into a description another listener can test. It also prevents common category errors: a melody is not the same thing as a chord, a tempo, a lyric, or the loudest instrument. Those features may support a melodic line, but they answer different musical questions.

## Learning Objectives

- Define melody as an organized succession of pitches through time.
- Distinguish melody from harmony, rhythm, and timbre.
- Describe melodic range, register, direction, and contour in a short passage.
- Distinguish predominantly stepwise motion from motion that includes larger leaps.
- Identify a short recurring motive or a phrase boundary using audible evidence.

## The College Version

### Melody is a line of pitches through time

A melody is a coherent succession of pitches through time. In everyday listening, it is often the tune a person can hum or whistle after a song ends. That description is useful, but it becomes more precise when we notice what the line does. A melodic line can rise, fall, stay near one pitch area, repeat a shape, pause, or move toward a sense of arrival. It can be carried by a voice, violin, flute, keyboard, or another sound source, and it can be supported by accompaniment without becoming identical to that accompaniment.

The word succession is important. Melody normally concerns pitches heard one after another as a line. Harmony concerns pitches heard together, often as chords. A singer who performs a tune over guitar chords may supply the melody while the guitar supplies harmonic support. Both layers can be important, and the guitar can sometimes play the melody too, but the analytical distinction remains useful: ask whether you are describing a horizontal line of successive pitches or a vertical combination of simultaneous pitches.

Melody also differs from rhythm. Rhythm is the organization of durations, attacks, and silences in time. The same pitch sequence can sound different when its durations change, and the same rhythm can support different pitch sequences. A careful report may therefore name both: “The melody rises by small steps while its final pitch lasts longer than the earlier ones.” The rising pitch pattern is melodic; the longer duration is rhythmic. Tempo, dynamics, and timbre can also shape the listener’s experience, but they do not replace the pitch sequence that defines the melody.

A melody need not be the loudest sound in every moment. In dense music, the most noticeable line may shift between instruments, or several lines may be active at once. The goal of introductory analysis is not to force one answer where the texture is genuinely complex. It is to identify the line you are following and explain the evidence: its pitch motion, repeated shape, entrance, register, or relationship to the accompaniment.

### Describe a melody by range, register, and contour

Range is the distance between a melody’s lowest and highest pitches. A melody with a narrow range stays within a relatively small span; a melody with a wide range covers a larger span. Range is not the same as register. Register describes the general pitch area in which a line sits—low, middle, or high. Two melodies can have the same range but occupy different registers. For example, one line may cover a fifth in a low area and another cover the same fifth much higher. The size of the span is similar, but the general placement is different.

Contour is the overall up-and-down shape of a melody. It gives a listener a way to describe direction without assigning every pitch a letter name. A line that repeatedly rises has ascending contour; one that falls has descending contour; one that rises and then falls can form an arch-like contour. Contour is a useful listening tool because it can remain recognizable even when a melody is moved to a new pitch level or performed by a different instrument. It is not a complete description, however. Two melodies can share a broad rise-and-fall shape yet differ in their exact intervals, rhythm, or phrase structure.

Musicians also describe the motion between consecutive pitches. Stepwise motion moves to nearby pitches, often by a second in common Western terminology. A leap skips over one or more nearby pitch positions. A predominantly stepwise melody can still include a leap, and a line with several leaps may also contain steps. The point is not to label one motion superior to another. It is to say what the line does. A description such as “the opening rises mostly by step, then jumps upward before settling downward” gives a second listener a specific pattern to check.

When reading Western notation, a listener can see contour in the relative height of noteheads on a staff. But notation is not required for first analysis. You can trace a contour in the air while listening, compare whether the next sound is higher, lower, or similar, and mark the places where a repeated shape returns. This approach supports careful listening while avoiding an unwarranted guess about exact pitches from a recording.

### Phrases and motives organize a melodic line

Melodies often contain smaller units. A phrase is a relatively complete musical thought with a sense of beginning, continuation, and end. In a vocal setting, a phrase may align with a line of text, but lyric layout and melodic phrasing are not always identical. A pause, sustained arrival, change in accompaniment, or repeated pattern can help a listener hear a boundary. Instead of assuming that every phrase has the same length, listen for how a line begins, where it seems to pause or arrive, and what starts next.

A motive is a short, recognizable musical idea, often smaller than a full phrase. It might be a compact pitch shape, a rhythm, or a combination of both. When a motive returns at a different pitch level, it is often called a sequence. Repetition can make a melody easier to recognize, but exact repetition is not the only relationship worth noticing. A later phrase may begin with a similar contour, retain the rhythm while changing pitches, or repeat an idea with a different ending. Describe the degree of similarity rather than overstating it.

Consider a made-up four-note opening that rises by step, rises again by step, then falls by step. If a later passage repeats the same three-direction shape higher in the register, the listener can reasonably say that the contour or motive has returned at a new level. If the later passage keeps only the rhythm but uses a different pitch shape, it is more accurate to say that the rhythmic pattern recurs than that the melody is identical. These distinctions matter because musical repetition works through several features at once.

Phrase and motive vocabulary are tools for analysis, not proof of a single correct story about a piece. Different musical traditions organize pitch and time in different ways, and an excerpt may not offer enough evidence to identify a firm boundary. Start with the observations that are available: a long rest, a repeated opening, a sustained tone, a new contour, or a change of accompaniment. Then make a claim proportional to that evidence.

### Make a melodic observation that another listener can test

A strong melodic description begins with the line rather than with a vague judgment. Try this sequence: identify the sound source you are following; describe its general register and range; trace its contour; name stepwise motion or leaps when they are audible; then locate any repetition or likely phrase ending. For example: “The vocal line begins in a middle register and rises mostly by step. After a larger upward leap, it holds the highest pitch before falling by step. The next line begins with a similar rising shape but ends differently.” This gives another listener a route through the excerpt.

Avoid calling every prominent pitch a melody. A chord can be striking without forming a successive melodic line, and a bass pattern can be rhythmic or harmonic without being the tune the listener is tracking. In polyphonic textures, more than one independent melodic line may matter. In those cases, say which line you are describing and do not assume that a single “main melody” is the only meaningful feature.

Melody becomes a powerful listening concept when it points to evidence: pitch direction, span, height, interval motion, repetition, and segmentation. Those observations can support richer claims about form, performance, or expressive effect later. They are also useful on their own, because they tell us how a line moves through time.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Melody:** A coherent succession of pitches through time that is heard as a musical line or tune.
- **Pitch:** The perceived highness or lowness of a musical sound.
- **Range:** The distance between the lowest and highest pitches in a melody.
- **Register:** The general high, middle, or low pitch area in which a melody occurs.
- **Contour:** The overall rising, falling, or mixed shape traced by a melodic line.
- **Stepwise motion:** Melodic movement between nearby pitches, often a second in common Western terminology.
- **Leap:** Melodic movement that skips over one or more nearby pitch positions.
- **Phrase:** A relatively complete musical thought with a perceptible beginning, continuation, and end.
- **Motive:** A short recognizable musical idea that can recur or be transformed within a passage.

## Eli-10

A melody is the path of high and low sounds that makes a tune. If you can hum the part of a song that you remember, you are probably following its melody. The sounds come one after another, and their path can climb, fall, or make a little hill shape.

A melody can move in small nearby steps or make a bigger jump. It can also repeat a tiny pattern. The rhythm tells how long the sounds last, and harmony tells what sounds happen together; melody is the line of pitches you follow. You do not need to know every note name to describe it. You can listen for whether it goes up, down, stays in one area, repeats, or takes a leap.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a melody as a trail across a landscape. Each pitch is a place on the trail, and the contour shows whether the trail climbs, descends, or turns. A phrase is like one stretch of trail that feels complete before the next stretch begins.

**Where the analogy stops:** A melody is heard through time, not seen from above, and musical pitch does not literally have physical height. The analogy helps with direction and shape but does not describe rhythm, harmony, tone color, or every kind of musical organization.

## Worked Example

A listener follows a flute line over quiet chords. It begins with three nearby pitches that rise, then makes one larger jump upward and holds that high pitch. The next two pitches descend by step, followed by a short pause. A second flute phrase begins with the same three rising nearby pitches but, instead of the large leap, turns downward. The listener can describe the first phrase as mostly stepwise with one upward leap, a high-point arrival, and descending motion before a boundary. The opening motive returns in the second phrase, but the ending changes. The chords are harmony supporting the line; the pause and note lengths are rhythmic details; neither fact replaces the melodic contour being described.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling any group of simultaneous pitches a melody.** A melody is a succession of pitches; pitches heard together are generally analyzed as harmony or a chord.
- **Treating range and register as the same property.** Range measures the span from low to high; register identifies the general pitch area in which that span occurs.
- **Assuming a melody must move only by steps or only by leaps.** Describe the predominant motion and the exceptions. Most melodic lines can combine nearby movement with larger jumps.
- **Assigning exact pitch names from a recording when the evidence does not support them.** Start with audible contour, register, direction, and repetition; use exact notation only when a reliable score or clear reference supports it.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melody | Harmony | Melody is a successive line of pitches, whereas harmony concerns pitches sounding together and their relationships. |
| Range | Register | Range is the distance from a melody’s low point to its high point; register is its general pitch area. |
| Contour | Exact pitch sequence | Contour describes the broad direction of a line, while an exact pitch sequence specifies the individual pitches it uses. |

## Key Takeaway

Melody is a coherent succession of pitches through time. Follow its contour, range, register, motion, repetitions, and boundaries, then distinguish those pitch observations from rhythm, harmony, and other musical features.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which description best defines melody?**
   - A. The loudness pattern of a performance.
   - B. A coherent succession of pitches heard as a musical line through time.
   - C. The grouping of beats into measures.
   - D. The simultaneous combination of pitches in a chord.
   - **Answer:** B. Melody is a succession of pitches forming a line or tune.

2. **A tune spans from its lowest note to a much higher note, but remains generally in a high pitch area. Which statement is most accurate?**
   - A. It has no range because it stays high.
   - B. Its register must be low because the span is large.
   - C. It can have a wide range and a high register at the same time.
   - D. Range and register always mean the same thing.
   - **Answer:** C. Range is the size of the low-to-high span, while register is the general pitch area.

3. **A vocal line rises through three nearby pitches, jumps to a higher pitch, then descends through nearby pitches. Which description is best supported?**
   - A. The line has only harmony because pitches occur in sequence.
   - B. The line must have changed meter when it jumped upward.
   - C. The line has no contour because it changes direction.
   - D. The melody is mostly stepwise but includes an upward leap and a rise-then-fall contour.
   - **Answer:** D. Nearby pitch motion is stepwise, the larger jump is a leap, and the whole rise followed by descent forms a contour.

4. **Which statement most accurately distinguishes melody from harmony?**
   - A. Melody is a successive pitch line, while harmony concerns pitches sounding together.
   - B. Melody names the speed of beats, while harmony names their grouping.
   - C. Melody is only for voices, while harmony is only for instruments.
   - D. Melody is a dynamic marking, while harmony is a tempo marking.
   - **Answer:** A. Melody is heard horizontally as a succession of pitches, while harmony is heard vertically as pitch combinations.

5. **A short rising pitch idea returns later at a higher level, with the same general shape but a new ending. What is the most careful description?**
   - A. The later passage is unrelated because the pitches are not identical.
   - B. The later passage proves that the tempo has doubled.
   - C. A melodic motive or contour has returned with variation, so the listener should describe both the similarity and the changed ending.
   - D. The later passage is necessarily the same chord.
   - **Answer:** C. A recognizable short pitch idea may recur at a new level or with variation.
