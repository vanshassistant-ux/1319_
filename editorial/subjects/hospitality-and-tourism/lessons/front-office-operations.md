# Front Office Operations

## In 30 Seconds

The front office is the front desk and the departments around it, and it runs the guest cycle: pre-arrival, when a reservation creates the guest record; arrival, when the guest is registered and given a ready room; occupancy, when charges post to the guest's folio; and departure, when the account is settled. Overnight, the night audit reconciles every account, posts room and tax charges, and closes the hotel's financial day so the next day starts from clean numbers.

## Why This Matters

The front desk is where a lodging property's promises become transactions: the right room, the right rate, the right bill. Almost every later topic in a rooms course assumes you can already place an activity in the guest cycle and read a folio. Learning the night audit teaches something that transfers well beyond hotels, the idea of a daily close that freezes a set of numbers so errors cannot compound silently. And the vocabulary here, from due-out to no-show to guaranteed reservation, is the shared language every front-office role, property management system, and shift handover is built on, so it pays off in the first week of any front-desk job.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the guest cycle and list its four stages in order.
- Explain the front desk's role as the operational hub of the rooms division.
- Distinguish guaranteed from non-guaranteed reservations and the main room-status terms.
- Explain what the night audit reconciles and why closing the financial day matters.
- Apply folio and occupancy arithmetic to a check-out and a desk report.

## The College Version

### The front office and the guest cycle

The front office is the cluster of functions built around the front desk: reservations, registration, guest services, cashiering, and the night audit. It sits inside the rooms division and is directed by the front office manager, who controls which rooms are available and how the desk runs each day. Open-textbook treatments call the front office the property's nerve center, because it is where most of a guest's direct transactions with the hotel actually happen. The work is organized around the guest cycle, the standard operational model that follows a guest from first contact to final bill through four stages: pre-arrival, arrival, occupancy, and departure. Some texts add a fifth post-departure stage for guest-history follow-up, but the four-stage version is the one to know. Each stage is a set of tasks with its own paperwork and its own record in the property management system, and the point of the cycle is that nothing about a guest is improvised: there is a defined step for booking them, checking them in, tracking their charges, and settling their account.

### Pre-arrival: the reservation

The cycle starts before the guest is anywhere near the lobby. A reservation creates the guest record, holds inventory for specific dates, and quotes a rate. Reservations differ by how firmly each side is committed. A non-guaranteed reservation holds the room only until a cutoff, commonly 6 p.m. on the arrival date, the so-called 6 p.m. hold, after which the desk may release the room to someone else. A guaranteed reservation is secured by an advance deposit or a credit-card guarantee, so the room is held all night and the guest is liable for a no-show charge under the hotel's policy. A confirmed reservation is simply one the hotel has acknowledged in writing; it may be guaranteed or not. Getting this right at booking is what lets the desk promise a room and, when someone fails to arrive, decide correctly whether a charge applies. Reservations agents often sell more than the room at this stage, adding amenities and activities to the record.

### Arrival and occupancy: registration, room status, and the folio

At arrival the front desk registers the guest, confirms identity and payment method, and assigns a room. Assignment depends on room status, the shared vocabulary that keeps the desk and housekeeping from selling the same room twice or handing a guest a dirty one. A room is occupied when a guest is registered to it; vacant and ready when it is clean and sellable; on-change when the last guest has departed but housekeeping has not finished; a stayover when the current guest is staying another night; and due-out when the guest is expected to leave after today's check-out time. A room pulled from inventory for repair is out-of-order and cannot be assigned. Once the guest is in-house, the occupancy stage is mostly about the folio, the running account of every charge and credit for that stay. Room and tax post to it, and so do incidentals the guest signs to the room, restaurant meals, parking, telephone, spa. The property management system holds the folio, but the discipline is human: every charge must land on the right account, or check-out becomes an argument.

### Departure and the coordination role

Departure closes the guest side of the cycle. The agent reviews the folio with the guest, collects the outstanding balance, settles to the guarantee on file, and updates the room's status to on-change so housekeeping can turn it over. A skipper, a guest who leaves without settling, is the failure mode this stage exists to prevent, which is why cashiering and identity checks matter as much as courtesy. Underneath these four stages sits the front desk's quieter job: coordination. It is the information hub of the rooms division, relaying room-status changes to housekeeping, passing special requests to the right department, and holding the current picture of who is in the house. The Bureau of Labor Statistics describes desk clerks doing exactly this spread of work, checking guests in and out, assigning rooms, processing payments, keeping occupancy records, and taking, confirming, or changing reservations, while lodging managers, whose median wage was $68,130 in May 2024, coordinate reservations and room assignments and direct the desk staff who carry it out.

### The night audit: closing the financial day

A hotel never really stops, so it needs a moment each day when it agrees on its numbers. That moment is the night audit, run in the quiet overnight hours by a night auditor who is part front-desk clerk and part bookkeeper. The audit is a control process: it reconciles guest accounts and the day's departmental activity so the books are accurate before a new business date begins. The work follows a recognizable sequence. The auditor completes any outstanding charge postings, then posts the day's room charge and room tax to every occupied room, the single largest batch of the night. It verifies no-show reservations and applies charges where the reservation was guaranteed. It checks a rate-variance report to catch rooms sold at the wrong price. It balances each revenue department, food and beverage, telephone, parking, against what the front-office ledger shows, so a charge rung up in the restaurant matches the amount that reached the guest's folio. It reconciles cash and settles credit-card batches. Then it produces the reports management reads over coffee and runs the end-of-day process in the property management system, which rolls the business date forward. That roll is the close: once it happens, the day's totals are frozen, and any error found later is corrected against a fixed record rather than a moving one. The audit also generates the operating statistics the front office lives by, including the occupancy percentage on the desk report.

## Key Vocabulary

- **guest cycle** — The standard operational model of a guest's progress through a lodging stay in four stages: pre-arrival, arrival, occupancy, and departure, each with defined front-office tasks.
- **front office** — The cluster of guest-facing functions built around the front desk, including reservations, registration, cashiering, and the night audit, directed by the front office manager within the rooms division.
- **folio** — The itemized running account of all charges and credits posted to a particular guest's stay, settled when the guest checks out.
- **posting** — Recording a charge or payment onto a guest account, such as adding the nightly room rate and tax or a restaurant bill signed to the room.
- **guaranteed reservation** — A reservation secured by a deposit or credit-card guarantee, so the room is held overnight and the guest is liable for a no-show charge under hotel policy.
- **non-guaranteed reservation** — A reservation the hotel holds only until a cutoff, commonly 6 p.m. on the arrival date, after which the room may be released to another guest.
- **room status** — The current condition of a room for sale, such as occupied, vacant and ready, on-change, stayover, due-out, or out-of-order, used to coordinate the desk with housekeeping.
- **no-show** — A reservation whose guest neither arrived nor cancelled; if guaranteed, the hotel may post a no-show charge during the night audit.
- **night audit** — The daily control process that reconciles guest accounts and departmental revenue, posts room and tax charges, and rolls the business date to close the hotel's financial day.
- **occupancy percentage** — An operating statistic equal to rooms sold divided by rooms available, expressed as a percentage, reported on the night audit's desk report.

## Eli-10

Think of a hotel front desk as the check-in counter, cashier, and information booth for a building full of rooms. When you book, they write down who you are and save a room for you. When you arrive, they hand you a key to a room that has been cleaned and is ready. While you stay, every time you buy dinner or park the car, they add it to your tab, called a folio. When you leave, they add it all up and you pay. Late at night, after most guests are asleep, one worker called the night auditor checks every tab, adds that night's room charge and tax to each one, makes sure the restaurant's numbers match the front desk's numbers, and then locks the day so nobody can change yesterday's totals. The next morning the hotel starts fresh with numbers everyone trusts.

## Eli's Analogy

The night audit is like a cashier counting the drawer and printing the Z-report at the end of a shift: no more sales go on today's tape, the totals are locked in, and the next shift starts from a clean, agreed number.

**Where it breaks down:** A cash-register close balances one drawer for one shift; the night audit reconciles hundreds of open guest accounts at once, pulls in revenue from separate departments like the restaurant and the garage, posts charges to rooms whose guests have not paid yet, and rolls a shared business date for the whole property, so it is closer to a nightly company-wide closing than to zeroing out a till.

## Worked Example

A guest checks out after 3 nights at a rate of $129.00 per night with an 11% occupancy tax. During the night audit each night, the auditor posts the room charge plus its tax to the folio: $129.00 + $14.19 = $143.19 per night, so room and tax total $429.57 (3 × $143.19), matching $387.00 in room charges plus $42.57 in tax. During occupancy the guest signed a $58.40 restaurant charge to the room and used self-parking at $22.00 per night, adding $66.00, so incidentals total $124.40. Total folio charges come to $387.00 + $42.57 + $124.40 = $553.97. The guest left a $150.00 advance deposit at booking, so the balance due at check-out is $553.97 − $150.00 = $403.97.

For that night's desk report, the auditor also computes occupancy. With 132 rooms sold in a 150-room property, occupancy is 132 / 150 = 88.0%. If the property instead counts only the 147 rooms available for sale after setting 3 rooms out-of-order, occupancy reads 132 / 147 = 89.8%. The convention varies by property, so a desk report should say which denominator it uses. All arithmetic here was executed and verified in Python/Bash.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the front office as just the person at the desk who hands out keys.** The front office is a set of functions, reservations, registration, cashiering, and the night audit, that together run the whole guest cycle. Key-handling is one moment in the arrival stage, not the job.
- **Assuming every reservation guarantees the room all night.** Only a guaranteed reservation, backed by a deposit or credit-card guarantee, is held overnight and lets the hotel post a no-show charge. A non-guaranteed reservation is typically released after a 6 p.m. cutoff on the arrival date.
- **Thinking the night audit is just a security guard watching the lobby overnight.** The night auditor's defining task is financial: posting room and tax charges, verifying no-shows, balancing departmental revenue against the front-office ledger, and rolling the business date to close the day's books.
- **Confusing a room's physical readiness with its sales status.** Room-status terms carry both. Vacant and ready means sellable now; on-change means the guest left but the room is not yet clean; due-out means still occupied but leaving after check-out; out-of-order means removed from inventory entirely. Assigning by the wrong status double-sells or delays a guest.
- **Rolling revenue-management pricing into front-office occupancy.** Occupancy percentage is an operational count, rooms sold over rooms available, that the audit reports for the desk. How the room was priced, and metrics like ADR and RevPAR, belong to revenue management, a separate topic.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Guaranteed reservation** vs **non-guaranteed reservation** — A guaranteed reservation is backed by a deposit or credit-card guarantee, so the room is held overnight and a no-show can be charged; a non-guaranteed reservation is held only to a cutoff (commonly 6 p.m. on the arrival date), after which the desk may release the room and no charge applies.
- **Registration (check-in)** vs **night audit** — Registration is a single guest-facing arrival transaction, matching one guest to one ready room; the night audit is a back-office daily process touching every account at once to reconcile charges and close the financial day.
- **On-change room** vs **out-of-order room** — On-change is temporary and expected: the guest has departed and housekeeping will soon make the room sellable. Out-of-order is deliberate removal from inventory for repair or deep work, so it is not counted as available for sale.
- **Occupancy percentage (front office)** vs **ADR / RevPAR (revenue management)** — Occupancy is an operational count of rooms sold over rooms available that the night audit reports; ADR and RevPAR are pricing and yield measures owned by revenue management and are not computed to run the desk.

## Key Takeaway

The front office runs the guest cycle, pre-arrival, arrival, occupancy, departure, with the front desk as the rooms division's operational hub; the night audit then reconciles every folio, posts room and tax charges, and rolls the business date to close the financial day so each new day starts from numbers everyone trusts.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. What are the four stages of the guest cycle, in order?** *(recall)*

- A. Pre-arrival, arrival, occupancy, departure ✓
- B. Marketing, booking, cleaning, billing
- C. Reservation, night audit, housekeeping, check-out
- D. Arrival, occupancy, departure, post-departure

The guest cycle is conventionally taught as pre-arrival (reservation), arrival (registration and room assignment), occupancy (guest services and folio postings), and departure (check-out and settlement). The other lists mix in departments, a back-office process, or drop the pre-arrival stage; some texts append a fifth post-departure stage, but it follows departure rather than replacing pre-arrival.

**2. During the night audit, what is the single largest batch of charges the auditor posts to guest accounts?** *(understanding)*

- A. Restaurant and bar checks from the evening service
- B. Refunds for guests who checked out that morning
- C. The night's room charge and room tax for every occupied room ✓
- D. Advance deposits for reservations arriving next week

Posting the day's room charge and room tax to every occupied room is the defining, highest-volume posting of the night audit. Restaurant and bar revenue is balanced during the audit but reconciled against the ledger rather than being the room-charge batch; refunds and future deposits are not the nightly room posting.

**3. A guest with a non-guaranteed reservation has not arrived or called by 9 p.m. on the arrival date, and the hotel is filling up. Under standard front-office practice, what may the desk do?** *(application)*

- A. Post a full no-show charge to the guest's credit card
- B. Hold the room untouched until the guest's original departure date
- C. Require the guest to pay for the entire stay before the room is released
- D. Release the room to another guest, because the 6 p.m. hold has passed and no charge applies ✓

A non-guaranteed reservation is held only until a cutoff, commonly 6 p.m. on the arrival date. After that, with no deposit or credit-card guarantee on file, the desk may release the room and cannot post a no-show charge. A no-show charge would be appropriate only for a guaranteed reservation.

**4. A guest stays 2 nights at $140.00 per night with a 10% occupancy tax, signs a $30.00 dinner to the room, and paid a $100.00 deposit at booking. What balance is due at check-out?** *(application)*

- A. $338.00
- B. $210.00
- C. $238.00 ✓
- D. $208.00

Room charge is 2 × $140.00 = $280.00; tax is 10% of $280.00 = $28.00; the dinner adds $30.00, giving total folio charges of $338.00. Subtracting the $100.00 advance deposit leaves a balance due of $238.00. $338.00 forgets to credit the deposit; $210.00 omits the tax; $208.00 omits the dinner charge.

**5. Why does the night audit roll the business date only after accounts are reconciled, and what does that accomplish?** *(analysis)*

- A. It lets the desk keep editing the day's charges into the following afternoon
- B. It freezes the day's totals so any later error is corrected against a fixed record rather than a moving one ✓
- C. It transfers all unpaid folios to the housekeeping department for collection
- D. It sets room rates for the coming day based on the occupancy just achieved

Rolling the business date is the daily close: once the day's reconciled totals are frozen, later corrections are made against a fixed record, so errors cannot compound silently. It does not keep the day editable, hand folios to housekeeping, or set rates, pricing is a revenue-management function, not part of closing the books.
