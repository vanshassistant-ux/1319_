# Housekeeping Operations

## In 30 Seconds

Housekeeping is the department that turns a checked-out, dirty room back into a clean, sellable one. It cleans and inspects guest rooms and public areas, tracks each room's status, and hands the front office rooms that are ready to sell. It is the most labor-intensive part of the rooms division, so its whole job is organizing people, time, and linen well enough that a guest never sees the work that went into the room they walk into.

## Why This Matters

Rooms are where a hotel makes most of its money, and a room cannot be sold until housekeeping says it is ready. That makes this department the quiet hinge between the front desk and the guest: get the timing wrong and check-ins stall, get the cleaning wrong and reviews suffer. Because the work is done by hand, room by room, labor is the department's largest cost, which is why housekeeping is measured so closely on minutes per room and rooms per shift. Understanding par levels, room status, and productivity here gives you the vocabulary that later lessons on the front office, revenue, and staffing all assume you already have.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the housekeeping department's role in room readiness, cleanliness, and the rooms-division cost structure.
- Describe the cleaning workflow from room assignment through cleaning standards to inspection.
- Distinguish the main room status terms and explain how they coordinate housekeeping with the front office.
- Apply a par-level calculation to size linen inventory.
- Apply a productivity standard to estimate rooms per shift and the staffing a day's arrivals require.

## The College Version

### What housekeeping is responsible for

Housekeeping is the operational department that keeps a lodging property clean and its guest rooms ready to sell. Its reach is wider than the bedrooms: guest rooms, corridors, lobbies and other public areas, the linen and uniform rooms, and usually the laundry all fall under it. The department's product is a specific, perishable thing, a room that is clean, stocked, inspected, and available right now, and everything it does is organized around producing that product on time. Because a hotel earns most of its revenue from rooms, and a room that is not ready cannot be sold, housekeeping sits directly on the property's ability to make money even though guests rarely think about it. The work is done by hand and room by room, which is why the department is the most labor-intensive in the rooms division. University hospitality texts describe labor as the single largest cost element within housekeeping and the department as holding the largest workforce in the hotel; linen is typically its second-largest expense. Those two facts, labor first and linen second, explain almost everything about how the department is managed: it is a constant exercise in getting enough clean rooms produced per paid hour of work while keeping enough linen in circulation to never run out.

### The cleaning workflow

A shift begins with assignments. A supervisor divides the property's rooms into sections and gives each room attendant a worksheet or mobile task list showing which rooms to clean and in what state each one is: a checkout that needs a full clean, a stayover that needs a lighter service, a room flagged for a special request. Attendants stock a cart with linen, amenities, and cleaning supplies, and work each room to a written standard, a standard operating procedure that specifies the order and detail of the work so that every room of a given type comes out the same regardless of who cleaned it. Consistency is the point of the SOP: a guest should not be able to tell which attendant made up the room. After cleaning, a room is not automatically ready to sell. A supervisor or inspector checks a sample or all of the cleaned rooms against the standard, and only an inspected, passing room is released as sellable. This three-step rhythm, assign, clean to standard, inspect, is the core of the operation, and each step produces information about room status that the rest of the hotel depends on.

### Room status and the language housekeeping shares with the front office

Every room is always in some status, and the status is a shared vocabulary that lets housekeeping and the front office stay synchronized. The common labels: occupied (a guest is registered to the room); on-change, also written vacant-dirty, meaning the guest has departed but the room is not yet cleaned; vacant-clean, meaning it has been cleaned; vacant-ready, meaning it has been cleaned and inspected and can be assigned to a new guest; and out-of-order, meaning it has been pulled from sale, usually for maintenance. An out-of-order room is deducted from the count of rooms the front office can sell that night, which is why the status matters to revenue and not just to cleaning. These two departments make and sell the same product, so they exchange information constantly: the front office sends housekeeping the day's arrival and departure lists and passes along guest requests, complaints, and lost-and-found reports, while housekeeping tells the front office which rooms have moved to ready. This lesson stops at the shared status link; the reservations, check-in, and night-audit side of that coordination belongs to Front Office Operations.

### Status discrepancies and why they are dangerous

Because two departments are updating a picture of the same rooms, their pictures can drift apart, and a housekeeping room status report exists specifically to catch that drift. An attendant physically checks each room and records its actual state; that report is then compared against what the front-office system shows. A mismatch is a status discrepancy, and the classic cases have names. A sleep-out is a room the front office shows as occupied but which the guest did not sleep in. A skipper is a guest who has left with no intention of paying. A discrepancy in the other direction is the dangerous one for service: if housekeeping's cleaned-and-ready rooms are not reflected in the front-office system, the desk cannot sell rooms it actually has, or, worse, it can assign a room the system wrongly shows as vacant to a new guest while the previous guest is still in it. Resolving discrepancies quickly is therefore not clerical tidiness; it protects both revenue and the guest from an embarrassing double-assignment.

### Par levels and linen inventory

Linen is the department's second-largest expense and its most easily mismanaged, because it is always moving: onto beds, into the laundry, back onto shelves. The tool that keeps it from running out is the par level. A par is one complete set of the linen a property needs to outfit all of its beds and baths once; the par level, or par stock, is the number of those complete sets management decides to own and keep in circulation. A hotel never runs on a single par, because at any moment one set is in use and another is being washed. Hospitality texts commonly teach carrying roughly three to five par, and one worked example distributes four par as one set in the rooms, one in the laundry, one in the floor linen closet, and one in the main linen room. The right number depends on how fast the laundry turns linen around: a property with a slow or outsourced laundry needs more par to bridge the gap, while a fast on-premise laundry can run on less. The same logic governs guest amenities and cleaning supplies, which are reordered against minimum stock levels so the carts are never short. Linen also moves under exchange rules, most simply one-for-one, where fresh linen is issued only when an equal count of soiled linen is returned, which keeps the inventory honest.

### Productivity, staffing, and laundry

Since labor is the largest cost, housekeeping is measured on how efficiently it converts paid hours into ready rooms. Two related standards do this. Minutes per room sets a target time to clean a room of a given type to standard, typically longer for a checkout than for a stayover. Rooms per shift, sometimes counted in credits so that a demanding checkout counts for more than a light touch-up, sets how many rooms an attendant is expected to complete in a shift after breaks, briefings, and cart restocking are subtracted from paid time. A manager uses these to translate the day's expected departures into a staffing number: expected dirty rooms divided by rooms per attendant gives the attendants needed. Setting the standard is a genuine trade-off, because pushing minutes per room too low raises injury risk in what is already a physically hard job. California recognized this directly: Cal/OSHA's Title 8, Section 3345, effective July 1, 2018, requires covered hotels to maintain a written Musculoskeletal Injury Prevention Program, with worksite evaluation, training, and hazard correction, and it is the first ergonomics standard in the United States written specifically for hotel housekeepers, since there is no federal ergonomics standard. Laundry closes the loop on all of this. Whether run on-premise or sent out, the laundry sets how fast soiled linen becomes clean par again, and that turnaround time is exactly what determines how many par the department must own.

## Key Vocabulary

- **housekeeping department** — The operational department responsible for the cleanliness and readiness of guest rooms and public areas, and for the linen, uniform, and usually laundry functions that support them.
- **room status** — A label describing a guest room's current condition and availability, such as occupied, vacant-dirty, vacant-clean, vacant-ready, or out-of-order, shared between housekeeping and the front office.
- **on-change (vacant-dirty)** — A room whose guest has checked out but which has not yet been cleaned, so it cannot be sold until housekeeping services it.
- **vacant-ready** — A room that has been cleaned and inspected and may be assigned to an arriving guest; the end state housekeeping is producing.
- **out-of-order (OOO)** — A room removed from sale, usually for maintenance or deep cleaning, and therefore deducted from the count of rooms the front office can sell.
- **status discrepancy** — A mismatch between the room condition housekeeping physically observes and the condition the front-office system shows, such as a sleep-out or a skipper, that must be reconciled.
- **par level (par stock)** — The number of complete sets of linen or supplies a property keeps in circulation so that clean stock is always available while other sets are in use or being laundered.
- **minutes per room** — A performance standard setting the target time to clean a room of a given type to the property's standard, used to plan workloads and staffing.
- **rooms per shift (credits)** — The number of rooms an attendant is expected to complete in a shift, sometimes weighted in credits so that heavier checkout cleans count for more than light stayover services.
- **standard operating procedure (SOP)** — A written cleaning standard specifying the order and detail of the work so that every room of a given type is produced consistently regardless of who cleans it.

## Eli-10

A hotel sells the same rooms over and over, but only after each one is cleaned back to like-new. Housekeeping is the crew that does that. Every morning a supervisor hands each cleaner a list of rooms and whether each needs a big clean (someone checked out) or a quick tidy (someone is staying another night). The cleaner follows the same checklist every time so rooms come out matching, then a supervisor inspects before the room is called ready. Everyone tracks each room with a status word, like dirty, clean, ready, or out-of-order, so the front desk knows exactly which rooms it can hand to the next guest. The department also has to own enough sheets and towels that it never runs out while a batch is in the wash, and it watches the clock closely because paying people to clean is its biggest expense.

## Eli's Analogy

Housekeeping is like the pit crew in a car race. The car (the room) only makes money while it is out on the track being sold, so the crew's whole job is to get it cleaned, restocked, and checked and back out as fast as possible without cutting corners, and the front desk is the driver waiting for the signal that it is ready to go.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A pit stop is over in seconds and the same crew touches one car; a hotel has dozens of rooms cleaned in parallel by different attendants working to a written standard, with an inspection step in between, and the guest, not a driver, is the one who lives in the result. The race analogy also makes it sound purely about speed, when housekeeping is really a balance between speed, consistent quality, and the safety of workers doing physically hard labor all day.

## Worked Example

Two everyday calculations show how the department is sized.

**Par level.** A 120-room hotel gives each occupied room 4 bath towels and sets a 3-par standard (one towel set in the room, one in the laundry, one on the shelf). Towels to own and keep in circulation = 120 rooms x 4 towels x 3 par = **1,440 towels**. Owning less risks running out whenever the laundry falls behind.

**Staffing from a productivity standard.** Attendants work a 7.5-hour shift = 450 minutes, but roughly 60 minutes go to briefing, breaks, and restocking the cart, leaving 390 productive minutes. At a standard of 30 minutes per checkout room, each attendant can clean 390 / 30 = **13 rooms per shift**. If 156 rooms are due to be cleaned, the department needs 156 / 13 = **12 attendants**. Push the standard down to 26 minutes per room and each attendant covers 15 rooms and only about 11 attendants are needed, which cuts labor cost but raises the pace, and the pace is exactly what worker-safety rules like Cal/OSHA's Section 3345 are meant to keep in check.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Thinking a room is sellable the moment an attendant finishes cleaning it.
  **Correction:** A cleaned room is vacant-clean, not vacant-ready. It becomes sellable only after inspection passes it, which is why the workflow is assign, clean to standard, then inspect.
- **Mistake:** Treating room status as a housekeeping-only detail.
  **Correction:** Room status is the shared language between housekeeping and the front office. An out-of-order room drops out of the sellable count, and a status discrepancy can cause the desk to assign an occupied room or fail to sell a ready one, so status directly affects revenue and guest experience.
- **Mistake:** Assuming a hotel only needs one full set of linen for its beds.
  **Correction:** At any moment one set is in use and another is in the wash, so properties carry several par. The right par depends on laundry turnaround: slower or outsourced laundry needs more par to avoid running out.
- **Mistake:** Reading rooms per shift as a fixed number that ignores non-cleaning time.
  **Correction:** The figure is derived from productive minutes after breaks, briefings, and restocking are removed, divided by the minutes-per-room standard. Ignoring the non-cleaning time overstates how many rooms an attendant can actually finish.
- **Mistake:** Believing the United States has a federal rule capping how much a housekeeper must clean.
  **Correction:** There is no federal ergonomics standard. California's Cal/OSHA Section 3345 (effective July 1, 2018) requires a written injury-prevention program for hotel housekeepers, but it is a state standard, and it mandates a program rather than a specific square-footage cap.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Vacant-clean vs. Vacant-ready:** Vacant-clean means an attendant has finished the room; vacant-ready means it has also passed inspection and can be assigned to a guest. Only vacant-ready is sellable, so the inspection step separates the two.
- **On-change (vacant-dirty) vs. Out-of-order:** An on-change room is simply awaiting cleaning and will return to inventory today; an out-of-order room has been deliberately pulled from sale, usually for maintenance, and is subtracted from the sellable room count until it is fixed.
- **Minutes per room vs. Rooms per shift (credits):** Minutes per room is the time standard for one room of a given type; rooms per shift is what that standard produces across a whole shift after non-cleaning time is removed, sometimes weighted in credits so heavier cleans count for more.
- **Sleep-out vs. Skipper:** A sleep-out is a guest who is registered but did not use the bed, a status the physical check catches; a skipper is a guest who has left without paying. Both surface as discrepancies between housekeeping's observation and the front-office record, but one is a revenue-collection problem and the other is not.

## Key Takeaway

Housekeeping produces the hotel's core, perishable product, a clean and inspected room that is ready to sell, through a workflow of assign, clean to standard, and inspect; it stays synchronized with the front office through shared room-status labels, and because labor is its largest cost it is managed tightly by par levels for linen and by minutes-per-room and rooms-per-shift standards for people.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In the shared room-status vocabulary, what does a room marked vacant-ready mean?**
   - A. A guest is registered to the room but has not yet arrived
   - B. The room has been cleaned and inspected and can be assigned to a new guest ✓
   - C. The guest has checked out but the room has not yet been cleaned
   - D. The room has been pulled from sale for maintenance

   Vacant-ready is the end state housekeeping is producing: cleaned and then passed by inspection, so the front office may assign it. A guest not yet arrived describes an occupied or reserved room; a departed but uncleaned room is on-change (vacant-dirty); a room pulled for maintenance is out-of-order.

2. **A 150-room hotel puts 3 bath towels in each occupied room and sets a 4-par standard for towels. How many bath towels should it own and keep in circulation, and why more than one set?**
   - A. 1,800 towels, because at any moment sets are in the rooms, in the laundry, and on the shelves while others are held in reserve ✓
   - B. 450 towels, because a hotel only needs one complete set for its rooms
   - C. 600 towels, because par level counts only the towels currently on the beds
   - D. 150 towels, because par level counts rooms rather than the items inside them

   Par stock is complete sets kept in circulation, so towels to own = 150 rooms x 3 towels x 4 par = 1,800. A hotel carries several par because at any moment one set is in use and another is being laundered; a single par (450) would run out whenever the laundry falls behind, and par counts the items per room across all sets, not just the beds or the rooms alone.

3. **During a physical room check, an attendant finds a room the front-office system lists as occupied, but the bed is unused and the guest's belongings are gone. Why does housekeeping report this immediately rather than treat it as routine?**
   - A. Because an unused bed means the room does not need cleaning and can skip inspection
   - B. Because the front office, not housekeeping, is responsible for assigning room numbers
   - C. Because out-of-order rooms must always be reported before out-of-service rooms
   - D. Because it is a status discrepancy that can flag a sleep-out or a skipper and must be reconciled to protect revenue and prevent a mis-assignment ✓

   A mismatch between the observed condition and the front-office record is a status discrepancy; an unused bed with the guest gone can indicate a sleep-out or a skipper, both of which affect billing and inventory and must be resolved so the desk neither loses revenue nor assigns a room in the wrong state. The room still needs servicing and inspection, room numbering is not the issue, and there is no rule ordering out-of-order before out-of-service reports.

4. **Why does marking a room out-of-order matter to the front office and not only to housekeeping?**
   - A. It moves the room to the front of the cleaning queue for that shift
   - B. It automatically raises the nightly rate charged for the remaining rooms
   - C. It removes the room from the count of rooms available to sell that night ✓
   - D. It transfers responsibility for cleaning that room to the front desk

   An out-of-order room is deliberately taken out of sale, usually for maintenance, so it is deducted from the sellable room count the front office works from, which affects availability and revenue. It does not reprioritize the cleaning queue, does not change room rates, and does not move cleaning responsibility to the front desk.

5. **Attendants work a 7.5-hour shift, of which about 60 minutes go to briefing, breaks, and restocking. At a standard of 30 minutes per checkout room, roughly how many attendants are needed to clean 156 checkout rooms?**
   - A. 12 attendants, because each attendant cleans about 13 rooms per shift ✓
   - B. 9 attendants, because each attendant cleans about 18 rooms per shift
   - C. 16 attendants, because the full 7.5 hours is spent cleaning
   - D. 6 attendants, because two rooms can be cleaned at once

   A 7.5-hour shift is 450 minutes; subtracting 60 non-cleaning minutes leaves 390 productive minutes, and 390 / 30 = 13 rooms per attendant. To clean 156 rooms, 156 / 13 = 12 attendants. Using the full 450 minutes ignores non-cleaning time and overstates output; the other figures do not follow from the standard.
