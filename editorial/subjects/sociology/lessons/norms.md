# Norms

## In 30 Seconds

Norms are the shared expectations about how people should behave — the unwritten rules that keep everyday life running. Sociologists sort them into folkways, everyday customs like standing on the right side of an escalator, and mores, morally charged rules like keeping a serious promise. Some norms are formalized as laws. Norms are learned, enforced through approval and disapproval, and often internalized until following them feels automatic. Break one and people react — though the same act gets very different reactions in different settings, and norms themselves change over time.

## Why This Matters

Academically, norms are a cornerstone of sociology: the folkway/mores distinction, formal norms, and sanctions reappear across courses on culture, deviance, and social control. Practically, norms explain everyday puzzles — why a quiet library feels violated by a loud phone call, why a new workplace runs on habits nobody wrote down, why some rules feel moral and others merely customary. And it matters forward-looking: as norms change, so do expectations about everything from road safety to how people treat one another. Learning to spot norms, and to notice when they shift, is a skill for every group you will ever join.

## The College Version

### What norms are

Norms are the shared expectations about how people should behave. Sociologists describe them as the visible and invisible rules of conduct through which societies are structured — rules most members know and mostly follow (OpenStax, Introduction to Sociology 3e, section 3.2). Three details matter. First, norms are shared: they belong to a group, not to one person, so a personal habit is not a norm. Second, they are mostly unwritten: no sign in an elevator says to face the doors, yet nearly everyone does, and the rare rider who faces the back wall gets noticed. Third, they cover everything from tiny routines — which side of the escalator to stand on, how close to stand when talking — to weighty expectations about trust. Original example: in a college library, students routinely save a seat by leaving a backpack on it while browsing. A first-year student unused to the practice moves the backpack and sits; the returning owner is startled, and the newcomer is embarrassed. Neither broke a written rule — but both felt the pull of an expectation nobody had explained.

### Folkways and mores

Sociologists sort norms into two broad kinds, a distinction traced to William Graham Sumner (1906). Folkways are norms without moral weight: everyday customs that grease daily routines — how people greet each other, how close they stand, which side of the escalator they ride. Breaching a folkway draws mild disapproval: a frown, a glance, a muttered comment (Britannica, "Folkway"). Original example: in many U.S. workplaces it is a folkway to say hello when you pass a coworker in the hall. The colleague who walks past silently is not immoral — but people notice, and the silence reads as odd. Mores (pronounced MOR-ays) are norms that carry a group's moral views. Violating a more is treated as a moral failure: the person is seen as shameful, and the reaction is strong disapproval or punishment (OpenStax 3.2). Original example: borrowing a friend's car and returning it with a dent, without mentioning it, is treated as a betrayal of trust — a moral judgment, not a breach of etiquette. The two kinds shade into each other: folkways can harden into mores over time as a group comes to see a custom as essential to its welfare (Britannica).

### Laws: the formalized norms

Some norms are written down and given formal enforcement. Sociologists call these formal norms, and laws are the clearest example: established, written rules, the most specific and strictly enforced kind of norm (OpenStax 3.2). One line captures it: a law is a norm that a society has formalized — written into a rule and enforced through its institutions. The traffic code, workplace policies, and building codes are all formal norms. The full story of how laws relate to crime belongs to a separate topic; here it is enough to see that beneath every law sits a shared expectation about how people should behave.

### How norms work: learned, enforced, internalized

Norms operate through three processes. Learned: nobody is born knowing to cover a cough or wait in line. Children pick up norms by watching others and being corrected — the toddler who learns, after a few gentle reminders, to cover her mouth when she sneezes is absorbing a norm (OpenStax: folkways are learned by observation and imitation). Enforced: norms are maintained by sanctions, the rewards and punishments that encourage conformity — a smile and a thank-you for letting someone board first, a scolding for shoving (OpenStax 3.2). Original example: the cyclist who rolls through a red light earns honks and glares; the one who waits earns nothing — yet the asymmetry steers behavior daily. Internalized: with time, many norms become part of a person's own sense of right and wrong, followed automatically even with nobody watching. Original example: standing a comfortable distance from a stranger while talking — most people adjust it without thinking, not from fear of a penalty but because crowding someone simply feels wrong.

### Violations, reactions, and two honest notes

Breaking a norm draws reactions, and sociologists have long used deliberate violations to expose the unwritten rules people rely on; breaching experiments reveal the invisible structure of everyday life (OpenStax 3.2). Original example: at a quiet film screening, a viewer answers a phone call and chats at full volume. Heads turn, people sigh, someone shushes. The honest note about reactions: they depend on context. The same loud call in a packed stadium draws no reaction at all — the act is identical, only the setting and audience differ. The honest note about norms themselves: they change over time. A grandparent who rode loose in the back seat as a child would find that today the same parent straps a toddler into a car seat without a second thought — what was once unremarkable is now expected, and what was once a folkway has hardened into a moral expectation. Norms are not eternal; they are living expectations that groups revise. The honest framing: norms are the invisible traffic lights of social life. They coordinate daily interactions — who goes, who waits, who yields — without a single posted sign, and you notice them most when one goes missing. The reality check: unlike real traffic lights, norms are not uniform or permanent. A green light means the same in every city; a norm that is polite in one group can be rude in another, and every norm is a product of its time and place.

## Key Vocabulary

- **norm** — A shared expectation about how people should behave in a given situation; the visible and invisible rules of conduct through which societies are structured.
- **folkway** — A norm based on everyday custom rather than morality, such as which side of an escalator to stand on; breaching one draws only mild disapproval. The term was coined by William Graham Sumner (1906).
- **more** — A norm that carries moral weight for a group, such as keeping a serious promise; violating one brings shame, strong disapproval, or punishment. Pronounced MOR-ay.
- **formal norm** — A norm that has been established as a written rule, such as a law, an employee manual, or a posted safety rule.
- **law** — A formal norm: a written rule that a society establishes and enforces through its institutions.
- **sanction** — A reward or punishment used to encourage conformity to norms, ranging from a smile or a thank-you to a frown or a scolding.
- **internalization** — The process by which a norm becomes part of a person's own sense of right and wrong, so that they follow it automatically even when no one is watching.
- **breaching experiment** — A research technique in which someone deliberately breaks a norm in order to expose the unwritten rules that people normally rely on without noticing.

## Eli-10

**Simplified explanation.** A norm is a shared expectation about how people should behave — an unwritten rule that most people in a group follow without being told. Some norms are small customs, like which side of the escalator to stand on. Some carry moral weight, like keeping a serious promise. A few are written into law. People pick norms up by watching and being corrected, follow them partly because others approve or disapprove, and often end up following them automatically. Break a norm and people react — but the same act gets different reactions in different places, and norms themselves change over time.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** Real traffic lights are posted, uniform, and enforced by a visible authority; norms are none of those. A green light means the same thing in every city, but a norm that is polite in one group can be rude in another, and norms drift with the times. And unlike a traffic light, which only tells you when to stop and go, norms carry moral weight: some violations feel like wrongs, not just mistakes.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a busy intersection with no traffic light. Cars would tangle within minutes — yet at a crosswalk, pedestrians and drivers coordinate smoothly all day long without anyone directing them. Norms work the same way: they are the invisible traffic lights of social life. They tell people when to go, when to wait, when to yield — who speaks first in a conversation, how close to stand, what counts as rude — and they keep millions of daily interactions moving without a single posted sign. You notice them most when one goes missing, just as you notice a broken traffic light only when the intersection suddenly stops working.

## Worked Example

Rosa, a sociology student, wants to see a norm in action. On her way to class she boards a half-empty elevator and deliberately faces the back wall instead of the doors, humming softly. The other rider glances at her, edges to the far corner, and gets off a floor early. Nobody scolds Rosa and no rule is posted, yet the discomfort is unmistakable: she breached a folkway, and the sanction was silent avoidance. Later she tries the same act in a crowded rush-hour elevator, where people are packed in and facing every direction anyway — and no one reacts at all. Same behavior, different reactions. Rosa writes in her notes that the norm was real in both settings; only the audience and the reading of her act changed.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Norms and laws are the same thing."** — Laws are formal norms — written rules with formal enforcement — but most norms are unwritten. Folkways like how close to stand and mores like keeping a promise are enforced by reactions and relationships, not by institutions.
- **"Every norm violation gets the same reaction."** — Reactions depend on the setting and the audience. A loud phone call in a hushed library draws glares; the same call in a packed stadium draws nothing. The act is identical — only the context differs.
- **"Norms are fixed and universal."** — Norms vary across groups and change over time: folkways can harden into mores as a group's views shift, and small variations in how people observe norms allow change to accumulate.
- **"If no one punishes me, the norm does not exist."** — Sanctions are often subtle — a frown, a pause, someone edging away. Breaching experiments show that norms exist precisely because breaking them produces discomfort, even when no one says a word.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Folkway vs. more:** Both are norms, but a folkway is an everyday custom without moral weight (escalator side, hallway greetings) and draws only mild disapproval when breached, while a more carries a group's moral views (keeping a serious promise) and draws shame or strong punishment. The distinction is attributed to William Graham Sumner (1906).
- **Norm vs. law:** Every law is a formal norm — written, established, and formally enforced — but most norms are never written down. The unwritten expectation to face the elevator doors is a norm but not a law; the traffic code is both.
- **Norm vs. value:** Values are the ideals and principles a group holds in high regard; norms are the rules of behavior that express those ideals in everyday conduct. A group may value honesty (a value) and expect its members to own up to mistakes (a norm).

## Key Takeaway

Norms are the shared expectations about how people should behave — learned, enforced, and often internalized rules that range from everyday folkways to morally charged mores and written laws. They keep social life moving, and they change over time.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In sociology, what is a norm?**
   - A. A shared expectation about how people should behave in a given situation
   - B. A written rule enacted and enforced by a government
   - C. A personal preference that varies from person to person
   - D. A statistical average of what most people happen to do

   **Answer: A.** A norm is a shared expectation about how people should behave — the visible and invisible rules of conduct that structure social life (OpenStax 3.2). Option B describes a law, which is one kind of norm (a formal norm), not the general definition; C describes a personal preference, which is individual rather than shared; and D confuses norms with statistical averages, since norms are expectations, not measurements.

2. **Dario rides a crowded escalator standing on the left, blocking the stream of people who want to pass. Riders frown and some mutter, but nobody treats him as immoral. How would a sociologist classify the escalator rule Dario broke?**
   - A. A more, because violations bring shame and strong disapproval
   - B. A folkway, because it is a day-to-day custom whose breach draws only mild disapproval
   - C. A law, because the rule is enforced by the reactions of others
   - D. A value, because it reflects what the group holds in high regard

   **Answer: B.** The escalator rule is a folkway: an everyday custom with no moral weight, breached with only mild disapproval — frowns and muttering — rather than shame or punishment (OpenStax 3.2; Britannica). Option A mislabels it as a more, since the reactions carry no moral judgment; C confuses informal reactions with formal law, which is written and enforced by institutions; and D mistakes a value — an ideal the group holds in high regard — for a rule of behavior.

3. **On a deserted midnight street, with no cars and no witnesses, Lena still waits for the walk signal before crossing — crossing against it would simply feel wrong. Which process does this best illustrate?**
   - A. Enforcement, because the signal is a formal rule with penalties
   - B. Learning, because she was taught the rule as a child
   - C. Internalization, because the norm now operates as her own sense of right and wrong, with nobody watching
   - D. Sanctioning, because she is rewarding herself for obedience

   **Answer: C.** Lena follows the norm with no external pressure, which is what internalization means: the expectation has become part of her own judgment, so it operates automatically (OpenStax 3.2, on norms people follow without thinking). Option A is wrong because no enforcement is occurring — no authority, no penalty, no audience; B may describe how she acquired the norm but not why she follows it now with no one watching; and D misuses the term sanction, which refers to external rewards or punishments, not private feelings.

4. **Which contrast best illustrates the honest note that norms change over time?**
   - A. A commuter stands on the right side of the escalator so others can pass on the left
   - B. A driver buckles up because the car chimes until the belt clicks
   - C. A student waits her turn in the cafeteria line
   - D. A grandparent recalls riding loose in the back seat as a child, while today the same family straps a toddler into a car seat without a second thought

   **Answer: D.** Option D shows two generations holding different expectations about the same act — child passenger safety — which is exactly what it means for a norm to change over time; folkways can harden into mores as a group's views shift (Britannica; OpenStax 3.2). Options A and C describe steady folkways with no change, and B describes enforcement of an existing norm — the car's chime — rather than a change in the norm itself.

5. **Two friends take phone calls at full volume — one inside a hushed library, one in a packed stadium. The library call draws glares and shushing; the stadium call draws no reaction at all. What does this contrast best show?**
   - A. That the library call broke a more while the stadium call broke only a folkway
   - B. That libraries enforce their norms through formal laws
   - C. That reactions to norm violations depend on the setting and the audience
   - D. That norms do not exist in crowded stadiums

   **Answer: C.** The act is identical; only the setting and audience differ, and the reactions differ with them — the honest note that reactions to norm violations are contextual (OpenStax 3.2, on breaching experiments and how reactions reveal norms). Option A mislabels the library call: a loud phone call breaches an everyday norm, not a morally charged more. B confuses informal reactions — glares and shushing — with formal law. D is false: stadiums have plenty of norms; this particular act simply does not violate them there.
