# Social Stratification

## In 30 Seconds

Social stratification is the ranking of people into layers of society, with each layer holding a different share of resources. Sociologists name three systems — slavery, caste, and class — and sort them as closed, with little room to move, or open, where movement is allowed. Two classic theories disagree about the layers: the Davis-Moore thesis says unequal rewards are necessary, while Marx's conflict view says layers protect the powerful. People do move between layers, but scholars are blunt: mobility is rarer than the stories suggest.

## Why This Matters

Stratification is the skeleton of inequality — the structure that decides who gets what. Learn to see it and you can read almost any social pattern: why some blocks have parks and others have parking lots, why some jobs carry prestige, why mobility is possible but uneven. The concept also connects the rest of the discipline, because class, poverty, race, and gender are all layers of the same structure. Naming the skeleton is not accepting it. It is the first step to seeing inequality clearly.

## Learning Objectives

- Define social stratification using the attributed working definition and explain what is meant by layers of society.
- Name the three systems of stratification — slavery, caste, and class — with one factual, attributed line about each.
- Distinguish closed from open systems of stratification, with original examples of each.
- Contrast the Davis-Moore functionalist view with Marx's conflict view of stratification, one attributed line each.
- Define social mobility with an original example and state the honest finding that mobility varies and is not as easy as many people believe.
- Explain intersectionality as the overlap of layers, attributed to Kimberlé Crenshaw, and restate the honest framing of stratification as the skeleton of inequality.

## The College Version

### Layers: the working definition

Sociologists use the term social stratification for the system of social standing in a society. OpenStax's Introduction to Sociology 3e presents the working definition: social stratification refers to a society's categorization of its people into rankings based on factors like wealth, income, education, family background, and power. The word borrows from geology, where stratification describes the distinct vertical layers found in rock. Society's layers are made of people, and they represent the uneven distribution of society's resources: the people with more resources sit at the top, and groups with fewer and fewer resources form the lower layers. An individual's place in this arrangement is called socioeconomic status, often shortened to SES. Original example: in the fictional city of Meridian Falls, a bank president, a nurse, a warehouse worker, and a high school student who delivers groceries live in the same town at different layers — different neighborhoods, different schools, different access to the town's resources. The layers themselves are the stratification. (The finer class categories are a sibling topic of their own; this lesson stays with the structure.)

### Three systems: slavery, caste, and class

Sociologists sort systems of stratification into two kinds before naming them. Closed systems accommodate little change in social position: they do not allow people to shift levels, and they do not permit social relationships between levels. Closed systems include estate, slavery, and caste systems. Open systems are based on achievement and allow movement and interaction between layers and classes. The three systems most often named are slavery, caste, and class. Slavery, historically, is a condition in which one human being was owned by another; Britannica notes that a slave was considered by law as property, or chattel, and was deprived of most of the rights ordinarily held by free persons. A caste system is a closed system in which people can do little or nothing to change the social standing of their birth — it determines occupations, marriage partners, and housing. A class system is based on both social factors and individual achievement: a class is a set of people who share similar status based on wealth, income, education, family background, and occupation, and unlike caste systems, class systems are open. Original contrast: in the fictional kingdom of Aldermere, birth decides everything — the daughter of a baker is a baker, and a gifted child of a laborer can never train as a scholar. That is a closed system. In the same writer's other story, Lena's parents packed boxes in a warehouse, and Lena studied engineering and now designs the warehouses. That is an open system — movement is allowed, even when it is not guaranteed.

### Two theories: Davis-Moore and Marx

Sociologists disagree about why the layers exist, and two classic positions face each other across the debate. The functionalist answer is the Davis-Moore thesis, published in 1945 by Kingsley Davis and Wilbert Moore: the greater the functional importance of a social role, the greater must be the reward. Stratification, on this view, represents the inherently unequal value of different work — harder, more important jobs carry bigger rewards to attract qualified people. The conflict answer draws on Karl Marx: conflict theorists are deeply critical of stratification, asserting that it benefits only some people, not all of society. Marx saw stratification as growing out of people's relationship to production — owners (the bourgeoisie) and workers (the proletariat) — with the resulting inequality creating class conflict. Each view gets one line and a century of argument behind it; this lesson stays at that level. Both theories are attributed, and both are still debated.

### Mobility, overlap, and the honest picture

Social mobility is the ability of individuals to change positions within a social stratification system — moving up, moving down, or holding still across a lifetime or across generations. Original example: Priya's grandmother washed dishes in a school cafeteria; Priya's mother managed the cafeteria's accounts; Priya teaches at the university. Each generation sits in a different layer — that is mobility across generations. The honest finding comes from the scholars who study it: mobility is not as common or easy to achieve as many people think. It happens; it is just rarer and harder than the popular stories suggest. And the layers do not sit in neat, separate stacks. Kimberlé Crenshaw coined the term intersectionality in 1989 to describe how dimensions of identity — race, gender, class, and others — intertwine and overlap, so a person can stand at the meeting point of several layers at once. Original example: a fictional first-generation college student who also uses a wheelchair faces a combination of barriers that neither her family background alone nor her disability alone would predict. That is the honest framing in one sentence: stratification is the skeleton of inequality — the underlying structure that shapes who gets what, everywhere, all the time.

## Key Vocabulary

- **social stratification** — A society's categorization of its people into rankings based on factors like wealth, income, education, family background, and power.
- **socioeconomic status (SES)** — An individual's place within the layers of stratification, combining factors like income, education, and occupation.
- **closed system** — A system of stratification that accommodates little change in social position and does not permit social relationships between levels, such as estate, slavery, and caste systems.
- **open system** — A system of stratification based on achievement that allows movement and interaction between layers and classes.
- **caste system** — A closed system of stratification in which people can do little or nothing to change the social standing of their birth.
- **class system** — A system of stratification based on both social factors and individual achievement, in which movement between levels is possible.
- **social mobility** — The ability of individuals to change positions within a social stratification system, upward or downward.
- **structural mobility** — Movement up or down the social ladder that happens when societal changes move a whole group of people at once.
- **intersectionality** — Kimberlé Crenshaw's term for how dimensions of identity intertwine and overlap, placing a person at the meeting point of several social layers.

## Eli-10

Picture society as a tall apartment building with many floors. The top floors have sunlight, elevators, and room to spare; the lower floors are cramped and dark. Social stratification is the name for the building itself — the arrangement that decides who lives on which floor and what each floor gets. Some buildings have locked stairwells: you are born on a floor and you stay there. That is a closed system like a caste. In other buildings the stairs work, and people climb up or slide down over their lives. That is an open system like a class system. Two theories argue about why the building exists: one says the floors are fair because hard jobs deserve better views; the other says the floors were built by the people on top to keep themselves on top. People do move between floors — but the honest truth is that most people end up near the floor they started on.

## Eli's Analogy

The layers of stratification are like the strata in a cliff face: distinct bands of rock stacked on top of each other, each layer a different color and texture. The cliff's bands were laid down by slow forces over a long time and they hold the cliff's shape together — just as society's layers were built up by history, hold their shape, and look different depending on where you stand.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The cliff analogy fails where people are not rock. Rock never climbs from one band to another; people do — that is mobility, rare but real. Rock bands do not notice their place; people feel theirs, and they can organize to reshape the cliff. And a cliff's layers were made by nature, while society's layers were made by people — which means they can be unmade by people too.

## Worked Example

Take the fictional Osei family across three generations. In the 1960s, Elias Osei worked as a porter at a train station — a low layer, with little education and little pay. His daughter Rosa finished high school and became a dental assistant, a step up. Rosa's son Marcus earned a degree in mechanical engineering and now manages a plant. The family climbed the building floor by floor — intergenerational mobility, movement between layers across generations. The honest footnote: Marcus is the success story, and scholars find such climbs are possible but not typical. The same family also shows the overlap of layers — Marcus's sister Maya, a first-generation college graduate who is also a wheelchair user, navigates barriers that neither layer alone explains. That is intersectionality at work in one family.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Social stratification just means being rich or poor."** — It is the structure, not the score: the ranking of whole layers of people and the uneven distribution of resources between those layers, apart from any single person's bank balance.
- **"Closed and open systems differ only in how much money people have."** — They differ in how position is assigned. Closed systems fix position by birth and block movement, and even relationships, between levels; open systems assign position partly by achievement and permit movement.
- **"The Davis-Moore thesis and Marx's theory say basically the same thing."** — They face each other. Davis and Moore said unequal rewards are necessary because important work must attract qualified people; conflict theorists following Marx said stratification benefits only some people and grows out of the relationship to production.
- **"If mobility exists at all, then hard work always pays off."** — Mobility exists, and scholars are explicit that it is not as common or easy to achieve as many people think. Upward moves happen; they are not guaranteed by effort alone.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Closed system vs. Open system:** Closed systems fix social position at birth and allow little change or movement between levels; open systems are based on achievement and allow movement and interaction between layers. A caste fixes position; a class system permits movement.
- **Davis-Moore thesis vs. Marx's conflict view:** Davis and Moore (1945) saw stratification as a necessary reward system for functionally important work; conflict theorists following Marx saw it as a structure that benefits only some people and grows out of the relationship to production.
- **Social stratification vs. Social class:** Stratification is the whole structure — the ranking of people into layers with unequal resources. Social class is one of the systems within that structure, and it gets its own full treatment in a sibling lesson.

## Key Takeaway

Social stratification is the ranking of people into layers with unequal access to resources; slavery, caste, and class are the named systems, closed or open, two classic theories dispute why the layers exist, mobility between them is real but rarer than believed, and the layers overlap — stratification is the skeleton of inequality.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In sociology, what does social stratification refer to?**
   - A. A society's categorization of its people into rankings based on factors like wealth, income, education, and power.
   - B. The set of laws a government passes to manage its economy.
   - C. The movement of people from rural areas into cities.
   - D. The shared customs and beliefs that hold a community together.

   **Answer: A.** Social stratification is the ranking of people into layers of society, with the layers representing the uneven distribution of resources — the OpenStax working definition. Option B describes law and economic policy, C describes urbanization, and D describes culture.

2. **Which system of stratification is closed, with people able to do little or nothing to change the social standing of their birth?**
   - A. A class system based on achievement.
   - B. A caste system that fixes occupations, marriage partners, and housing.
   - C. A meritocracy in which standing follows personal effort.
   - D. An open system that allows movement between layers.

   **Answer: B.** A caste system is a closed system: birth fixes standing, and the system determines occupations, marriage partners, and housing. Class systems and meritocracies are open in principle, and option D names openness outright.

3. **Marco's parents worked on an assembly line. Marco attended community college, became a registered nurse, and now earns more than either parent. Which term best describes his movement between layers of society?**
   - A. Structural mobility.
   - B. Intersectionality.
   - C. Intergenerational mobility.
   - D. Downward mobility.

   **Answer: C.** Intergenerational mobility is movement across generations — children ending up in a different social layer than their parents, which is exactly Marco's story. Structural mobility moves a whole group at once, intersectionality is about overlapping identities, and Marco moved up, not down.

4. **A sociologist argues that surgeons earn more than grocery clerks because the work is more functionally important and requires more training, and the bigger reward is what attracts qualified people. Which theory does this argument reflect?**
   - A. Structural mobility.
   - B. Kimberlé Crenshaw's intersectionality.
   - C. Karl Marx's conflict theory.
   - D. The Davis-Moore thesis.

   **Answer: D.** That is the Davis-Moore thesis (1945): the greater the functional importance of a role, the greater must be the reward. Conflict theory (C) would call the same gap a benefit to the powerful, and the other options are unrelated concepts.

5. **A friend claims that anyone can rise in society if they simply work hard enough. A sociologist who studies social mobility would most honestly respond that...**
   - A. Mobility is guaranteed for anyone who earns a college degree.
   - B. Downward mobility never happens in open systems.
   - C. Mobility is real, but scholars find it is not as common or easy as many people think.
   - D. Social position is fixed at birth in every society.

   **Answer: C.** The honest finding, per OpenStax's review of mobility research, is that mobility is not as common or easy to achieve as many people think — possible, not guaranteed. The other options overclaim in both directions.
