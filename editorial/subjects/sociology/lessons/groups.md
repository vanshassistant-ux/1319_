# Groups

## In 30 Seconds

A social group is two or more people who interact with some frequency and share a sense that their identity is aligned with the group. Sociologists distinguish groups from categories, which share a trait but no interaction, and aggregates, which share a place but no bond. Groups come in primary and secondary forms, every group creates an inside and an outside, and even the smallest groups — dyads and triads — behave differently. Groups are the building blocks of society.

## Why This Matters

Groups are where society becomes real. Academically, the concept is the discipline's starting point: sociology is often described as the science of groups and group interactions. Practically, naming the groups around you — the family that grounds you, the task team that employs you, the crowd that merely surrounds you — sharpens your sense of where support, pressure, and belonging come from. Forward-looking, understanding in-groups and reference groups helps you see the measuring sticks you compare yourself against and choose them deliberately. The honest value: groups glue society together, and knowing how the glue works is the first step to using it well.

## The College Version

### What makes a group a group

Sociologists use the word group more precisely than everyday speech does. OpenStax's Introduction to Sociology 3e offers the working definition this lesson follows: a group is any collection of at least two people who interact with some frequency and share a sense that their identity is somehow aligned with the group. Two elements do the work. The first is interaction — people actually engaging with one another, not merely existing near each other. The second is a shared sense of identity: members feel that the collection is part of who they are. Original example: the Riverside Striders are fourteen runners who meet every Tuesday evening, train together, and call themselves a club. They interact regularly, and their identity is bound up in the group — a textbook case. Not every gathering earns the label, which is why sociologists name two near-misses.

### Not groups: categories and aggregates

People who share similar characteristics but are not tied to one another form a category. Original example: everyone born in 2003 shares a birth year, but that shared trait alone does not make them a group — most of them have never met and do not interact. Categories can produce groups: first-generation college students are a category until a campus group of them starts meeting weekly, at which point the category has given birth to a group. An aggregate is people who happen to be in the same place at the same time without interacting or sharing an identity. Original example: the crowd waiting at the 6th Street bus stop during the morning rush is an aggregate — same place, no bond. Aggregates can also become groups, as neighbors who meet at a shelter during a flood may discover when a shared experience turns co-location into connection.

### Primary and secondary groups

Sociologist Charles Horton Cooley (1864–1929) proposed that groups divide broadly into two kinds (Cooley 1909). Primary groups are usually small, made up of people who engage face-to-face in long-term emotional ways, and they serve expressive functions — meeting emotional needs rather than practical ones. The classic example is the family. Original example: the Osei household — three generations at Sunday dinner, calling each other with news during the week, showing up when someone is sick — is a primary group. Secondary groups are often larger and impersonal, task-focused and time-limited, serving instrumental functions: they exist to get something done. Original example: a six-person audit team assembled for a three-month project is a secondary group. The boundary is not rigid. Coworkers who start as a secondary group can, over years of shared work and common interests, become a primary group — and the same people can hold both kinds of ties at once.

### In-groups, out-groups, and the size of groups

Sociologist William Sumner (1840–1910) developed the concepts of in-group and out-group (Sumner 1906). An in-group is the group a person feels she belongs to and sees as an integral part of who she is. An out-group is a group she does not belong to — one she may view with competition or disdain. Original example: for the members of the Meridian High debate team, the team is an in-group; the robotics club at the rival school is an out-group. Size also changes the game. Georg Simmel (1858–1915) distinguished the dyad, a two-member group, from the triad, a three-member group (Simmel 1902). A dyad ends if one member withdraws — two business partners, one retires, the partnership dissolves. A triad can survive one departure, and with three people, two can align against the third.

### Reference groups and the honest framing

A reference group is a group people compare themselves to — it provides a standard of measurement. Peer groups are common reference groups: people watch what their peers wear, listen to, and do, and measure themselves against it. Original example: a first-year nursing student measures herself against the senior nursing students she admires — a reference group she is not yet part of. Here is the honest note. Groups are the social glue: nearly everything we do happens in, through, or because of groups, and sociology is often described as the science of groups and group interactions. But the glue has two faces. The same belonging that holds an in-group together draws the line that marks an out-group. Groups support us and press on us at the same time, which is exactly why understanding them repays the effort.

## Key Vocabulary

- **social group** — A collection of at least two people who interact with some frequency and share a sense that their identity is aligned with the group.
- **aggregate** — People who happen to be in the same place at the same time without interacting or sharing a sense of identity.
- **category** — People who share a similar characteristic, such as a birth year, but are not tied to one another by interaction.
- **primary group** — A small, face-to-face group whose members share long-term emotional ties and that meets expressive needs; the family is the classic example.
- **secondary group** — A larger, more impersonal group organized around a task or goal, often time-limited, serving instrumental functions.
- **in-group** — The group a person feels she belongs to and sees as an integral part of who she is.
- **out-group** — The group a person does not belong to, often viewed with competition or disdain.
- **reference group** — A group people compare themselves to, providing a standard of measurement for behavior and norms.
- **dyad** — A two-member group, which ceases to exist if one member withdraws.
- **triad** — A three-member group, which can continue if one member leaves and in which two members can align against the third.

## Eli-10

When sociologists call something a group, they are making a precise claim: these people interact, and they feel that the interaction is part of who they are. A waiting room full of strangers fails the test; a book club passes it. Groups also run at different temperatures. Some are warm and permanent — the people who have known you forever. Some are cool and practical — the people assembled to finish a task. And every group draws a line: inside it you belong, outside it you do not. Even two people can be a group, and a third person changes everything.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a group as a rope. A category is a pile of loose threads — people who share a trait but are not tied together. An aggregate is threads lying on the same table — sharing a place, not a bond. A group is the threads twisted into a rope: they interact and hold together, and the rope is stronger than any single thread. Primary groups are the ropes that hold you up; secondary groups are the ropes that pull a load.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The rope analogy breaks down because ropes do not choose. People choose their groups — and can be chosen or excluded by them. A rope either holds or snaps, while a group can hold you up and squeeze you at the same time, welcoming insiders while marking outsiders. Ropes also stay twisted, but groups change: loose threads can become a rope, and a strong rope can fray back into threads.

## Worked Example

Consider Nadia's Tuesday. In the morning she runs with the Riverside Striders, fourteen people who train together and call themselves a club — a group by the working definition, since they interact regularly and share an identity. At lunch she waits at a crowded bus stop, an aggregate: same place, no bond. That evening her study group of three — the same three people every week — loses one member when Maya transfers colleges; the triad survives, though two-against-one votes now feel closer. Throughout the week she quietly measures her running times against the Striders' fastest members, a reference group she is not yet part of, and at the end of the day she feels most like herself on the phone with her family, her primary group.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Any gathering of people is a group."** — A crowd at a bus stop is an aggregate, and people who merely share a trait form a category. A group requires both interaction and a shared sense of identity.
- **"A primary group is just a group you like best."** — The label describes the quality of the tie — small, face-to-face, long-term, emotional, expressive — not how fond you are. A work team you enjoy is still a secondary group while it stays task-focused and time-limited.
- **"In-groups and out-groups are fixed for life."** — Membership shifts with context. The same person is inside her debate team and outside the rival robotics club, and groups themselves change over time.
- **"A group of three works just like a group of two."** — Simmel's point is that size changes the structure: a dyad ends when one member withdraws, while a triad survives one departure and opens the door to two-against-one coalitions.
- **"A reference group is a group you belong to."** — A reference group is usually the one you measure yourself against — the senior students you admire, the athletes you follow — whether or not you are a member.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Group vs. Category and aggregate:** A group requires interaction plus a shared sense of identity; a category is people who share a trait without being tied together, and an aggregate is people who share a place without interacting.
- **Primary group vs. Secondary group:** Primary groups are small, face-to-face, long-term, and emotional (expressive); secondary groups are larger, impersonal, task-focused, and time-limited (instrumental). Cooley's distinction, taught as a contrast of tie quality rather than a rigid boundary.
- **Dyad vs. Triad:** A dyad (two members) ends if one withdraws; a triad (three members) survives one departure and allows two-against-one dynamics. Simmel's size effect.

## Key Takeaway

Groups — collections of people who interact and share an identity — are the building blocks of society: they come in primary and secondary forms, define insiders and outsiders, and change character with size, making them the social glue with two faces.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **According to the working definition in this lesson, what makes a collection of people a social group?**
   - A. They interact with some frequency and share a sense that their identity is aligned with the group.
   - B. They share a single trait, such as a birth year or a birthplace.
   - C. They happen to be in the same place at the same time.
   - D. They all hold the same opinion on a public issue.

   **Answer: A.** The working definition requires both regular interaction and a shared sense of identity. Option B describes a category, C describes an aggregate, and D describes people who merely agree — none of these, by themselves, make a group.

2. **Dozens of strangers wait for the same delayed train. They do not speak and feel no bond. Three of them later start a weekly carpool and come to think of themselves as the carpool crew. What labels fit the waiting crowd and the carpool, in that order?**
   - A. Category; group
   - B. Aggregate; group
   - C. Group; aggregate
   - D. Aggregate; category

   **Answer: B.** The waiting crowd shares a place but no interaction or identity — an aggregate. The carpoolers interact regularly and share an identity — a group. A category would require a shared trait like a birth year, not a shared location.

3. **Priya spends her days on a six-person project team assembled to finish a budget report in three months, a team she knows only professionally. Her book club, which she has attended for ten years, is where she turns when she needs support. Which pairing is correct?**
   - A. The project team is a primary group; the book club is a secondary group.
   - B. Both are primary groups because they involve other people.
   - C. Both are secondary groups because they meet regularly.
   - D. The project team is a secondary group; the book club is a primary group.

   **Answer: D.** Cooley's distinction runs on the kind of tie, not on fondness: the project team is larger, formal, task-focused, and time-limited — secondary. The book club is small, enduring, face-to-face, and emotionally supportive — primary.

4. **Members of the Meridian High debate team feel that the team is a core part of who they are, and they eye the robotics club at the rival school as a group they compete against. Which terms describe the debate team and the rival robotics club, in that order?**
   - A. Out-group; in-group
   - B. Category; aggregate
   - C. In-group; out-group
   - D. Primary group; secondary group

   **Answer: C.** The debate team is the group members feel they belong to and identify with — an in-group. The rival club is a group they do not belong to and compete against — an out-group. The category/aggregate and primary/secondary labels answer different questions about these same people.

5. **Two bakery owners run a business together; when one retires, the business dissolves. Three roommates share an apartment; when one moves out, the remaining two keep the apartment and often find themselves voting two against one. What do these two situations illustrate?**
   - A. Simmel's point that a dyad ends if one member withdraws, while a triad can continue and develop coalitions.
   - B. The difference between a primary group and a secondary group.
   - C. The difference between a category and an aggregate.
   - D. Cooley's claim that families are the best example of a primary group.

   **Answer: A.** The two-person partnership is a dyad: one withdrawal ends it. The three-person household is a triad: it survives one departure and allows two-against-one dynamics. The other options describe tie quality, traits or co-location, or Cooley's family example — none of which explain the size effect at work here.
