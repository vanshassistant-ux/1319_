# Equity

## In 30 Seconds

Equity is the owners' stake in a business: the value that remains after subtracting what the business owes from what it owns. It is the balancing piece of the accounting equation — assets minus liabilities. Owner investments and profits increase equity; withdrawals and losses decrease it. On the balance sheet, corporate equity splits into contributed capital, the money owners put in, and retained earnings, the profit the business kept. Because equity is the remainder, it absorbs the risk: when value falls, the owners' claim shrinks first.

## Why This Matters

Equity is where ownership shows up in the numbers. When you read a balance sheet, equity tells you how much of the business actually belongs to its owners after everyone else is accounted for — the difference between owning your shop outright and owing most of it to a bank. Entrepreneurs use it to track their growing stake, lenders use it to judge how much cushion a business has, and investors use it to understand what they are buying: a share of stock is a share of equity. It also keeps expectations honest. Because equity is what remains after liabilities, it is the claim that shrinks first when things go wrong. Understanding equity means understanding who really carries a business's risk.

## The College Version

### What equity is: the owners' claim

Equity is the owners' stake in the business — the claim the owners hold on the business's assets. The working definition used in this lesson comes from two sources. OpenStax's Principles of Accounting puts it simply: equity is the value of an item that remains after considering what is owed for that item. CFI states the same idea from the owners' side: equity is the value attributable to the owners of a business. Put the two together and you get the idea accountants use every day — equity is what is left of the business's assets after the business's liabilities are subtracted. It is a measured claim, not a pile of cash. The owners cannot point to a drawer of equity money; they can only point to assets and to the calculation that shows how much of those assets belongs to them. That is why OpenStax also calls equity net worth: the value of the organization.

### Equity in the equation: the remainder

The accounting equation lines up the three categories: assets = liabilities + equity. Every asset the business owns was funded one of two ways — with borrowed money, which creates a liability, or with the owners' money and reinvested profit, which creates equity. OpenStax describes this as a sources-and-claims view: every asset has a claim against it, by creditors and owners. Because the equation always balances, equity is the remainder: equity = assets − liabilities. Original example: Mara's Bike Repair owns a van, tools, and a small stock of parts worth $30,000 in total, and owes $9,000 on the van loan and to suppliers. The owners' equity is $30,000 − $9,000 = $21,000. If the shop later pays off $3,000 of the loan, liabilities drop to $6,000 and equity rises to $24,000 — same assets, bigger owners' stake, because less of what the business owns is spoken for by outsiders.

### What changes equity

Equity moves for four reasons, and each has a name. Owner investment increases it: when Mara puts another $2,000 of her own money into the shop, her stake grows. Profits increase it: when the shop earns $1,500 more in a month than it spends, that net income belongs to the owners, so equity grows by $1,500. Withdrawals decrease it: when Mara takes $500 out of the till for personal use, her stake shrinks. Losses decrease it: when expenses exceed revenue, the loss eats into equity. OpenStax lists the same four movers on the statement of owner's equity — investments by owners and net income increase the value of the organization, while distributions to owners and net loss decrease it. The income statement's profit or loss feeds straight into equity, which is one reason the financial statements interrelate.

### Owner claims versus outsider claims

Equity and liabilities are both claims on the business's assets, but they come from different directions. Liabilities are outsider claims: the bank that lent the money, the supplier waiting to be paid. Those claims are fixed amounts the business is obligated to pay. Equity is the owners' claim: what is left after the outsiders' claims are satisfied. That makes equity residual — it rides on top of liabilities, not beside them. The distinction matters when a business struggles. If the value of the assets falls, the owners' stake absorbs the loss first; the bank's claim stays intact until the business actually fails to repay. This lesson focuses on the owners' side; the liabilities topic in this subject covers the outsider side in depth.

### The parts, the forms, and the honest framing

Corporate equity has two broad parts. Contributed capital is the money owners put into the business in exchange for ownership — in a corporation it appears as common stock. Retained earnings is the profit the business earned and kept over time instead of paying it out as dividends. The same concept carries different labels in different business forms: a sole proprietor's stake is called owner's capital, partners hold partner capital accounts, and shareholders hold stockholders' equity made up of common stock and retained earnings. Whatever the label, the honest framing is the same: equity is the balancing piece. It is what makes the equation balance, and because it is the remainder, it absorbs the risk of the business. When a business gains, the owners' claim grows first; when it loses, their claim shrinks first. In the numbers, ownership is the claim that stands last in line.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Equity:** The owners' claim on a business: the value that remains after subtracting what the business owes from what it owns.
- **Assets:** Everything of value a business owns or controls, such as cash, equipment, and inventory.
- **Liabilities:** Amounts a business owes to outsiders, such as bank loans and unpaid supplier bills.
- **Contributed capital:** Money or other value owners put into a business in exchange for ownership; in a corporation it appears as common stock.
- **Retained earnings:** Profit a business has earned and kept over time instead of paying it out to owners; cumulative net income minus dividends.
- **Withdrawals:** Cash or other assets owners take out of a business for personal use; withdrawals reduce equity.
- **Net income:** Profit — the amount left when a business's expenses are subtracted from its revenue; net income increases equity.
- **Stockholders' equity:** The name for equity in a corporation, made up of contributed capital such as common stock, plus retained earnings.

## Eli-10

Equity is the part of a business that belongs to the people who own it. It is not a cash pile — it is a measured claim: take everything the business owns, subtract everything it owes, and what is left is the owners' stake. The claim moves on its own. Put more money into the business and the stake grows. Earn a profit and the stake grows. Take money out for yourself, or lose money, and the stake shrinks. That is why equity is the honest part of the balance sheet: it is the number that quietly absorbs every gain and every loss before anyone else's claim changes.

## Eli's Analogy

A pizza costs $20. A friend lends you $12 to buy it, and you pay the other $8 yourself. The whole pizza is an asset, the $12 you owe is a liability, and your $8 share of ownership — what is left over — is your equity. Now imagine the pizza's value drops to $16. The debt stays $12, so your share shrinks to $4. The friend who lent you the money does not lose a cent; you absorb the whole loss. That is what it means for equity to carry the risk.

The analogy has limits. A pizza's size does not change by itself, but a business's value changes constantly with profits and losses, and owners can add money in or take money out. Equity is also a bookkeeping claim, not a spending fund: the owners cannot spend their equity directly — they can only take cash out of the business, which is exactly what reduces equity. And for a large corporation with thousands of shareholders, the owner is not one person holding a pizza slice; it is a crowd, each holding a tiny piece of the same residual claim.

## Worked Example

Mara starts Mara's Bike Repair with $15,000 of her own money, so the shop's assets are $15,000 and equity is $15,000. The bank lends her $9,000 for a used work van: assets rise to $24,000 and liabilities to $9,000, while equity stays $15,000. She buys $21,000 of equipment and parts, leaving $3,000 in cash. Check the equation: assets of $24,000 = liabilities of $9,000 + equity of $15,000. Over the year the shop earns $6,000 of net income, so equity climbs to $21,000. Mara then withdraws $2,000 for personal use, dropping equity to $19,000. At year-end the equation still balances: assets of $28,000 = liabilities of $9,000 + equity of $19,000. Every change ran through one of the four movers — investment, profit, withdrawal — and the books stayed in balance.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating equity as the cash the owner can spend.** Equity is a measured claim — assets minus liabilities — not a bank balance. Owners can only get cash out as withdrawals, and every withdrawal reduces equity.
- **Thinking a bank loan increases the owner's stake.** A loan adds the same amount to assets and liabilities, so equity is unchanged. Equity grows only through owner investments and profits, and shrinks through withdrawals and losses.
- **Using one set of labels for every business form.** A sole proprietor has owner's capital and withdrawals, partners have capital accounts, and corporations have stockholders' equity — common stock, retained earnings, and dividends.
- **Confusing equity with the value of the whole business.** Equity is only the owners' residual claim. A business with $100,000 of assets and $70,000 owed has $30,000 of equity, not $100,000.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Equity vs. Liabilities:** Both are claims on the business's assets, but liabilities are fixed claims owed to outsiders, while equity is the owners' residual claim — what remains after the liabilities.
- **Contributed capital vs. Retained earnings:** Contributed capital is the money owners put in; retained earnings is profit the business earned and kept rather than paid out.
- **Owner's equity (sole proprietorship) vs. Stockholders' equity (corporation):** The same concept wears different labels: a sole owner's stake is tracked as capital and withdrawals, while a corporation's owners hold common stock and its equity also includes retained earnings.

## Key Takeaway

Equity is the owners' claim on a business — assets minus liabilities — the balancing piece that grows with investment and profit, shrinks with withdrawals and losses, and absorbs the risk of the business.

## Practice Question Bank

**Question 1.** How do accountants measure the owners' claim — equity — in a business?

- A. As the value left after subtracting what the business owes from what it owns
- B. As the total value of everything the business owns
- C. As the amount the business owes to banks and suppliers
- D. As the cash in the register at the end of the year

**Answer: A.** Equity is measured as the remainder: assets minus liabilities. OpenStax defines equity as the value that remains after considering what is owed, and CFI states equity equals assets minus liabilities; the other options describe assets, liabilities, and cash, not the owners' claim.

**Question 2.** Which event increases the equity of a business?

- A. The owner withdraws cash for personal use
- B. The business takes out a bank loan
- C. The business earns a profit
- D. A customer pays an existing bill

**Answer: C.** Net income increases the value of the organization and therefore its equity, per OpenStax. A withdrawal decreases equity, a loan adds equally to assets and liabilities, and a customer paying a bill swaps one asset for another — none of those changes equity.

**Question 3.** Delgado Catering has assets of $90,000 and liabilities of $38,000. What is the owners' equity?

- A. $128,000
- B. $52,000
- C. $38,000
- D. $90,000

**Answer: B.** Equity is assets minus liabilities: $90,000 − $38,000 = $52,000. The $128,000 option adds the two instead of subtracting; the other options are the liability and asset totals on their own.

**Question 4.** During a slow season, a food truck's equipment loses resale value, cutting total assets by $4,000 while liabilities stay the same. What happens to the owners' equity?

- A. It increases by $4,000
- B. It stays the same
- C. It is unchanged until the equipment is sold
- D. It decreases by $4,000

**Answer: D.** Because equity is the remainder after liabilities, a $4,000 fall in assets with liabilities unchanged reduces equity by $4,000. This is the risk-absorbing property of equity: asset losses hit the owners' residual claim first.

**Question 5.** On a corporation's balance sheet, stockholders' equity is built from which two broad parts?

- A. Contributed capital and retained earnings
- B. Revenue and expenses
- C. Cash and inventory
- D. Accounts payable and notes payable

**Answer: A.** CFI states that share capital plus retained earnings equals equity, and OpenStax's expanded equation treats contributed capital and retained earnings as the elements of equity. Revenue and expenses are income statement items, cash and inventory are assets, and payables are liabilities.
