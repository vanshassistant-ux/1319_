# Income Statement

## In 30 Seconds

The income statement is the scoreboard for a period. It reports a business's revenue at the top, its expenses below, and the profit or loss at the bottom — this lesson's working definition follows OpenStax's Principles of Accounting. The math is one subtraction: revenue minus expenses. Gross figures come before other items are subtracted; net figures come after. The statement shows earnings, not cash movements, and it answers one question: did the business make money this period?

## Why This Matters

Every money decision about a business starts with its income statement. Owners read it to see whether their work made money and how much they can take out or reinvest. Lenders read it to decide whether the business can repay a loan. Investors read it to judge whether profits are growing before they buy or hold shares. It is also the easiest statement to misunderstand: a profitable business can still run short of cash, and a big revenue number can hide a loss. Learn to read the scoreboard — what the lines mean, what the bottom line is, and what it does not tell you — and you can judge any business's performance for yourself.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the income statement using the working definition in this lesson, attributed to OpenStax's Principles of Accounting: a statement that shows the organization's financial performance — its revenues and expenses — for a given period of time, ending in profit or loss.

- Name who reads the income statement — owners, lenders, and investors — what each looks for, and apply the honest framing: the statement answers one question, did the business make money this period?

## The College Version

### What an income statement is: the scoreboard for a period

This lesson's working definition follows OpenStax's Principles of Accounting: the income statement is a statement that shows the organization's financial performance for a given period of time. CFI describes the same document as one of a company's core financial statements, showing profit and loss over a period of time and summarizing all revenues and expenses from operating and non-operating activities. The period matters: it can be a month, a quarter, or a year, and the statement's heading is labeled with the dates it covers — "for the month ended July 31," not "as of July 31." The scoreboard analogy fits: the statement keeps score for a stretch of play, not for a single moment. A statement that shows a snapshot at one moment — what a business owns and owes — is the balance sheet, a sibling topic referenced here only.

### The shape: revenue at the top, expenses below, profit or loss at the bottom

The statement has a fixed shape, and you can describe it in words without a spreadsheet. Revenue sits at the very top — CFI notes that sales revenue is displayed at the very top of the statement, and it is the first line anyone reads. Below it come the expenses: the direct cost of what was sold first, then operating expenses such as wages and rent, and further down items such as interest and taxes. The bottom line is the profit or loss — net income when revenues and gains are greater than expenses and losses, net loss when the reverse is true, as OpenStax states it. Readers work top to bottom: the top shows how much business happened, the middle shows what it cost, and the bottom shows what was left. That bottom line is the number the whole statement is built to produce.

### The simple arithmetic: revenue minus expenses

The whole statement rests on one subtraction: revenue minus expenses equals net income or net loss. OpenStax writes the income statement as the formula Revenue − Expenses = Net Income/(Loss). Take a small bakery's month. Meadow & Main Bakery sells 2,400 loaves at $3.50 in July, earning $8,400 in revenue. Its expenses for the month — flour and other ingredients, wages for two bakers, rent, utilities, and delivery fuel — come to $6,150. Revenue minus expenses: $8,400 − $6,150 = $2,250 of net income. If expenses had instead reached $9,100, the same subtraction gives $8,400 − $9,100 = −$700, a net loss of $700. One subtraction, two possible endings: profit or loss.

### Gross vs net: before and after

The words gross and net mark where you are in the statement. A gross figure comes before other items are subtracted; a net figure comes after everything is subtracted. CFI's example is gross profit: it is calculated by subtracting the cost of goods sold from sales revenue — for a bakery, the cost of the ingredients that went into the bread — before any rent, wages, or utilities are taken out. Net income, by contrast, is the figure after every expense on the statement has been deducted. For Meadow & Main, the bread line's gross profit is $8,400 − $1,900 = $6,500: the bread money minus what the bread itself cost. The month's net income, $2,250, is what remains after the other $4,250 of expenses come out too. Gross is the earlier, bigger number; net is the final one.

### Profit vs cash: earnings are not banknotes

Here is the honest note of this lesson: the income statement shows earnings, not cash movements. A profitable month does not guarantee a fat bank account, and a loss does not mean the business is out of cash. OpenStax's own opening example makes the point: a landscaping business earned $1,400 and spent $1,150, a $250 gain on paper, while its checking account told a slightly different story depending on when bills were paid. Customers who pay later, purchases made before the month, and loan repayments all move cash without showing up as this month's revenue or expense. The statement that tracks cash movements — operating, investing, and financing — is the statement of cash flows, a sibling topic referenced here only.

### Who reads it: owners, lenders, and investors

Different readers come to the income statement with different questions. Owners read it to see whether the business made money and what they can take out or leave in; OpenStax notes that owners use the financial statements to make decisions about their businesses. Lenders read it to judge whether the business earns more than it spends and can repay what it borrows — OpenStax gives the example of a banker who studies the statements to decide whether to lend. Investors read it to watch whether profits are growing, which is why CFI calls the statement vital to investors and company management. Each reader lands on the same bottom line, but each reads it for a different decision.

### The honest framing: one question

The honest framing of this lesson: the income statement answers one question — did the business make money this period? It does not answer whether the business is healthy overall, whether it can pay its bills next month, or whether it is a good investment; those questions belong to the balance sheet, the statement of cash flows, and ratio analysis, each a sibling topic. A single loss is not a verdict on a business, any more than one winning month proves it is safe. The statement is a scoreboard for the period it covers: read the dates in the heading, read the subtraction, and you know what the score was.

## Key Vocabulary

- **income statement** — A financial statement that shows an organization's financial performance for a given period of time, reporting revenue, expenses, and the resulting profit or loss; also called the profit and loss statement.
- **period** — The stretch of time an income statement covers, such as a month, a quarter, or a year, stated in the heading of the statement.
- **revenue** — The value of the goods and services a business provides to its customers, displayed at the top of the income statement.
- **expenses** — The costs a business incurs to provide goods and services, listed below revenue on the income statement and subtracted from it.
- **net income** — The profit for the period, which occurs when revenues and gains are greater than expenses and losses.
- **net loss** — The loss for the period, which occurs when expenses and losses are greater than revenues and gains.
- **gross profit** — Revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold, computed before other expenses such as rent and wages are subtracted.
- **bottom line** — The nickname for the final figure on the income statement, the net income or net loss, because it sits at the bottom.
- **statement of cash flows** — The sibling statement that reports a business's cash movements from operating, investing, and financing activities; referenced in this lesson only.

## Eli-10

An income statement is the scoreboard for a stretch of business time — a month, a quarter, or a year. It lists what the business earned at the top, what it spent below that, and the profit or loss at the very bottom. The math is one subtraction: earnings minus spending. Gross figures come before other costs are subtracted; net figures come after everything is subtracted. And the scoreboard shows the score, not the cash: a business can show a profit and still be short on cash, because customers may pay later and bills may be due sooner. The income statement answers one question: did the business make money this period?

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a basketball scoreboard at the end of the quarter. The board shows points scored at the top and the final score at the bottom — the whole game's result on one board. An income statement is that scoreboard for a business's period. The revenue line is the points scored; the expense lines are the points given up; the bottom line is the final score. Fans glance at the final score first, and so do owners, lenders, and investors.

The analogy has limits. A game ends with a final whistle, but a business plays on, period after period, and no single quarter's score is the whole season. The scoreboard also shows points, not the team's bank account: sales made on credit count as revenue before the cash arrives, and the scoreboard says nothing about whether the team can pay its next payroll. Cash movements have their own statement, the statement of cash flows.

## Worked Example

Meadow & Main Bakery closes its books for July. The month's revenue: 2,400 loaves sold at $3.50 each, $8,400. The month's expenses: ingredients, $1,900; wages for two bakers, $2,400; rent, $1,200; utilities, $450; delivery fuel, $200 — $6,150 in total. The income statement runs top to bottom: revenue $8,400, expenses $6,150, and the bottom line, $2,250 of net income, because $8,400 − $6,150 = $2,250. The bread line alone shows a gross profit of $6,500 — the $8,400 of sales minus the $1,900 of ingredients — before the other $4,250 of expenses are subtracted. None of this says the bakery's bank account grew by $2,250: a wholesale customer pays in August, and the rent check clears before month-end. The statement reports July's earnings; cash is another statement's job.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading the income statement as a snapshot of one moment.** The statement covers a period — a month, a quarter, or a year — and its heading says so; a point-in-time picture of what a business owns and owes is the balance sheet, a sibling topic.
- **Treating net income as cash in the bank.** Net income is earnings, not cash movements; customers who pay later and bills due sooner can make a profitable period look short on cash, which is why the statement of cash flows exists.
- **Confusing gross and net.** Gross figures come before other items are subtracted — gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold; net income comes after every expense on the statement.
- **Judging the whole business on one period's bottom line.** One month's loss is not a verdict on a business; the income statement answers only whether the business made money in the period it covers.
- **Reading the bottom line without checking the period.** A $5,000 profit means very different things over a month and over a year; read the dates in the heading first.

## Compare / Contrast

- **income statement vs. balance sheet:** The income statement covers a period — revenue, expenses, and profit or loss — while the balance sheet is a snapshot at a single moment, showing what a business owns and owes; the balance sheet is a sibling topic.
- **gross vs. net:** A gross figure comes before other items are subtracted (gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold); a net figure comes after everything is subtracted (net income is revenue minus all expenses).
- **profit vs. cash:** The income statement reports earnings for the period; the statement of cash flows reports actual cash movements, and the two can differ whenever payments and expenses fall in different periods.

## Key Takeaway

The income statement is the scoreboard for a period: revenue at the top, expenses below, and profit or loss at the bottom. It is built on one subtraction — revenue minus expenses — and it answers one question: did the business make money this period? Gross comes before, net comes after, and earnings are not the same as cash.

## Practice Question Bank

**Question 1.** A bakery's income statement for June lists its sales at the top, its costs below, and one final figure at the bottom. What does that final figure report?

- A. The bakery's cash balance on June 30
- B. The profit or loss for June
- C. The value of the bakery's ovens and counters
- D. The amount the bakery owes its suppliers

**Answer: B.** The bottom line of an income statement is the profit or loss for the period it covers — net income when revenue exceeds expenses, net loss when expenses exceed revenue; cash balances, equipment values, and debts appear on other statements.

**Question 2.** Meadow & Main Bakery earns $8,400 in revenue for July and records $6,150 in expenses. What is the month's net income?

- A. $2,250
- B. $8,400
- C. $6,150
- D. $14,550

**Answer: A.** Net income is revenue minus expenses: $8,400 minus $6,150 equals $2,250; the other choices confuse revenue, expenses, and the sum of the two with the difference.

**Question 3.** The bread line at a bakery brings in $8,400 in sales, and the ingredients for that bread cost $1,900. Which figure comes before the bakery's rent, wages, and utilities are subtracted?

- A. Net income
- B. The bottom line
- C. Gross profit
- D. The net loss

**Answer: C.** Gross profit is revenue minus the direct cost of what was sold, computed before other expenses such as rent and wages are subtracted; net income and net loss come after all expenses.

**Question 4.** A bakery reports a profit for July, yet its bank account ends the month lower than it started. Which is the most sensible reading?

- A. The income statement must be wrong
- B. The accountant forgot to record some revenue
- C. Profit always equals the increase in cash
- D. Profit measures earnings, while cash moved separately on credit sales and bill payments

**Answer: D.** The income statement shows earnings, not cash movements; customers who pay later and bills paid early can shrink the bank account in a profitable month, which is why the statement of cash flows exists.

**Question 5.** A lender is reading a bakery's income statements before deciding whether to approve a loan. Which question is she most directly answering?

- A. Does the bakery own its building?
- B. Does the bakery earn more than it spends, period after period?
- C. Who are the bakery's best customers?
- D. How much cash is in the register today?

**Answer: B.** Lenders study the income statement to judge whether a business earns more than it spends and can repay what it borrows; ownership of assets, customer names, and today's cash balance are different questions.
