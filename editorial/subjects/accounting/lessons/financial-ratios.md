# Financial Ratios

## In 30 Seconds

Financial ratios are simple divisions between two numbers on a company's financial statements. They turn large figures into comparable measurements: profit per sales dollar, assets per liability dollar, debt per equity dollar. Four families — profitability, liquidity, leverage, and efficiency — each answer one question about how the business runs. Four classics do most of the work: current ratio, profit margin, return on equity, and debt-to-equity. A single ratio says little on its own; ratios earn their meaning when compared over time and against similar companies.

## Why This Matters

Ratios convert statement numbers into the questions people actually ask about a business: Can it pay its bills? How much of each sales dollar stays as profit? How much of the company is financed by debt? Lenders use ratios to size up repayment risk before lending, investors use them to judge profitability before buying shares, and managers use them to spot weaknesses early. Because a ratio divides one number by another, it strips out size: the same measurement works for a corner bakery and a national chain, and for this year compared with last. The four classics are the shared language of that conversation.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a financial ratio as a relationship between two numbers drawn from a company's financial statements.
- Explain what each of the four ratio families — profitability, liquidity, leverage, and efficiency — shows about a business.
- Calculate the current ratio, profit margin, return on equity, and debt-to-equity from simple statement numbers.
- Analyze what a higher or lower value of each classic ratio tends to mean for a company.
- Analyze why a single ratio means little until it is compared over time and against peers.
- Distinguish what ratios can show — the past as reported — from what they cannot: a prediction of the future.

## The College Version

### What a financial ratio is: the statements turned into questions

A financial ratio is a relationship between two numbers from a company's financial statements: one figure divided by another, expressed as a single number. The working definition used across the profession is that ratios are calculations that compare two or more figures from the financial statements to measure performance and financial health. Why divide at all? Because raw numbers carry scale: a national chain earns $40 million and a neighborhood bakery earns $40 thousand, and those figures cannot be compared directly. Dividing by another statement number removes the size difference, so both businesses can be measured on the same scale. The current ratio divides current assets by current liabilities — both balance-sheet numbers — and the result is a measurement that works whether the company is tiny or enormous. Ratios pull their inputs from whichever statement holds the numbers they need: the income statement provides revenue and profit figures, the balance sheet provides asset, liability, and equity figures, and the statement of cash flows provides cash figures.

### The families: four questions, four sets of ratios

Ratios are grouped into families, and each family answers one question about the business. Profitability ratios show profit relative to sales, assets, or equity — how much of what flows in stays as earnings. Liquidity ratios show short-term payment ability — whether the company can cover the bills coming due within about a year. Leverage ratios show debt and capital structure — how much of the company is funded by borrowing rather than by owners' money. Efficiency ratios show asset use and productivity — how hard the assets work to generate sales. The families overlap in practice: a healthy company needs reasonable numbers in more than one, and analysts typically use combinations of ratios rather than any single one.

### The four classics: formulas and what the numbers tend to mean

Four ratios appear so often in real analysis that they are worth knowing by heart. The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities. It measures how well current assets can cover short-term obligations: a ratio of 2.0 means two dollars of current assets for every dollar of bills due within the year, and a higher number means more cushion. Profit margin is net income divided by revenue. It shows the share of each sales dollar left as profit after all expenses — 10% means ten cents of every sales dollar became net income — and a falling margin can signal costs rising faster than prices. Return on equity is net income divided by shareholders' equity. It measures profit relative to the owners' stake: how effectively the company turns owners' capital into earnings, where higher means the owners' money is working harder. Debt-to-equity is total debt divided by shareholders' equity. It shows the financing mix: a higher number means more debt in the mix, a lower number means the company leans more on owners' money. These tendencies are factual, not verdicts: what counts as a good level for any of them depends on the industry and on the company's own history.

### Ratios in context: time and peers

A single ratio means little alone. Analysts rarely rely on one number; they track a company's ratios over time — is this year's margin better than last year's? — and they benchmark them against peers — is this company more or less leveraged than its competitors? The same current ratio of 1.5 can look like a strength for a stable utility and a warning sign for a startup burning cash. Ratios make comparison possible precisely because they strip out size, and the comparison itself is where the meaning comes from.

### Who uses them, and the limits

Lenders and creditors use ratios to judge whether a borrower can meet its obligations. Investors use them to monitor financial performance and evaluate whether their capital is earning a decent return. Managers use them to run the business: spotting weak spots and measuring the effect of their own decisions. External and internal users apply the same numbers to different questions. The limits are real. Ratios are computed from reported figures, which describe what already happened. They summarize the past; they do not predict the future. A strong current ratio does not guarantee next year's bills get paid, and last year's good margin says nothing certain about next year's demand.

### The honest framing: a lens, not a verdict

The honest way to hold a ratio is as a lens for looking at the statements, not a verdict on the company. A lens brings one relationship into focus — profit per dollar of sales, assets per dollar of bills — while the rest of the picture stays outside the frame. Read a ratio as a question to investigate (why did this move?) rather than a sentence to accept (this company is good or bad). Combined with history, peers, and the rest of the statements, ratios are among the most useful tools in finance. Alone, they are just division.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Financial ratio** — A calculation that compares two or more figures from a company's financial statements, used to measure performance and financial health.
- **Profitability ratios** — The family of ratios that shows earnings relative to sales, assets, or owners' equity — how much of what a company earns stays as profit.
- **Liquidity ratios** — The family of ratios that shows short-term payment ability — whether a company can cover obligations due within about a year.
- **Leverage ratios** — The family of ratios that shows debt and capital structure — how much of a company's financing comes from borrowing rather than owners' money.
- **Efficiency ratios** — The family of ratios that shows asset use and productivity — how effectively a company's assets generate sales.
- **Current ratio** — A liquidity ratio computed as current assets divided by current liabilities, measuring the ability of current assets to cover short-term obligations.
- **Profit margin** — A profitability ratio computed as net income divided by revenue, showing the share of each sales dollar that ends up as earnings.
- **Return on equity** — A profitability ratio computed as net income divided by shareholders' equity, measuring how effectively owners' capital generates earnings.
- **Debt-to-equity ratio** — A leverage ratio computed as total debt divided by shareholders' equity, showing how much financing comes from debt relative to owners' money.
- **Benchmark** — A reference point used to interpret a ratio, such as the company's own past ratios or the ratios of similar companies.

## Eli-10

A financial ratio is one statement number divided by another — a way of putting companies of different sizes on the same scale. The bakery's $40,000 profit and the chain's $40 million profit are hard to compare directly, but 'ten cents of profit per dollar of sales' is a measurement both can be judged by. The four families ask four questions: Are we making money? (profitability) Can we pay the bills coming due? (liquidity) How much debt are we carrying? (leverage) Are our assets working hard? (efficiency). The four classic ratios — current ratio, profit margin, return on equity, and debt-to-equity — are the most common answers to those questions.

## Eli's Analogy

A ratio is like a blood-pressure reading. One number is a compressed summary of something important about the body. On its own, a single reading tells you little: you want your history, and you want to know how you compare with people of your age and build. A company's ratios work the same way — a compressed summary of financial health that only means something in comparison, over time and against similar companies.

The analogy has limits. The analogy breaks down in an important way. Blood pressure has standard healthy ranges that apply to nearly everyone, but ratios have no universal 'normal'. A current ratio that suits a grocery store can look wrong for a software firm, and no single ratio — or even a handful — captures the whole financial picture the way a full checkup combines many measurements.

## Worked Example

Harbor & Pine Cafe ends its year with these numbers. Income statement: revenue of $240,000 and net income of $24,000. Balance sheet: current assets of $60,000 (cash $12,000, customer receivables $8,000, inventory $40,000), current liabilities of $30,000, total liabilities of $90,000, and shareholders' equity of $60,000. Profit margin = $24,000 / $240,000 = 10%, so ten cents of every sales dollar stayed as profit. Current ratio = $60,000 / $30,000 = 2.0, so two dollars of current assets stand behind each dollar of bills due within the year. Return on equity = $24,000 / $60,000 = 40%, or forty cents of profit for every dollar of owners' capital. Debt-to-equity = $90,000 / $60,000 = 1.5, a dollar and a half of debt for every dollar of equity. Now the context step: last year's margin was 8%, so 10% is an improvement; the cafe across town runs 12%, so there is still ground to gain.

## Common Mistakes

- **Judging a company from a single year's ratio.** *Correction:* A ratio earns meaning in comparison: track it over time and against peers before drawing any conclusion.
- **Pulling the wrong statement's numbers into a ratio.** *Correction:* The current ratio uses balance-sheet figures (current assets, current liabilities); profit margin uses income-statement figures (net income, revenue). Check which statement a number comes from.
- **Reading a ratio like 2.0 as a percentage.** *Correction:* A ratio of 2.0 is a comparison — two dollars per dollar — not 2%. Percentages appear only when a ratio is multiplied by 100, as in a 10% profit margin.
- **Assuming a higher number is always better.** *Correction:* Context decides. Cash behind a very high current ratio can be sitting idle instead of working, and a high debt-to-equity can be routine in capital-heavy industries. Ask what the level means for this company in this industry.
- **Treating ratios as predictions.** *Correction:* Ratios describe the reported past. They can flag strengths and risks, but they do not forecast what will happen next.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Current ratio** vs **Debt-to-equity ratio**: Both divide balance-sheet numbers, but they answer different questions: the current ratio is about short-term bill-paying ability (liquidity), while debt-to-equity is about the long-term financing mix (leverage).
- **Profit margin** vs **Return on equity**: Both are profitability ratios with different denominators: profit margin measures profit per dollar of sales, while return on equity measures profit per dollar of owners' capital.
- **A ratio** vs **A raw statement number**: A raw number shows scale — the chain earned $40 million — while a ratio shows a relationship, such as ten cents of profit per sales dollar, which stays comparable as the business grows or across companies of different sizes.

## Key Takeaway

A financial ratio is a lens for reading the statements, not a verdict on the company: it summarizes one relationship between numbers, and it earns meaning only when compared over time and against peers.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. Which family of financial ratios shows whether a company can cover its short-term bills?**
- [✔] Liquidity
- [ ] Profitability
- [ ] Leverage
- [ ] Efficiency
  - **Answer:** option A
  - **Why:** Liquidity ratios show short-term payment ability — whether current assets can cover obligations due within about a year. Profitability concerns earnings, leverage concerns the debt-equity mix, and efficiency concerns how hard assets work.
  - **Difficulty:** recall · **Skill:** Identifying which family of ratios answers the short-term bill-paying question · **Sources:** cfi-financial-ratios

**2. The current ratio is computed by dividing which two numbers?**
- [ ] Net income by revenue
- [✔] Current assets by current liabilities
- [ ] Net income by shareholders' equity
- [ ] Total debt by shareholders' equity
  - **Answer:** option B
  - **Why:** The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities, a liquidity measure of how well current assets cover short-term obligations. The other pairings are profit margin, return on equity, and debt-to-equity.
  - **Difficulty:** recall · **Skill:** Recalling the current ratio formula · **Sources:** cfi-current-ratio, cfi-financial-ratios

**3. Harbor & Pine Cafe reports current assets of $60,000 and current liabilities of $30,000. What is its current ratio, and what does it mean?**
- [ ] 0.5 — it can cover only half of its short-term bills
- [ ] 0.5 — it owes only half as much as it owns
- [✔] 2.0 — two dollars of current assets for every dollar of short-term obligations
- [ ] 2.0 — it owes twice as much as it owns
  - **Answer:** option C
  - **Why:** Dividing $60,000 of current assets by $30,000 of current liabilities gives 2.0: two dollars of current assets stand behind each dollar of bills due within the year. The other readings either invert the ratio or confuse assets with debts.
  - **Difficulty:** application · **Skill:** Computing the current ratio from balance-sheet numbers and interpreting it · **Sources:** cfi-current-ratio, openstax-principles-accounting-2-2

**4. A furniture maker's income statement shows revenue of $200,000 and net income of $16,000. Which classic ratio can be computed from these two numbers, and what is it?**
- [ ] Debt-to-equity of 12.5
- [ ] Current ratio of 12.5
- [ ] Return on equity of 12.5
- [✔] Profit margin of 8%
  - **Answer:** option D
  - **Why:** Profit margin is net income divided by revenue: $16,000 divided by $200,000 equals 8%, meaning eight cents of profit per sales dollar. The other three ratios need balance-sheet numbers such as liabilities, equity, or current assets.
  - **Difficulty:** application · **Skill:** Computing profit margin and recognizing which statement supplies the inputs · **Sources:** cfi-profit-margin, cfi-financial-ratios

**5. A company reports a debt-to-equity ratio of 1.5 for a single year. Which reading of that one number is the most defensible?**
- [ ] It proves the company relies on more debt than every competitor
- [✔] It must be compared with the company's own history and its industry peers before conclusions are drawn
- [ ] It guarantees the company will struggle to borrow again
- [ ] It shows the company earned one and a half times its equity this year
  - **Answer:** option B
  - **Why:** A single ratio means little alone: analysts compare ratios over time and against peers, because the same number can be routine in one industry and alarming in another. The other readings overclaim what one figure can prove.
  - **Difficulty:** analysis · **Skill:** Evaluating how much a single ratio can support a conclusion · **Sources:** cfi-financial-ratios
