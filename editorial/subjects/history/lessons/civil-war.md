# The Civil War

## In 30 Seconds

The Civil War (1861-1865) was the war between the United States and eleven Southern states that seceded to protect slavery. The central dispute was over slavery's expansion into new western territories. It began at Fort Sumter in April 1861, became a war to end slavery after the 1863 Emancipation Proclamation, killed more Americans than any other war, and ended in Union victory and the abolition of slavery by the Thirteenth Amendment.

## Why This Matters

The Civil War settled two questions the Founders left open: whether a state could leave the Union, and whether a nation "conceived in liberty" could keep holding four million people in slavery. Its answers still shape American law and identity, from birthright citizenship to debates over how the war is remembered. Understanding it also builds a core historical skill: reading the causes of a conflict from the participants' own words rather than from later myths, and telling established facts about what happened apart from contested interpretations of what it meant.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain that the central cause of the Civil War was slavery, above all the conflict over its expansion into new territories.
- Distinguish the "states' rights" argument as the constitutional vehicle for defending slavery from the Lost Cause claim that slavery was incidental.
- Sequence the war's key events: secession, Fort Sumter, the Emancipation Proclamation, Gettysburg and Vicksburg, and Appomattox.
- Describe what the Emancipation Proclamation did and did not do, and why the Thirteenth Amendment was needed.
- Evaluate a historical claim about the war's causes using the seceding states' own declarations as evidence.

## The College Version

### The central cause: slavery and its expansion

By 1860 the United States had argued about slavery for decades, but the specific fault line was its expansion. Every time the country added western territory, Americans fought over whether slavery would be allowed there, from the Missouri controversy of 1819 to the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854. The Republican Party formed around one plank: keeping slavery out of the territories as the nation grew. When Abraham Lincoln won the presidency in 1860 on that platform, slaveholding states concluded that the political future belonged to a movement hostile to slavery's growth. We do not have to guess at their reasoning, because several seceding states published formal declarations of their causes. Mississippi's begins, "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery -- the greatest material interest of the world." These documents name the protection of slavery, plainly and repeatedly, as the reason for leaving the Union. That is why historians identify slavery, and above all the fight over its expansion, as the central cause of the war.

### States' rights and the Lost Cause

People sometimes say the war was about "states' rights," not slavery. The honest way to state the relationship is that states' rights was the constitutional vehicle, and the protection of slavery was the cargo. The specific right the seceding states claimed was the right to hold human beings as property and to carry that institution into the territories, and they simultaneously demanded that the federal government override Northern states' resistance to returning escaped slaves. After the war, a set of ideas known as the "Lost Cause" recast the Confederacy's motive as a noble defense of local self-government and downplayed slavery. Reading the secession declarations shows why that framing does not hold up: the participants themselves put slavery at the center. Distinguishing the participants' stated causes from a later, sanitized narrative is exactly the kind of sourcing work historians do, and it matters because the Lost Cause version still circulates today.

### Secession, Fort Sumter, and the war's scale

South Carolina seceded first, on December 20, 1860. Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas followed by February 1861, and together they formed the Confederate States of America, whose constitution explicitly guaranteed the right of property in enslaved people. The shooting war began on April 12, 1861, when Confederate artillery bombarded the U.S. garrison at Fort Sumter in Charleston harbor; the fort surrendered two days later, and four more states then joined the Confederacy. What followed was the deadliest war in American history. The traditional estimate is roughly 620,000 soldier deaths; a widely cited 2011 demographic study puts the toll closer to 750,000, and some estimates run higher. More Americans died in the Civil War than in any other conflict the country has fought.

### Emancipation and the turning of the war

At first the Union fought only to restore the Union, not to end slavery. That changed on January 1, 1863, when Lincoln's Emancipation Proclamation took effect. It declared enslaved people in areas still in rebellion to be free and, crucially, authorized their enlistment as Union soldiers; roughly 180,000 Black men would serve. Its reach was limited: as a war measure it did not apply to the loyal border states or to Confederate areas already under Union control, and it could not free anyone where the Union army had no power. But it changed the war's purpose from union alone to union and freedom. That summer, two Union victories turned the military tide. At Gettysburg (July 1-3, 1863), Lee's invasion of the North was thrown back; the next day, July 4, the surrender of Vicksburg gave the Union control of the Mississippi River and cut the Confederacy in two.

### Union victory and the end of slavery

Lincoln's leadership held the Union's political coalition together through military setbacks, a divisive draft, and his own re-election campaign in 1864. After Grant's relentless 1864-1865 campaign in Virginia, the Confederacy's main eastern army under Robert E. Lee surrendered to Ulysses S. Grant at Appomattox Court House, Virginia, on April 9, 1865; remaining Confederate forces gave up over the following weeks. The Emancipation Proclamation had been a wartime order that did not reach every enslaved person, so the war's central issue was settled permanently by constitutional amendment. The Thirteenth Amendment, passed by Congress on January 31, 1865, and ratified on December 6, 1865, abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime, everywhere in the United States. The war that began over slavery ended by destroying it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Secession** — The formal withdrawal of a state from the United States. Eleven Southern states declared secession in 1860-1861 to form the Confederacy.
- **Confederate States of America (Confederacy)** — The government formed by the seceding Southern states in February 1861; its constitution explicitly protected the ownership of enslaved people.
- **Fort Sumter** — The U.S. fort in Charleston harbor, South Carolina, whose bombardment by Confederate forces on April 12, 1861, began the fighting.
- **Emancipation Proclamation** — Lincoln's order, effective January 1, 1863, declaring enslaved people in rebelling areas free and authorizing Black men to enlist in the Union army.
- **Thirteenth Amendment** — The 1865 constitutional amendment that abolished slavery and involuntary servitude, except as punishment for a crime, throughout the United States.
- **Lost Cause** — A post-war narrative that recast the Confederacy's motive as a defense of states' rights and minimized slavery; contradicted by the seceding states' own declarations.
- **Popular sovereignty** — The pre-war idea that settlers in a territory should vote on whether to allow slavery; applied by the 1854 Kansas-Nebraska Act, it intensified the sectional conflict.
- **Appomattox Court House** — The Virginia village where Lee surrendered his army to Grant on April 9, 1865, effectively ending the war.

## Eli-10

The United States split into two sides and fought a war with itself. Southern states wanted to keep enslaving Black people and to spread slavery into new western land. When a president was elected who wanted to stop slavery from spreading, those states tried to leave the country and start their own. The rest of the country said they could not leave, and the fighting started in 1861. Partway through, the war became a fight to end slavery for good. The side that wanted to keep the country together and end slavery won in 1865, and a new rule was added to the Constitution making slavery illegal everywhere.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a huge household where some members are forced to work with no pay and no rights, and other members grow rich off that work. When the family votes for new rules that would stop that arrangement from spreading to new rooms of the house, the members who profit try to break away and take the unpaid workers with them. The rest refuse to let the household break apart, and a terrible fight follows over whether the forced labor can continue at all.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A country is not a family, and "breaking up a household" sounds far gentler than a war that killed hundreds of thousands of people. It also cannot capture that the enslaved people were not passive "workers" in a dispute among others: many escaped, resisted, and nearly 200,000 fought as Union soldiers to win their own freedom.

## Worked Example

Suppose you are handed the claim, "The Civil War was really about states' rights and taxes, not slavery," and asked to test it like a historian. You would not settle it by opinion; you would go to the sources closest to the decision. The seceding states each published a declaration of causes. Mississippi's opens, "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery." South Carolina's complaint centers on Northern states refusing to return escaped slaves. The Confederate constitution guaranteed the right of property in enslaved people. The "right" the states invoked was specifically the right to own people and expand slavery. So the evidence written by the participants themselves, at the moment of secession, points to slavery as the central cause. "States' rights" names the legal argument they used, not the thing they were fighting to protect.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Believing the war was mainly about states' rights or tariffs, with slavery a minor factor.
  **Correction:** The seceding states' own declarations name the protection of slavery as their cause. States' rights was the constitutional argument used to defend slavery, not a separate reason.
- **Mistake:** Thinking the Emancipation Proclamation instantly freed all enslaved people in the United States.
  **Correction:** It applied only to areas in rebellion, not to loyal border states or Union-occupied regions, and it freed people only as Union forces advanced. Nationwide abolition came with the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865.
- **Mistake:** Assuming the Union fought from the start to end slavery.
  **Correction:** The Union's initial war aim was to restore the Union. Ending slavery became an official war goal with the Emancipation Proclamation, taking effect January 1, 1863.
- **Mistake:** Placing the war's start with Lincoln's election or with Appomattox.
  **Correction:** The election (November 1860) and secession preceded the fighting; the war began with the bombardment of Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861, and the major fighting ended with Lee's surrender on April 9, 1865.
- **Mistake:** Treating the death toll as precisely known.
  **Correction:** Estimates range from the traditional ~620,000 soldier deaths to a widely cited ~750,000; it is best given as a range, while the fact that it was the nation's deadliest war is not in doubt.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Emancipation Proclamation (1863) vs. Thirteenth Amendment (1865):** The Proclamation was a wartime executive order that freed enslaved people only in rebelling areas as Union armies advanced. The Amendment was a permanent change to the Constitution that abolished slavery everywhere in the nation.
- **Union war aim in 1861 vs. after January 1863:** In 1861 the North fought to restore the Union without touching slavery in loyal states. After the Emancipation Proclamation, the war's purpose expanded to include ending slavery.
- **Participants' stated cause of secession vs. the later "Lost Cause" narrative:** The 1860-1861 declarations name the protection of slavery directly. The Lost Cause, written after the war, minimizes slavery and reframes the cause as states' rights, contradicting the primary record.

## Key Takeaway

The Civil War was fought over slavery, above all the fight to keep it from spreading, as the seceding states said in their own declarations. It began at Fort Sumter in 1861, turned into a war to end slavery with the 1863 Emancipation Proclamation, and ended in 1865 with Union victory at Appomattox and the abolition of slavery by the Thirteenth Amendment.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **According to the seceding states' own declarations of causes, what was the central issue that drove them to leave the Union?**
   - A. A dispute over federal tariff rates on manufactured goods
   - B. The protection and expansion of slavery
   - C. Disagreement over the location of the national capital
   - D. A conflict over immigration from Europe

   **Answer: B.** The declarations, such as Mississippi's ("Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery"), name the protection of slavery as the cause. Tariffs, the capital's location, and immigration were not the stated reasons for secession.

2. **A student argues, "The war was about states' rights, not slavery." Using the seceding states' declarations, what is the most accurate response?**
   - A. The student is correct; slavery played no role in secession
   - B. States' rights and slavery were unrelated issues at the time
   - C. The war was only about tariffs, so both parts of the claim are wrong
   - D. States' rights was the constitutional argument used to defend slavery, which the declarations name as the central cause

   **Answer: D.** The declarations invoke a specific "right"—the right to own enslaved people and extend slavery. States' rights was the legal vehicle for defending slavery, not a separate cause, so the two are linked rather than opposed.

3. **Which event marked the start of the fighting in the Civil War?**
   - A. The Confederate bombardment of Fort Sumter in April 1861
   - B. The Union victory at Gettysburg in July 1863
   - C. Lincoln's issuing of the Emancipation Proclamation in January 1863
   - D. Lee's surrender at Appomattox in April 1865

   **Answer: A.** The war began when Confederate forces bombarded Fort Sumter on April 12, 1861. Gettysburg and the Emancipation Proclamation came in 1863, and Appomattox marked the war's effective end in 1865.

4. **Why did the United States need the Thirteenth Amendment even after the Emancipation Proclamation had taken effect?**
   - A. The Proclamation had freed enslaved people in the loyal border states but not in the Confederacy
   - B. The Proclamation had already been struck down by the Supreme Court
   - C. The Proclamation applied only to areas in rebellion and was a wartime measure, so a constitutional amendment was needed to abolish slavery everywhere and permanently
   - D. The Amendment was needed only to compensate former slaveholders for their losses

   **Answer: C.** The Emancipation Proclamation, as a wartime order, did not reach the loyal border states or Union-occupied areas and could be challenged after the war. The Thirteenth Amendment (ratified December 6, 1865) abolished slavery nationwide and permanently. It did not exempt the Confederacy, was not struck down, and did not compensate slaveholders.

5. **Which statement about the scale and outcome of the Civil War is best supported by the evidence?**
   - A. It caused about 50,000 deaths, fewer than the American Revolution
   - B. It ended in a negotiated split into two permanent countries
   - C. Exactly 620,000 soldiers died, a figure historians regard as precise
   - D. It was the deadliest war in U.S. history, with roughly 620,000 to 750,000 deaths, and ended in Union victory

   **Answer: D.** Estimates range from the traditional ~620,000 to a widely cited ~750,000 soldier deaths, making it the deadliest U.S. war; it ended in Union victory, not a split. The 50,000 figure is far too low, the toll is a range rather than a precise number, and no permanent split occurred.
