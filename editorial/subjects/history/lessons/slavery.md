# Slavery in the United States

## In 30 Seconds

For nearly 250 years, American law treated millions of people of African descent as property. Chattel slavery meant enslaved people were owned for life, bought and sold, and their enslaved status passed to their children. By 1860 nearly four million people were enslaved, most forced to grow the cotton that anchored the U.S. economy. Yet the enslaved were never only victims: they built families, faith, and culture, and resisted in ways large and small until slavery was abolished in 1865.

## Why This Matters

Slavery is not a side chapter of American history; it shaped the nation's economy, laws, politics, and enduring inequalities. Understanding it means grasping how a society reconciled a founding ideal of liberty with the mass enslavement of human beings, and how enslaved people themselves resisted and helped end it. Studying slavery honestly builds the historical skills of weighing evidence, distinguishing established fact from interpretation, and centering the perspectives of people whom older narratives ignored. It also grounds later topics: the Civil War, emancipation, Reconstruction, and the long struggle for civil rights all begin here.

## Learning Objectives

- Define chattel slavery and explain what made enslaved status in the United States hereditary and permanent
- Explain how the cotton gin and the cotton boom made slavery central to the U.S. economy
- Describe the domestic slave trade after 1808 and its effect on enslaved families
- Analyze the forms of resistance and cultural survival through which enslaved people asserted their humanity
- Identify key figures and strategies of the abolition movement
- Explain how conflict over slavery drove the sectional crisis toward the Civil War

## The College Version

### What chattel slavery was

Slavery in the United States was a system of chattel slavery: enslaved people were legally defined as movable property, like livestock or furniture, that could be bought, sold, mortgaged, gifted, and inherited. Two features made it especially total. First, bondage lasted for life; an enslaved person had no fixed term of service and, in most cases, no legal path out. Second, enslaved status was hereditary and passed through the mother, so a child born to an enslaved woman was enslaved from birth regardless of the father. This principle turned slavery into a self-perpetuating institution that grew across generations. Slave codes across the Southern states enforced it by stripping enslaved people of legal personhood: they generally could not testify against white people in court, make binding contracts, own property securely, marry with legal recognition, or learn to read and write. OpenStax describes American slavery plainly as a dehumanizing, traumatizing crime against humanity, and the numbers convey its scale. About 3.2 million people were enslaved in the United States by 1850, and the enslaved population grew to nearly four million, 3,953,760 by the 1860 census, roughly one in eight people in the country. These were human beings held by force, not an abstraction, and every figure in this lesson stands for people with names, families, and inner lives.

### Cotton, the economy, and the Deep South

Slavery was not marginal to the American economy; it was central to it. In 1793 Eli Whitney patented the cotton gin, a simple machine that quickly separated seeds from raw cotton, a task that had been painfully slow by hand. The gin made short-staple cotton, which grows across the Deep South, hugely profitable, and cotton production exploded after the War of 1812. By 1860 the South produced about two-thirds of the world's cotton and roughly 80 percent of the global supply, and about 75 percent of the U.S. crop was exported. Cotton became the most valuable American commodity, its profits reaching far beyond the plantation to Northern textile mills, banks, insurers, and shippers. The South Carolina politician James Hammond captured the region's confidence with the boast that "cotton is king." To grow it, planters pushed slavery westward into the Mississippi River Valley, clearing forests and swamps in Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, and beyond with enslaved labor. Of the 3.2 million people enslaved in 1850, about 1.8 million worked in cotton. The wealth this produced concentrated in commercial centers like New Orleans, and it gave slaveholders enormous political power that they were determined to protect.

### The domestic slave trade and the assault on the family

In 1808 a federal law banning the importation of enslaved people from Africa took effect, the earliest date the Constitution allowed. Ending the Atlantic trade did not end slavery or even slow the demand for enslaved labor; instead it redirected that demand inward. As cotton spread across the Deep South, planters there bought enslaved people from the older Upper South states, Virginia, Maryland, and the Carolinas, where tobacco was declining. The result was a vast domestic, or internal, slave trade that forcibly moved roughly a million people south and west between 1808 and the Civil War, a forced migration sometimes called the "Second Middle Passage." Between 1790 and 1859, slaveholders in Virginia alone sold more than half a million people. Behind those figures lies a specific cruelty: the trade routinely tore families apart. Spouses, parents, and children could be sold to different buyers hundreds of miles apart, and enslaved women lived with the constant fear that a loved one might be sold away at any moment. Slave traders, auction blocks, and coffles of chained people marched overland became ordinary sights across the South.

### Humanity, agency, and resistance

Enslaved people were never merely victims of the system; they were its most determined opponents, and they built rich human lives against enormous odds. Families formed and endured even when the law refused to recognize them, and kin networks stretched across plantations. Faith was a source of strength: many enslaved people embraced Christianity on their own terms, and spirituals that invoked the Exodus story of deliverance from bondage carried coded messages of hope and resistance. Culture survived and adapted, from Brer Rabbit trickster tales that mocked the powerful to African-influenced naming patterns, music, foodways, and crafts such as basketmaking. Resistance ranged from the everyday to the dramatic. Enslaved people slowed the pace of work, feigned illness or ignorance, broke tools, and quietly sabotaged the demands placed on them. Some escaped, and the Underground Railroad, a network of routes and helpers of all races and backgrounds, aided freedom seekers, many of whom set out on their own before finding assistance. Others rose in armed revolt, including the 1811 German Coast uprising in Louisiana led by Charles Deslondes, and Nat Turner's rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia, in August 1831. Rebellions were brutally suppressed and triggered harsher laws, but together with daily defiance they show a people who never accepted their bondage as just.

### Abolition and the road to war

A movement to end slavery grew louder in the decades before the Civil War. In 1831 William Lloyd Garrison founded the newspaper The Liberator and demanded immediate, uncompensated abolition; in 1833 reformers organized the American Anti-Slavery Society, which counted roughly 250,000 members by 1838. Many abolitionists practiced "moral suasion," appealing to the public conscience, and rejected earlier schemes to relocate Black Americans to Africa. The movement's most powerful voices were often formerly enslaved people themselves. Frederick Douglass escaped slavery in 1838, published his searing autobiography in 1845, and founded the antislavery newspaper the North Star in 1847, becoming the era's leading Black orator and writer. Harriet Tubman, who escaped in 1849, returned to Maryland about 13 times between 1850 and 1860 and led more than 70 family and friends to freedom. Sojourner Truth, born enslaved in New York around 1797 and free from 1827, became a celebrated speaker for abolition and women's rights, most famously at the 1851 Akron convention. Meanwhile, conflict over whether slavery could expand westward pushed the nation toward crisis: the Compromise of 1850 with its harsh Fugitive Slave Act, the Kansas-Nebraska Act of 1854, and the Supreme Court's 1857 Dred Scott decision each forced the question onto the whole country and widened the gulf between free and slave states, a gulf that broke open into the Civil War, after which the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery in 1865.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Chattel slavery** — A system in which enslaved people are legally owned as movable property that can be bought, sold, and inherited, held for life with enslaved status passed to their children.
- **Slave codes** — Laws in the slaveholding states that defined enslaved people as property and denied them legal rights such as testifying in court, owning property, or learning to read.
- **Cotton gin** — A machine patented by Eli Whitney in 1793 that quickly separated seeds from raw cotton, making short-staple cotton highly profitable and expanding plantation slavery.
- **King Cotton** — The idea, voiced by Southern politicians, that cotton's dominance of world markets gave the slaveholding South decisive economic and political power.
- **Domestic (internal) slave trade** — The buying, selling, and forced relocation of enslaved people within the United States, which moved about a million people from the Upper South to the Deep South after the 1808 import ban.
- **Underground Railroad** — A shifting network of escape routes and helpers of many backgrounds that aided enslaved people seeking freedom, becoming more organized after 1850.
- **Abolitionism** — The movement to end slavery; its radical wing demanded immediate emancipation rather than gradual or compensated schemes.
- **Moral suasion** — An abolitionist strategy of appealing to the public conscience through argument and personal testimony to persuade people that slavery was a sin and a crime.

## Eli-10

For a long time in American history, the law let some people own other people. If you were enslaved, you were treated as property that could be sold, and any children you had were born enslaved too, so it never ended by itself. Most enslaved people were forced to grow cotton, which made owners and businesses very rich. But enslaved people were full human beings: they kept their families, their faith, and their music alive, and they resisted, by working slowly, by running away along the Underground Railroad, and sometimes by rising up. Brave people like Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman fought to end slavery, and after the Civil War it was finally abolished in 1865.

## Eli's Analogy

Think about the difference between a family member and a piece of furniture in a house. A family member has their own life, choices, and rights; furniture is listed on an inventory, can be sold, and is passed down when someone dies. Chattel slavery forced the law to treat living, thinking people as if they belonged in the second category, on the inventory rather than in the family.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** This comparison only shows the legal idea of treating people as property. It cannot capture the real horror, the violence, terror, and grief that enslaved people endured, nor their courage and humanity. Furniture feels nothing and does nothing; enslaved people loved, hoped, resisted, and helped end slavery. No analogy should ever make this history seem smaller than it was.

## Worked Example

Consider how one federal law reshaped slavery without weakening it. In 1808 the United States banned bringing enslaved people from Africa. You might expect that to shrink slavery, but trace the logic. Demand for enslaved labor was rising fast because the cotton gin (1793) had made Deep South cotton wildly profitable. With no new arrivals from overseas, that demand turned inward. Planters in Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana bought enslaved people from Virginia and Maryland, where older crops were fading. The outcome was a domestic slave trade that forced roughly a million people southward and split countless families, more than half a million sold out of Virginia alone between 1790 and 1859. The lesson: ending the Atlantic trade did not end slavery; it relocated its cruelty inside the nation's borders.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Assuming the cotton gin reduced the need for enslaved labor because it was a labor-saving machine. **Correction:** The gin sped up seed removal, which made growing cotton far more profitable and dramatically increased demand for enslaved field labor, expanding slavery rather than shrinking it.
- **Mistake:** Treating slavery as a purely Southern or economically minor institution. **Correction:** Cotton was the nation's most valuable export, tying Northern banks, mills, and shippers to slavery; it was central to the whole U.S. economy.
- **Mistake:** Picturing enslaved people as passive victims who simply endured. **Correction:** Enslaved people constantly asserted their humanity, preserving family, faith, and culture and resisting through everyday defiance, escape, and revolt.
- **Mistake:** Confusing the 1808 ban on importing enslaved people with the abolition of slavery. **Correction:** The 1808 law only stopped the Atlantic trade; slavery itself continued and expanded, and was not abolished until the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865.
- **Mistake:** Quoting "Ain't I a Woman?" as Sojourner Truth's exact 1851 words. **Correction:** Truth gave a famous speech in 1851, but the celebrated wording comes from Frances Gage's 1863 retelling; the earliest account, from a month after the speech, does not contain that phrase.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Chattel slavery vs. Indentured servitude:** An indentured servant worked for a fixed term and then went free; a chattel-enslaved person was property for life, and their children were born enslaved.
- **Atlantic slave trade vs. Domestic (internal) slave trade:** The Atlantic trade carried captives across the ocean from Africa and was banned by the U.S. in 1808; the domestic trade moved already-enslaved people within the United States and grew afterward.
- **Moral suasion vs. Armed rebellion:** Abolitionists like Garrison and Douglass tried to end slavery by changing public opinion, while uprisings like Nat Turner's sought to strike at the system directly; both were forms of resistance to slavery.

## Key Takeaway

Slavery in the United States was a system that treated nearly four million human beings as inheritable property and anchored the nation's cotton economy, yet the enslaved asserted their humanity through family, culture, and resistance, and, alongside abolitionists, helped drive the nation to end it.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What made slavery in the United States a system of chattel slavery?**
   - A. Enslaved people could purchase their freedom automatically after a fixed term of years.
   - B. Enslaved people were treated as inheritable property, held for life, with enslaved status passed to their children.
   - C. Slavery was limited to captives taken in war and did not extend to their descendants.
   - D. Enslaved people were bound to one plantation but were legally free to marry and own land.

   **Answer: B.** Chattel slavery meant enslaved people were owned as property for life, and enslaved status passed through the mother to their children, making bondage hereditary and permanent. A fixed term (A) describes indentured servitude; slavery was not limited to war captives' own generation (C); and slave codes denied enslaved people legal marriage and secure property ownership (D).

2. **How did the cotton gin, patented in 1793, affect slavery in the United States?**
   - A. It mechanized the harvest and steadily reduced the demand for enslaved labor.
   - B. It shifted cotton growing out of the South and into the northern states.
   - C. By making short-staple cotton profitable, it fueled a cotton boom that expanded plantation slavery across the Deep South.
   - D. It moved most cotton production from the United States to Britain.

   **Answer: C.** The gin quickly removed seeds from short-staple cotton, making it highly profitable and driving a boom that pushed plantation slavery westward across the Deep South. It increased rather than reduced demand for enslaved labor (A), cotton remained a Southern crop (B), and by 1860 the U.S. South, not Britain, produced most of the world's cotton (D).

3. **After Congress banned the importation of enslaved people from Africa effective in 1808, how did enslavers in the expanding Deep South mainly obtain labor?**
   - A. Through a domestic slave trade that forcibly moved about a million people from the Upper South, frequently separating families.
   - B. By legally importing enslaved people from the Caribbean to replace the African trade.
   - C. By replacing enslaved workers with paid European immigrant laborers.
   - D. By freeing enslaved people and rehiring them as wage workers on the plantations.

   **Answer: A.** With the Atlantic trade closed, demand shifted inward: a domestic slave trade moved roughly a million people from the Upper South to the Deep South and routinely split families. It was not met by legal Caribbean imports (B), by European wage laborers (C), or by freeing and rehiring workers (D), none of which describe the antebellum plantation economy.

4. **Which pairing correctly matches an abolitionist with their contribution?**
   - A. William Lloyd Garrison, who escaped slavery and wrote the Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass.
   - B. Harriet Tubman, who founded the newspaper The Liberator in 1831.
   - C. Sojourner Truth, who led about 13 rescue missions along the Underground Railroad.
   - D. Frederick Douglass, who escaped slavery in 1838 and became a leading abolitionist writer and orator, founding the North Star in 1847.

   **Answer: D.** Douglass escaped in 1838, published his Narrative in 1845, and founded the North Star in 1847. The other options swap people: Garrison founded The Liberator (he did not write Douglass's autobiography), The Liberator was Garrison's not Tubman's, and the roughly 13 Underground Railroad missions were Tubman's, not Truth's.

5. **Laws and rulings such as the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 and the Dred Scott decision of 1857 intensified the sectional crisis primarily because they**
   - A. abolished slavery throughout the western territories, provoking the South.
   - B. had almost no practical effect and were quickly repealed by Congress.
   - C. forced the question of slavery's expansion and enforcement onto the entire nation, deepening the divide between free and slave states.
   - D. applied only inside Washington, D.C., and affected no one living elsewhere.

   **Answer: C.** These measures made slavery a national issue: the Fugitive Slave Act compelled free states to help return escapees, and Dred Scott denied that Black Americans were citizens and limited Congress's power over slavery in the territories, sharpening the split that led to war. They did not abolish territorial slavery (A), were consequential rather than quickly repealed (B), and reached far beyond Washington (D).
