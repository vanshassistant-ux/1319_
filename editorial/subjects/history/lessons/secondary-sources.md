# Secondary Sources

## In 30 Seconds

A secondary source is an account created after the fact that analyzes, interprets, or summarizes primary evidence. It sits one step removed from the events: think textbooks, scholarly books, journal articles, biographies, and documentaries. Secondary sources are where historians make arguments about what happened and why. The same item can even be primary or secondary depending on the question you ask of it.

## Why This Matters

Most of what you read in a history course is secondary: textbooks, monographs, and journal articles that build an argument from primary evidence. Knowing that these are interpretations, not neutral records, changes how you read them. You start asking who wrote this, what evidence they used, whether other scholars agree, and how the argument has shifted over time. That habit protects you from mistaking one confident book for settled fact, and it is the foundation of every research paper, literature review, and evidence-based claim you will make in college and beyond.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a secondary source as a later account that interprets, analyzes, or summarizes primary evidence.
- Distinguish secondary sources from primary sources and classify common examples.
- Explain why the same item can be primary or secondary depending on the research question.
- Apply criteria—peer review, citation, author expertise, publisher—to judge a secondary source's reliability.
- Analyze how historians build on and argue with earlier scholarship (historiography).

## The College Version

### What makes a source secondary

A secondary source is a work created after the events it describes that analyzes, interprets, or summarizes evidence rather than recording the events firsthand. It sits one step removed from what happened. A primary source—a soldier's diary, a treaty, a census return, a photograph taken at the scene—comes directly from the moment under study. A secondary source is what a later author writes about that moment, usually by gathering and reasoning over many primary sources. When a historian reads dozens of letters, land records, and newspapers and then writes a book explaining why a rebellion began, the letters are primary and the book is secondary. The defining features are timing (created afterward) and function (it interprets rather than simply witnesses). Both matter: a memoir written decades later is still close to primary because its author participated in the events, whereas a scholar's monograph about those same events is secondary because its author is analyzing evidence from the outside.

### The common forms you will meet

In college the secondary sources you handle most are textbooks, scholarly monographs (single-topic academic books), peer-reviewed journal articles, biographies written after their subject's life, documentaries, dissertations, and book or exhibition reviews. Each carries a different weight. A textbook synthesizes a broad consensus for teaching and rarely makes an original argument. A journal article or monograph, by contrast, advances a specific claim and defends it with cited evidence. A documentary interprets a subject for a general audience—though it often embeds primary material, such as archival footage or interviews, which can be primary sources in their own right. Recognizing the form tells you what to expect: how original the argument is, how much evidence stands behind it, and how much interpretive distance separates the author from the events. Some frameworks add a third label, "tertiary," for reference works such as encyclopedias and textbooks that compile secondary scholarship; the primary/secondary line remains the distinction that matters most.

### Why classification depends on the question

A source is not permanently primary or secondary; its status depends on the research question you bring to it. A biology textbook is a secondary source if you are studying biology, because it summarizes the field's findings. But if your project is the history of science education—how textbooks portrayed evolution across the twentieth century—that same textbook becomes a primary source, because it is now direct evidence from the period you are studying. A 1990 history of World War II is secondary evidence about the 1940s, yet a primary source about how historians in 1990 understood the war. This is one of the most useful ideas in the discipline: before you label a source, ask what you are trying to find out. The label follows the question, not the object.

### Judging reliability and reading historiographically

Because secondary sources make arguments, you have to evaluate them rather than accept them. Scholarly work is filtered by standards, chief among them peer review, in which other experts assess an article or book before publication. Strong positive signals include an author with relevant expertise, a university press or recognized scholarly publisher, full citations that let you trace the evidence, and appropriate currency for the topic. These are not guarantees, but they distinguish vetted scholarship from unsupported opinion. Reading well also means reading historiographically. Historiography is the study of how historians have interpreted a topic over time. Scholars are in conversation: they build on, extend, and dispute one another's work, and two careful historians can read the same records and reach opposite conclusions because interpretation is shaped by their questions, methods, and context. So the goal is not to find the one true book but to locate a source within its debate—what it argues, whom it answers, and what evidence it rests on. Historians apply the same scrutiny to any source: who made it, for what audience, with what intent, and whether other sources corroborate it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Secondary source** — A work created after the events it describes that interprets, analyzes, or summarizes evidence rather than recording the events firsthand.
- **Primary source** — Firsthand or contemporary evidence produced by a participant or observer at the time of the events, such as a diary, treaty, or photograph taken at the scene.
- **Monograph** — A scholarly book that examines a single, focused topic in depth and advances an original argument.
- **Peer review** — A process in which experts in a field evaluate a manuscript's evidence and reasoning before it is published.
- **Historiography** — The study of how historians have interpreted and debated a topic over time, and of the methods they use.
- **Corroboration** — Checking a claim against additional sources to see whether independent evidence supports it.
- **Tertiary source** — A reference work, such as an encyclopedia, that compiles and condenses secondary scholarship rather than making an original argument.
- **Interpretation** — An evidence-based explanation of what past events meant or why they happened, which can reasonably differ between careful scholars.

## Eli-10

Imagine a soccer game. The players on the field and the video of the match are the primary sources—they are the event itself. A secondary source is the sports reporter who watches the game afterward and writes an article explaining who played well and why the team won. The reporter was not scoring goals; they are studying what happened and making sense of it. In history, the diaries, laws, and photographs are the game, and the textbooks, biographies, and history books are the reporters. Two reporters can watch the exact same game and still disagree about which player was the reason for the win, because explaining is not the same as just recording.

## Eli's Analogy

A secondary source is like a movie review: the movie is the primary source, and the review is a later, thoughtful take on it. You can enjoy a review, but you know it is one critic's argument, not the film itself.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** it understates the work behind good scholarship. A movie critic usually watches one film, but a historian's secondary source is built from many primary sources and is often checked by other experts through peer review before it is published—so it carries more evidence and vetting than a single opinion column.

## Worked Example

Suppose you are researching the 1889 Johnstown Flood. You gather survivors' letters, contemporary newspaper reports, and the Red Cross's relief records—these are primary sources. Then you read David McCullough's 1968 book about the flood: it is a secondary source, because it was written long after and interprets those primary materials into an argument about why the dam failed and who was responsible. Now change the question. If your new project is "how did American popular history writing portray corporate responsibility in the 1960s," McCullough's 1968 book flips roles and becomes a primary source—direct evidence from the period you are now studying. Same book, different classification, because the research question changed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a source as permanently primary or secondary based on what it is.** The label depends on your research question. Ask what you are studying before you classify; the same textbook can be secondary for its subject and primary for a study of textbooks.
- **Assuming a published history book states settled, neutral fact.** A secondary source is an argument built from evidence. Read it as one interpretation, and check what evidence supports it and whether other scholars agree.
- **Judging reliability only by how confident or recent a source sounds.** Weigh peer review, cited references, author expertise, and publisher. Confidence and recency alone do not make a claim trustworthy.
- **Confusing a memoir written decades later with a secondary source.** A participant's later memoir is still close to primary because its author lived the events; a secondary source is written by someone analyzing the events from outside.
- **Thinking that if two historians disagree, one must simply be wrong.** Interpretation legitimately varies. Careful scholars can reach different conclusions from the same evidence; the point is to weigh their arguments, not to assume error.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Primary source vs. Secondary source** — A primary source is firsthand evidence from the time of the events; a secondary source is a later work that interprets or analyzes such evidence.
- **Textbook vs. Scholarly monograph** — A textbook synthesizes broad consensus for teaching; a monograph advances an original, evidence-backed argument on a focused topic.
- **Secondary source vs. Tertiary source** — A secondary source analyzes primary evidence to make an argument; a tertiary source (e.g., an encyclopedia) compiles and condenses secondary work without an original thesis.

## Key Takeaway

Secondary sources are later works that interpret primary evidence, so read them as arguments—checking who made them, what evidence backs them, and how they fit the wider scholarly debate—while remembering that a source's classification depends on the question you ask of it.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best defines a secondary source in history?**
   - A. An account created after the events it describes that analyzes or interprets primary evidence
   - B. A firsthand record made by a participant or eyewitness during the events
   - C. Any document that is stored in a government or university archive
   - D. A source that has been translated from its original language into English

   **Answer: A.** A secondary source is defined by timing (created afterward) and function (it interprets or analyzes evidence). B describes a primary source. Archives (C) hold both primary and secondary materials. Translation (D) is unrelated to the category.

2. **Which of the following would normally be classified as a secondary source?**
   - A. A soldier's diary kept during a wartime campaign
   - B. A 1945 newspaper photograph taken during a battle
   - C. Census data collected by the government in 1900
   - D. A university-press biography written in 2010 about a nineteenth-century politician

   **Answer: D.** The 2010 biography was written long after its subject's life and interprets earlier evidence. The diary (A), battlefield photograph (B), and 1900 census (C) are firsthand, contemporary records—primary sources.

3. **A researcher is studying how American history textbooks changed between 1950 and 2000. In this specific project, the textbooks themselves function as:**
   - A. Secondary sources, because textbooks always summarize other scholarship
   - B. Primary sources, because they are the direct objects of study from the period being examined
   - C. Tertiary sources, because any reference-style book is automatically tertiary
   - D. Not sources at all, because textbooks cannot be used as evidence

   **Answer: B.** When the textbooks are the thing being studied—evidence from the period under examination—they act as primary sources. Classification depends on the research question.

4. **Two historians examine the same collection of letters and reach opposite conclusions about a leader's motives. What does this best illustrate about secondary sources?**
   - A. At least one historian must have misread the primary evidence
   - B. Secondary sources are unreliable and should generally be avoided
   - C. Secondary work involves interpretation, so scholars can build differing arguments from the same evidence
   - D. The letters are probably forgeries and cannot be trusted

   **Answer: C.** Writing a secondary source means interpreting evidence, and interpretation is shaped by a scholar's questions, methods, and context—so careful historians can legitimately disagree. This is the basis of historiography.

5. **When judging the reliability of a scholarly secondary source, which feature is the strongest positive signal?**
   - A. It was peer-reviewed and supports its claims with full citations to evidence
   - B. It states its conclusions with strong confidence and avoids hedging
   - C. It was published as recently as possible, regardless of the topic
   - D. It agrees with the opinions the reader already holds

   **Answer: A.** Peer review and full citation are core markers of vetted scholarship. Tone (B), recency alone (C), and agreement with prior belief (D) do not establish reliability.
