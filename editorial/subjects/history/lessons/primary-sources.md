# Primary Sources

## In 30 Seconds

A primary source is firsthand evidence: an object, document, or recording made at the time of an event by someone who witnessed or took part in it. Letters, laws, photographs, artifacts, and oral histories are all primary sources. They put you as close to the past as evidence allows, but each one is partial and shaped by its maker, so historians read them carefully and check them against other sources.

## Why This Matters

Primary sources are the raw material of history. Every credible claim about the past traces back to evidence created at the time, so knowing how to find, read, and question a primary source is the skill that separates informed judgment from repeating what others assert. The same habit transfers well beyond history class: evaluating an original document, dataset, or recording rather than a summary of it is central to research, journalism, law, and everyday media literacy. Learning to ask who made a source, when, and why builds the discipline of checking evidence before believing a story about it.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a primary source as firsthand evidence created at the time of the events studied.
- Distinguish a primary source from a secondary source in a specific case.
- Identify the major types of primary sources, from documents to artifacts to oral histories.
- Apply sourcing questions (who, when, why, for whom) to analyze a primary source.
- Explain why a single primary source is rarely enough and must be corroborated.

## The College Version

### What makes a source "primary"

A primary source is firsthand evidence: an object or document that comes directly from the time period being studied, created by someone who witnessed or took part in the events. A soldier's wartime letter, a treaty as it was signed, a census ledger, a photograph taken on the day, a pharaoh's statue carved during his reign — each is primary for the period it comes from. What matters is not the format but the relationship in time: the source was produced within the events, not written about them afterward. This is the key line against a secondary source, which is created after the fact and interprets or analyzes primary evidence — a modern biography of that pharaoh, a textbook chapter on a war, a documentary. Historians use both. Primary sources supply the direct evidence; secondary sources supply context and the accumulated interpretation of earlier scholars, a body of work called historiography. Because a primary source is defined by its relationship to a question, the same item can be primary for one inquiry and secondary for another: a 1950s history textbook is a secondary source about the events it describes, but a primary source for how history was taught in the 1950s.

### The main types of primary sources

Primary sources come in far more forms than the written page. Written documents are the most familiar: letters, diaries, laws, treaties, speeches, newspapers, and government records. Material culture — artifacts and objects such as tools, coins, buildings, clothing, and works of art — is primary evidence too, and often the only evidence for societies that left few or no written records. Images carry their own testimony: photographs, maps, paintings, posters, and political cartoons each capture how people saw and depicted their world. Oral histories — recorded interviews and testimony — preserve firsthand memory, especially for communities whose experiences were left out of official documents. Finally, quantitative records and data — censuses, tax rolls, ship manifests, price lists, and ledgers — let historians measure patterns across whole populations. The National Archives, for instance, provides analysis worksheets for nine distinct document types, including photographs, artifacts, maps, cartoons, sound recordings, and artwork, a reminder that evidence about the past is not limited to text.

### Reading a primary source: sourcing

You cannot take a primary source at face value; you have to interrogate it. Historians call the first step sourcing: before deciding what a document proves, ask who created it, when and where, why it was made, and for whom — its intended audience. A public proclamation written to justify a war reveals a government's official story, not necessarily what happened; a private diary reveals one person's candid view, not the whole event. Two widely used frameworks put this into practice. The Library of Congress teaches Observe, Reflect, Question: note the concrete details you can see or hear, interpret them using what you already know, then list the questions the source raises. The National Archives uses a parallel four-step sequence — meet the document, observe its parts, try to make sense of it, and use it as historical evidence — asking at each stage who wrote it, who received it, when, and why. Both frameworks slow you down so you make inferences from evidence rather than jumping to conclusions.

### Strengths and limits: why one source is rarely enough

Primary sources are valuable precisely because they are close in time to the events; the analogy is a court trial held quickly, while witness memory is still fresh. But proximity is not the same as objectivity. Every primary source is partial: it captures one vantage point, was made for a purpose, and often survives only because someone chose to preserve it — which means whole perspectives, especially those of the poor, the enslaved, the colonized, and the illiterate, can be missing from the record. A single source can be mistaken, one-sided, or deliberately misleading, and it cannot tell you what it does not contain. This is why historians corroborate: they compare a source against other independent sources, weigh agreements and contradictions, and treat a claim as well founded only when several lines of evidence support it. A primary source is where analysis begins, not where it ends. Recognizing the perspective built into a source, and building interpretations from that evidence, are skills the related topics on bias and perspective and on evidence and interpretation take further.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Primary source** — Firsthand evidence — an object, document, or recording — created at the time of the events being studied by a witness or participant.
- **Secondary source** — An account created after the fact that interprets, analyzes, or summarizes primary sources, such as a textbook or a scholarly biography.
- **Sourcing** — Analyzing a source by identifying who made it, when and where, why, and for what audience, before using it as evidence.
- **Corroboration** — Checking a claim by comparing a source against other independent sources to see whether they agree.
- **Material culture** — Physical objects and artifacts — tools, coins, buildings, art, clothing — used as primary evidence about the people who made and used them.
- **Oral history** — Recorded firsthand testimony or memory gathered through interviews, often preserving experiences absent from written records.
- **Provenance** — The origin and chain of custody of a source — where it came from and how it survived — which affects how much it can be trusted.
- **Historiography** — The study of how historians have interpreted and written about a topic over time.
- **Bias** — A source's built-in slant, arising from the maker's viewpoint, interests, or purpose, that shapes what it records and omits.

## Eli-10

A primary source is a piece of the past that was made while the past was actually happening. If your grandmother writes in her diary on the day something happens, that diary is a primary source. If a writer reads a stack of old diaries fifty years later and writes a book about them, the book is a secondary source. Primary sources aren't only writing — they can be a photograph, an old coin, a song, a map, or a recorded interview. To use one well, you become a detective: figure out who made it, when, and why they made it. That last question matters a lot, because people make things for reasons. A poster telling everyone the king is wonderful was made to make the king look good, so it tells you what the king wanted people to believe, not necessarily the truth. That is why detectives never trust just one clue. They gather several and see which story all the clues point to together.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of primary sources like the clues at the scene of a mystery. A fingerprint, a note, or a security photo comes straight from the event, so a good detective studies each one — but never trusts a single clue by itself and always checks whether the clues agree.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A crime scene has one hidden truth a detective can solve, while history is usually about many valid perspectives, not a single answer. Historical sources also can't be dusted for prints or cross-examined; many are lost forever, and the ones that survive were often kept by the powerful, so the "evidence" is unevenly preserved in a way a fresh crime scene is not.

## Worked Example

Suppose you are researching a 1911 factory fire. You find three primary sources: a fire-department incident report filed that week, a letter a surviving worker wrote to her sister two days later, and a newspaper photograph of the building taken during the blaze. Source each one. The incident report was written by officials to record cause and code violations, so it is strong on facts like the time and the locked exits but silent on how workers experienced it. The letter is firsthand and vivid but shaped by grief and by one person's limited view from inside. The photograph shows the scale of the fire but not why the exits failed. No single source is complete, and each carries its maker's purpose. By corroborating them — matching the report's locked-exit finding against the survivor's account of a jammed door and the photo's smoke pattern — you build an interpretation resting on several independent lines of evidence rather than on any one document alone.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a primary source as automatically true or objective because it comes from the time.** Firsthand does not mean unbiased. Every source has a maker with a viewpoint and a purpose; being close to the event makes a source valuable evidence, not a neutral fact.
- **Assuming primary sources are only written documents.** Artifacts, photographs, maps, art, recordings, oral histories, and datasets are all primary sources — often the only evidence for societies that left few written records.
- **Deciding a source is primary or secondary by its format alone (for example, "a book is always secondary").** Classification depends on the question. A published book is a secondary source about the events it describes but a primary source for the ideas and attitudes of the time it was written.
- **Building an argument on a single source because nothing contradicts it.** Silence is not confirmation. One source is partial and may simply omit what challenges it, so historians corroborate across multiple independent sources before trusting a claim.
- **Confusing sourcing with summarizing what a document says.** Summarizing repeats the content; sourcing analyzes the source — who made it, when, why, and for whom — which is what lets you judge how much to trust the content.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Primary source vs. Secondary source** — A primary source is created within the events by a witness or participant; a secondary source is created afterward and interprets or analyzes primary evidence. Good research uses both.
- **Sourcing a document vs. Summarizing a document** — Summarizing restates what the source says; sourcing steps back to ask who made it, when, why, and for whom, which is what lets you weigh its reliability.
- **Written documents vs. Material culture** — Both can be primary sources, but written records carry explicit statements, while artifacts, buildings, and objects testify indirectly and are often the only evidence for non-literate societies.

## Key Takeaway

A primary source is firsthand evidence made at the time of the events; it puts you closest to the past but is always partial and shaped by its maker, so historians source it (who, when, why, for whom) and corroborate it against other sources before trusting what it says.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What makes a source a primary source?**
   - A. It is the most reliable and unbiased account available on the topic
   - B. It was created at the time of the events by someone who witnessed or took part in them
   - C. It was written by a professional historian using careful methods
   - D. It summarizes and interprets earlier records for a general audience

   **Answer: B.** A primary source is defined by its relationship in time: it is firsthand evidence produced within the events by a witness or participant. It is not defined by reliability (primary sources are often biased), by professional authorship, or by summarizing others — the last describes a secondary source.

2. **A historian is studying the American Civil War. Which of the following would they classify as a primary source?**
   - A. A 2010 college textbook chapter about the war's major battles
   - B. An encyclopedia entry summarizing the causes of the war
   - C. A biography of a Civil War general written in 2005
   - D. A letter a soldier wrote home to his family during the war

   **Answer: D.** The soldier's wartime letter was created within the events by a participant, making it a primary source. The textbook, encyclopedia entry, and modern biography were all written after the fact and interpret earlier evidence, so they are secondary sources.

3. **When historians "source" a primary document, what are they primarily asking?**
   - A. Who created it, when, why, and for whom it was intended
   - B. How long the document is and what language it is written in
   - C. Whether present-day experts agree with its conclusions
   - D. How many copies of the document still survive today

   **Answer: A.** Sourcing means analyzing the origin and purpose of a source — its author, date, motive, and intended audience — before using it as evidence. Length and language, modern agreement, and survival counts may be noted but are not what sourcing centers on.

4. **A single diary kept by one plantation owner is the only surviving account of daily life on a particular estate. What is the best conclusion a historian should draw about using it?**
   - A. Because it was written at the time, the diary is objective and can be reported as fact
   - B. Because no other source contradicts it, the diary can be treated as the complete truth
   - C. It is valuable firsthand evidence but partial and one-sided, so its claims should be corroborated where possible
   - D. Because it is only one source, it proves nothing and should be set aside

   **Answer: C.** The diary is genuine firsthand evidence, so it is not worthless — but it reflects one perspective and omits the enslaved and other people's experiences. Being written at the time does not make it objective, and the absence of a contradicting source is not confirmation. The sound move is to use it critically and corroborate it.

5. **A historian studying public opinion in 1925 uses a newspaper editorial from that year arguing for a new law. In this use, the editorial is best described as:**
   - A. A secondary source, because editorials express opinion rather than fact
   - B. A tertiary source, because newspapers compile information from many places
   - C. Neither primary nor secondary, because opinion pieces are not evidence
   - D. A primary source, because it is firsthand evidence created at the time of the opinion being studied

   **Answer: D.** For the question of 1925 public opinion, the editorial comes directly from the period and phenomenon under study, so it is a primary source — even though it is opinion. Whether a source is primary depends on the research question, not on whether it contains opinion or is a newspaper.
