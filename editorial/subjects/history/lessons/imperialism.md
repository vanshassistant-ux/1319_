# Imperialism

## In 30 Seconds

Between about 1870 and 1914, industrial powers led by Britain, France, and other European states, joined later by the United States and Japan, conquered most of Africa and much of Asia. This "New Imperialism" was driven by hunger for raw materials and markets, by nationalist rivalry, and by racist ideas dressed up as a "civilizing mission." At the Berlin Conference of 1884-1885, Europeans carved up Africa with no African present. Conquest was often brutal, but colonized peoples resisted, and Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa in 1896.

## Why This Matters

Imperialism reshaped the modern world, and its effects are still visible. The borders of many African and Asian countries were drawn by outsiders at conferences like Berlin, ignoring existing peoples and polities, and those lines still generate conflict today. Colonial economies were built to extract wealth for distant powers, a pattern that shaped later poverty and dependence. Studying imperialism honestly means holding two things at once: naming documented injustices plainly, and recognizing the agency of colonized peoples, who negotiated, adapted, and fought back. It also sets up decolonization, the twentieth-century reversal in which those same peoples reclaimed independence, and it clarifies how industrial power, nationalism, and racism combined in a single historical moment.

## Learning Objectives

- Define "New Imperialism" and locate it in time (roughly 1870-1914), distinguishing it from earlier forms of colonization.
- Explain the economic, strategic-nationalist, and ideological motives behind late-19th-century imperialism, treating the "civilizing mission" as a rationalization rather than fact.
- Describe the Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference of 1884-1885, including that Africans had no representation.
- Describe the methods of imperial control and their human cost, using the Congo Free State under Leopold II as a documented example.
- Analyze the resistance and agency of colonized peoples, including Ethiopia's victory at Adwa in 1896, and explain imperialism's long-term legacies.

## The College Version

### What "New Imperialism" means

Imperialism is the policy of extending one state's power over other peoples and territories, whether through direct conquest, settlement, or economic and political control. Europeans had built overseas empires since the 1400s, but historians use the term "New Imperialism" for a distinct, intense wave running from roughly 1870 to 1914. Two things made it new. First, the Second Industrial Revolution gave a handful of industrial powers overwhelming advantages in weapons, steamships, railways, medicine (quinine against malaria), and communications, so that small European forces could project power deep into Africa and Asia. Second, the pace and scope were staggering: within a single generation, industrial states came to rule most of the globe. The leading powers were Britain and France, joined by Germany, Belgium, Italy, Portugal, and Russia, and later by non-European industrial states, the United States and Japan. This lesson centers Africa, where the transformation was most dramatic, while noting that the same dynamics reshaped South and Southeast Asia and the Pacific.

### Why the powers expanded: economic, strategic, and ideological motives

Historians usually group the motives into three strands. The first was economic: industrial economies wanted cheap raw materials (rubber, cotton, minerals, palm oil, ivory), new markets for factory goods, and places to invest capital. The second was strategic and nationalist: European states competed fiercely for prestige and for military advantage over rivals, and colonies became markers of great-power status, so that acquiring territory was partly about denying it to a competitor. The third was ideological, and it must be handled carefully. Imperialists justified conquest with a so-called "civilizing mission," the claim that they were uplifting supposedly backward peoples by bringing Christianity, commerce, and European civilization. This rhetoric rested, in the words of the historical record, on "ingrained assumptions of White superiority," often reinforced by a distorted "Social Darwinism" that ranked human societies. A careful student treats the civilizing mission as the imperialists' rationalization for domination and profit, not as an accurate description of what colonialism actually did. Naming the ideology is essential to understanding imperialism; endorsing it is not.

### The Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference

The most vivid example of New Imperialism is the "Scramble for Africa." At the start of the Second Industrial Revolution, Europeans controlled only about 10 percent of the African continent, mostly coastal footholds. By 1914 they controlled roughly 90 percent; only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. To keep their rivalry from erupting into war, European leaders met at the Berlin Conference, which ran from 15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885, convened by German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck. Fourteen states attended, thirteen European powers plus the United States, and, decisively, no representatives of any African kingdom, state, or people were present. The conference did not itself draw every border, but it set the rules of the race, most importantly the principle of "effective occupation," which recognized a claim only where a power actually controlled the territory. That rule turned a diplomatic contest into a rush to plant flags and impose administration on the ground, accelerating conquest rather than restraining it. The result was a continent partitioned by outsiders for their own convenience.

### Methods and human cost: the Congo Free State

Imperial powers controlled colonies through a mix of methods: coerced treaties with local rulers, outright military conquest with industrial weapons, "indirect rule" through protectorates that governed via existing elites while controlling foreign affairs and resources, and economic systems built to extract wealth. Infrastructure such as railways and ports was typically laid out to move raw materials from the interior to the coast for export, not to serve local needs. The human cost could be catastrophic, and the Congo Free State is the clearest documented case. Created in 1885 after the Berlin Conference, it was not a Belgian colony but the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium, run for his private profit behind humanitarian pretenses. To force the extraction of wild rubber and ivory, his regime relied on forced labor, hostage-taking, beatings, and mutilation, including the notorious severing of hands. Estimates of the death toll vary widely; historians put it at anywhere from at least one million to around ten million Congolese dead between 1885 and 1908. International exposure, including Roger Casement's investigation and the Congo Reform Association, eventually forced the Belgian state to take the colony over in 1908. These facts are established, not in dispute, and they are stated here plainly and without embellishment.

### Resistance and the agency of colonized peoples

Colonized peoples were never passive. Across Africa and Asia they resisted through diplomacy, revolt, and armed defense, and their choices shaped outcomes. The most striking military example is Ethiopia. When Italy tried to use the 1889 Treaty of Wuchale to claim a protectorate, a claim Emperor Menelik II rejected as a mistranslation, Ethiopia mobilized a large, well-supplied army. On 1 March 1896, at the Battle of Adwa, Menelik's forces decisively defeated the invading Italian army under General Oreste Baratieri, inflicting roughly 6,000 dead and taking some 3,000 prisoners in what has been called the worst defeat of a European army in the history of colonialism. Italy was forced to recognize Ethiopia's full independence, which the country preserved for decades. Adwa became a symbol of African resistance well beyond Ethiopia. It was not unique: colonized peoples mounted countless uprisings and long guerrilla struggles elsewhere. Keeping this resistance in view corrects a one-sided story told only from the conquerors' viewpoint.

### Legacies

Imperialism left long, damaging legacies. The arbitrary borders drawn by outsiders lumped together or split apart peoples with little regard for their own political and cultural realities, and many of those lines survived independence to fuel later conflict. Colonial economies geared toward extraction left many territories dependent on exporting a few raw materials, with infrastructure and institutions built for the colonizer's benefit rather than balanced development. Racial hierarchies embedded in colonial rule outlasted the empires themselves. At the same time, imperial conquest provoked the resistance and national movements that would, after 1945, drive decolonization, the topic that picks up this story. Understanding imperialism is therefore essential background for the twentieth century: it helps explain the causes of the World Wars, the map of the modern Global South, and ongoing debates about reparations, restitution, and historical memory.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Imperialism**: A policy or practice of extending one state's rule or dominating influence over other peoples and territories through conquest, settlement, or economic and political control.
- **New Imperialism**: The intense wave of empire-building from roughly 1870 to 1914, in which industrial powers rapidly conquered most of Africa and much of Asia, distinguished from earlier colonization by its speed, scale, and industrial technology.
- **Scramble for Africa**: The rapid partition and colonization of nearly the entire African continent by European powers between about 1880 and 1914, raising European control from roughly 10 percent to about 90 percent of Africa.
- **Berlin Conference (1884-1885)**: The meeting of fourteen states in Berlin, convened by Bismarck, that set rules for the European partition of Africa, notably "effective occupation," with no African representatives present.
- **Effective occupation**: The Berlin Conference principle that a European power's claim to an African territory was recognized only if it actually controlled the region, which spurred a rush to establish physical presence.
- **Civilizing mission**: The imperialists' claim that colonization uplifted "backward" peoples by bringing Christianity, commerce, and European civilization; understood by historians as a racist rationalization for conquest and profit, not as fact.
- **Indirect rule**: A method of colonial control in which the imperial power governed through existing local rulers and institutions while retaining authority over resources, defense, and foreign affairs.
- **Congo Free State**: The vast Central African territory (1885-1908) held as the personal private possession of King Leopold II of Belgium, whose forced-labor rubber system caused mass death before Belgium annexed it.
- **Battle of Adwa (1896)**: The 1 March 1896 battle in which Ethiopian forces under Emperor Menelik II decisively defeated an invading Italian army, preserving Ethiopia's independence and becoming a symbol of African resistance.

## Eli-10

In the late 1800s, a few countries with big factories, powerful guns, and steamships decided to grab as much of the world as they could. They wanted cheap materials for their factories, new customers, and bragging rights over their rivals. To feel good about it, they told themselves they were "helping" the people they conquered, which was not true. In 1884 and 1885, European leaders sat around a table in Berlin and split up nearly all of Africa on a map, and not a single African was invited. Some rulers, like King Leopold II in the Congo, treated whole regions as private money machines and did terrible things to the people there. But Africans and Asians did not just give up. In 1896 the army of Ethiopia, led by Emperor Menelik II, beat an invading Italian army at a battle called Adwa and kept their country free.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a group of powerful kids drawing lines all over a shared playground to claim each section as their own, deciding who owns what without ever asking the kids who were already playing there. Then they insist they are doing everyone a favor.

**Where the analogy breaks down**: The playground picture misses how serious and violent this was. Real imperialism involved armies, forced labor, and, in places like the Congo, millions of deaths, not a squabble over play space. And unlike playground kids who go home, colonized peoples lived under this rule for generations, and many fought back, sometimes winning, as Ethiopia did at Adwa.

## Worked Example

Suppose a document says a colonial power built a new railway to "develop" a colony, running from an inland mining district straight to a single coastal port, with no branches connecting the colony's own towns to one another. How should you read this evidence? Start by asking what the railway actually moved and for whom. A line that only connects a mine to a port is designed to export raw materials to the imperial homeland, not to knit the colony together or serve its people, so the stated motive of "development" looks like a cover for economic extraction. Next, contextualize: this fits the broader pattern of imperial economies built to pull wealth outward. Finally, corroborate by asking who profited and who was made to work, and under what conditions. This is the historian's method, sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration, applied to a real imperial claim, and it shows why the "civilizing" or "developing" language of empire should be tested against what the infrastructure and labor systems were actually for.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake**: Treating the "civilizing mission" as a true description of what colonialism did for colonized peoples.
  **Correction**: It was an ideological justification rooted in racist assumptions of European superiority. Historians name it to explain how imperialists rationalized conquest, not to endorse it; the record shows extraction and coercion, not uplift.
- **Mistake**: Believing the Berlin Conference had African participants who negotiated their own borders.
  **Correction**: No representatives of any African state or people were invited. Fourteen states attended, thirteen European powers plus the United States, and they set the rules of partition among themselves.
- **Mistake**: Assuming the Congo Free State was a normal colony of the Belgian government.
  **Correction**: From 1885 to 1908 it was the personal, private possession of King Leopold II, run for his own profit. Only after international outrage did the Belgian state annex it in 1908.
- **Mistake**: Thinking colonized peoples simply accepted European rule without resistance.
  **Correction**: Resistance was widespread, through diplomacy, revolt, and war. Ethiopia's decisive defeat of Italy at Adwa in 1896 is the clearest case, but uprisings and long struggles occurred across Africa and Asia.
- **Mistake**: Confusing the New Imperialism with earlier European colonization or the Atlantic slave trade.
  **Correction**: The New Imperialism (c. 1870-1914) was a distinct, industrial-era wave centered on Africa and Asia. Earlier Atlantic colonization and the slave trade were separate, earlier developments covered in other lessons.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Direct rule** vs. **Indirect rule**: Direct rule replaced local authorities with the imperial power's own administrators, while indirect rule governed through existing local rulers and institutions, keeping them in place as intermediaries under overall imperial control.
- **Stated motive ("civilizing mission")** vs. **Actual motives (extraction, markets, prestige)**: Imperialists publicly framed conquest as uplift, but the driving forces were economic gain, competition for great-power status, and strategic advantage; the two must be kept separate when reading imperial sources.
- **Ethiopia at Adwa (1896)** vs. **The Congo Free State (1885-1908)**: Adwa shows successful armed resistance that preserved independence, while the Congo shows the catastrophic human cost of unchecked extraction; together they capture both the agency of colonized peoples and the brutality of imperial rule.

## Key Takeaway

The New Imperialism of roughly 1870-1914 saw industrial powers conquer most of Africa and Asia for raw materials, markets, and prestige, justified by a racist "civilizing mission" and formalized when Europeans partitioned Africa at the Berlin Conference with no Africans present. Its methods ranged from indirect rule to the mass death of the Congo Free State, but colonized peoples resisted, as Ethiopia's victory at Adwa in 1896 shows, and its arbitrary borders and extractive economies left legacies that shaped the twentieth century.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **At the Berlin Conference of 1884-1885, how were the major decisions about the partition of Africa made?**
   - A. African kingdoms sent delegates who negotiated their own borders with European powers
   - B. European powers, meeting without any African representatives, set the rules for dividing the continent among themselves
   - C. An early version of the United Nations supervised a fair partition of the continent
   - D. Britain alone was awarded control of the entire African continent

   **Answer: B.** The Berlin Conference (15 November 1884 to 26 February 1885) gathered fourteen states, thirteen European powers plus the United States, with no representatives of any African state or people, to set rules such as "effective occupation" for dividing Africa among themselves. Africans were not invited (A), no United Nations existed until 1945 (C), and control was split among several powers, not given to Britain alone (D).

2. **The Congo Free State, established in 1885, was unusual among colonies in that it was:**
   - A. Jointly governed by the fourteen states that had attended the Berlin Conference
   - B. An independent African republic recognized by the European powers
   - C. The personal private possession of King Leopold II of Belgium, run for his own profit
   - D. A colony administered directly by the United States government

   **Answer: C.** From 1885 to 1908 the Congo Free State was not a colony of the Belgian government but the personal, private possession of King Leopold II, who ran its forced-labor rubber economy for his own enrichment. It was not jointly governed by the Berlin powers (A), it was the opposite of an independent African republic (B), and it had no connection to the United States (D). Belgium annexed it only in 1908 after international outrage.

3. **What was the significance of the Battle of Adwa in 1896?**
   - A. It marked the opening of the Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference
   - B. It was the moment the Belgian state annexed the Congo Free State
   - C. It established the principle of "effective occupation" for colonial claims
   - D. Ethiopian forces under Menelik II decisively defeated an invading Italian army, preserving Ethiopia's independence

   **Answer: D.** On 1 March 1896 at Adwa, Emperor Menelik II's Ethiopian army decisively defeated the invading Italian force under General Baratieri, forcing Italy to recognize Ethiopia's independence in what has been called the worst defeat of a European army in the history of colonialism. The Scramble and Berlin Conference began earlier, in 1884-1885 (A), Belgium annexed the Congo in 1908 (B), and "effective occupation" was a Berlin Conference principle, not a result of Adwa (C).

4. **A textbook notes that European powers claimed a duty to "civilize" the peoples they colonized. How should a careful historian treat this "civilizing mission"?**
   - A. As a justification imperialists used to rationalize conquest and domination, rooted in racist assumptions, rather than as an accurate account of what colonialism did
   - B. As a reliable description of the benefits that colonial rule delivered to colonized peoples
   - C. As a purely economic policy with no connection to ideas about race or superiority
   - D. As a concept invented by later historians and never actually used at the time

   **Answer: A.** The "civilizing mission" was the imperialists' own justification for conquest, resting on ingrained assumptions of White superiority; historians name it to explain how domination and extraction were rationalized, not to accept it as true. It did not accurately describe colonialism's effects (B), it was deeply tied to racial ideology rather than neutral economics (C), and it was a genuine contemporary rhetoric, not a later invention (D).

5. **In a colony, railways and ports were built mainly to move rubber, minerals, and cash crops from the interior to the coast for export to Europe, while providing little benefit to local communities. This pattern best illustrates which motive for imperialism?**
   - A. A humanitarian desire to modernize colonized societies for their own sake
   - B. Religious conversion as the single overriding goal of empire
   - C. Economic extraction: using the colony as a source of raw materials and profit for the imperial power
   - D. Defense of Europe's own borders against foreign invasion

   **Answer: C.** Infrastructure designed only to funnel raw materials from the interior to a port for export reflects economic extraction, the use of the colony to enrich the imperial power. It is not genuine local development or humanitarian modernization (A), religious conversion does not explain export-oriented railways (B), and colonial extraction had nothing to do with defending European borders (D).
