# What Historians Do

## In 30 Seconds

The past is everything that ever happened; history is the account historians build from the traces that survived. That work is a craft: ask a focused question, find and weigh sources, interpret the evidence, and argue a case. Because sources are partial and the people asking change, interpretations get revised, and two careful historians can read the same event differently. History is less a fixed list of facts than a disciplined, evidence-based conversation about the human past.

## Why This Matters

Knowing how history is made changes how you read everything that claims to describe the past, from a textbook to a documentary to a viral post. It lets you tell an established fact from an argument about meaning, and to ask the questions a historian would: Where does this come from? Who made it, and why? What is left out? Academically, this is the foundation the rest of a history course builds on, and it transfers to any field that reasons from evidence. Practically, it builds the habit of checking a claim against its sources rather than trusting whoever tells the story most confidently. And it prepares you for the fact that good history is revised, not because it was wrong, but because new evidence and new questions keep arriving.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish the past (everything that happened) from history (the evidence-based account historians construct)
- Describe the historian's research process: asking a question, finding and evaluating sources, interpreting evidence, and building an argument
- Define historiography and explain why historical interpretations change over time
- Distinguish primary from secondary sources and explain why each must be read critically
- Analyze a scenario to identify why two historians might reach different, defensible interpretations of the same event

## The College Version

### History is not the same as the past

It is tempting to treat history as a fixed record of what happened, a list of dates and names waiting to be memorized. That confuses two different things. The past is everything that ever occurred, most of which left no trace and is gone for good. History is the account that historians reconstruct from the fraction of the past that survived: documents, objects, buildings, images, memories, and data. Because that surviving evidence is always partial, history is not a transcript of the past but a disciplined interpretation of it. This is why the discipline is defined by a method rather than by a body of settled facts. Facts matter enormously, and many are firmly established, but the work of history is turning scattered evidence into a supported account of what happened and, harder still, why. Keeping the two apart is the single most useful habit in the subject: "the past" is the territory, and "history" is the map that people draw of it, always at some scale, always leaving things out, always drawn by someone with a point of view.

### The craft: question, sources, evidence, argument

Historians do not simply "know" the past; they investigate it, and the investigation follows a recognizable shape. It begins with a question, something focused enough to answer with evidence, such as how a community experienced a war rather than the unanswerable "what was the war like." The question guides the search for sources, the surviving materials that might bear on it. Historians then evaluate those sources, asking who made each one, for whom, and why, because a source is a piece of evidence about its maker as much as about its subject. Next comes interpretation: weighing what the evidence can and cannot show, noticing gaps and silences, and testing one source against others. Finally the historian constructs an argument or a narrative, a claim about what happened and what it meant, supported by the evidence and open to challenge by anyone who reads the same sources. This is why history is a form of reasoning, not memorization. Two students given the same documents should be able to argue for different readings, and the better argument is the one that accounts for more of the evidence and hides less of it.

### Reading sources: primary, secondary, and critical

The raw materials of the craft are sources, and historians sort them into two broad kinds. A primary source was created during the period under study, an eyewitness or a direct product of the moment: a letter, a treaty, a photograph, a tax roll, a pot, a song. A secondary source comes later and retells, analyzes, or interprets that period, such as a modern biography or a scholarly article. The line can blur, since a source can be primary for one question and secondary for another, but the distinction shapes how a source is used. Neither kind is simply believed. Reading a source critically means attending to its author, its intended audience, its purpose, and the context that produced it, then asking what the source is in a position to know and what it might distort or omit. A wartime poster and a soldier's diary both come from the same conflict, yet they were made for different reasons and reveal different things. Corroboration, checking a claim against independent sources, is how historians move from what a single document asserts to what they can reasonably conclude.

### Historiography: why history gets rewritten

If history were just the past copied down, it would never change. It changes constantly, and the study of how history has been written and rewritten is called historiography. There are several reasons interpretations shift. New evidence surfaces, as archives open or long-ignored materials are read for the first time. New questions get asked, often about people earlier historians overlooked, such as the enslaved, the colonized, workers, women, and indigenous communities. And the historians themselves change, bringing the concerns of their own moment to old events. The result is that the same well-documented event can carry very different meanings from different vantage points. Few historians doubt that Columbus reached the Caribbean in 1492, but whether that encounter is best understood as a discovery or an invasion, and how it was experienced by the people already living there, has been thoroughly rethought. Competing scholarly accounts are therefore normal and healthy, not a sign that history is unreliable. Revision is what a living discipline does; a field that never reconsidered its conclusions would have stopped investigating.

### History among its neighbors

History shares the study of the human past with several evidence-based fields and increasingly works alongside them. Archaeology reconstructs the past chiefly from material remains, objects, structures, and physical traces, which makes it indispensable for times and places that left few or no written records. Related fields such as anthropology, geography, economics, and the digital analysis of large datasets feed the same effort from other angles. The American Historical Association's shared vocabulary for this kind of thinking, sometimes summarized as the "five C's", names the concepts historians lean on across all these collaborations: change over time, context, causality, contingency, and complexity. What tends to distinguish history within this family is its central reliance on interpreting sources of every kind, especially the textual record, to build explanatory arguments about human choices and their consequences. The boundaries are practical rather than rigid, and the strongest work often crosses them, but the historian's particular contribution is turning surviving evidence into a reasoned, contestable account of why things happened as they did.

## Key Vocabulary

- **History (the discipline)**: The disciplined, evidence-based study and interpretation of the human past, expressed as accounts that historians construct from surviving sources.
- **The past**: Everything that actually happened, most of which left no surviving trace; it is the subject that history tries to reconstruct, not history itself.
- **Source**: Any surviving material a historian uses as evidence about the past, such as a document, object, image, building, or dataset.
- **Primary source**: A source created during the period under study, standing close in time to the events, such as a letter, treaty, photograph, or artifact.
- **Secondary source**: A later account that retells, analyzes, or interprets a period, usually at a distance of time or place, such as a modern biography or scholarly article.
- **Interpretation**: The reasoned conclusion a historian draws from evidence about what happened and what it meant, always open to challenge by others reading the same sources.
- **Historiography**: The study of how history has been written and rewritten over time, including how and why interpretations of the same events change.
- **Corroboration**: Checking a claim in one source against independent sources to judge how well the evidence supports a conclusion.
- **Archaeology**: The evidence-based study of the human past chiefly through material remains such as objects and structures, closely related to history and vital where written records are scarce.

## Eli-10

The past is everything that ever happened, and almost all of it is gone. History is what people called historians manage to figure out from the bits that stuck around, like old letters, tools, pictures, and buildings. They don't just memorize it. They act like detectives: they ask a clear question, hunt for clues, check who left each clue and why, and then explain what they think happened and why. Because clues are always missing, two honest historians can look at the same clues and tell slightly different stories. And when someone finds a new clue or asks a new question, the story gets updated. That updating is normal, not a mistake.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a detective piecing together what happened in a room after everyone has left. They can't watch it happen; they only have what got left behind, a coffee cup, a note, a muddy footprint. So they line up the clues, ask who left each one and why, and build the most careful explanation that fits all of them. If a new clue turns up under the couch, a good detective changes the story to match. Historians do the same thing with letters, records, and objects instead of coffee cups.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The detective usually hunts for one right answer, a single "who did it," and once the case is closed it stays closed. History is rarely that tidy. Many historical questions are about meaning and causes, not a single culprit, so more than one careful answer can be reasonable at the same time. And a historical "case" is never permanently closed: new evidence and new questions keep reopening it, which is why history is rewritten rather than solved once and filed away.

## Worked Example

Imagine two historians studying the same event: a factory town's strike in 1912. They read the same box of sources, a mill owner's ledgers, the local newspaper, a set of workers' letters, and city police reports. The first historian asks why the strike failed and leans on the ledgers and police reports, arguing that the company simply outlasted the workers financially. The second asks how the strikers themselves understood their fight and leans on the letters, arguing that the strike built a lasting sense of solidarity even though it "failed" on paper. Neither is making things up; each question steers attention toward different evidence, and each reads the shared sources critically, noting that the newspaper depended on mill advertising and that the police reports recorded arrests, not motives. Both accounts are defensible, and both could be revised if a strikers' union archive surfaced later. That is history as a craft: the same past, honestly interpreted, yielding more than one supported story.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating history and the past as the same thing.
  **Correction:** The past is everything that happened; history is the account historians reconstruct from surviving evidence. History is always partial and interpreted, because most of the past left no trace.
- **Mistake:** Assuming history is just memorizing settled facts.
  **Correction:** Facts matter, but the discipline is a method: asking questions, evaluating sources, interpreting evidence, and arguing a case. The reasoning is the point, not the recall.
- **Mistake:** Believing that when historians disagree, one of them must be lying or wrong.
  **Correction:** Because evidence is partial and questions differ, more than one careful interpretation can be defensible. Disagreement over meaning is normal scholarship, distinct from disputing an established fact.
- **Mistake:** Thinking that rewriting history means earlier historians got everything wrong.
  **Correction:** Interpretations are revised because new evidence surfaces, new questions get asked, and new perspectives emerge. Revision is what a healthy discipline does, not proof the earlier work was worthless.
- **Mistake:** Taking a source at face value because it is old or firsthand.
  **Correction:** Every source is read critically for its author, audience, intent, and context. A firsthand account still has a purpose and blind spots, and its claims are corroborated against other sources.

## Compare / Contrast

- **The past** vs. **History**: The past is everything that happened, most of it lost; history is the evidence-based account historians build from what survived, always partial and interpreted.
- **Primary source** vs. **Secondary source**: A primary source was created during the period studied; a secondary source is a later work that retells, analyzes, or interprets that period.
- **Established fact** vs. **Interpretation**: A fact is what can be firmly documented (what happened, when); an interpretation is a supported argument about why it happened and what it meant, which can be revised.
- **History** vs. **Archaeology**: Both study the human past from evidence; history relies heavily on interpreting sources including the textual record, while archaeology works chiefly from material remains.

## Key Takeaway

History is not the past itself but a disciplined, evidence-based interpretation of it: historians ask questions, evaluate sources, and argue supported accounts, and those accounts are legitimately revised as new evidence, questions, and perspectives appear.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best captures the difference between "the past" and "history" as a discipline?**
   - A. There is no difference; the two words mean exactly the same thing
   - B. The past is the evidence-based account historians write, while history is everything that happened
   - C. The past is everything that happened, while history is the account historians reconstruct from surviving evidence
   - D. The past is only what is written down, while history is only what is spoken aloud

   **Answer: C.** The past is everything that occurred, most of it lost; history is the interpreted account historians build from the evidence that survived. Option B reverses the two terms. Option A denies a distinction the discipline depends on. Option D invents a written-versus-spoken split that is not what the terms mean.

2. **A historian is beginning a new project. Which sequence best reflects the historian's research process?**
   - A. Memorize accepted dates, then repeat them accurately in a narrative
   - B. Decide the conclusion first, then discard any sources that disagree with it
   - C. Collect every object from a period, then display them without commentary
   - D. Ask a focused question, find and evaluate sources, interpret the evidence, and build a supported argument

   **Answer: D.** The craft moves from a focused question to finding and weighing sources, interpreting them, and constructing an argument open to challenge. Option A reduces history to memorization. Option B describes cherry-picking, the opposite of evaluating evidence. Option C collects without the interpretation that makes it history.

3. **You are handed a soldier's 1915 diary and a 2010 scholarly book about the same war. How should each be classified?**
   - A. Both are secondary sources because both discuss the war
   - B. The diary is a primary source and the book is a secondary source
   - C. The diary is a secondary source and the book is a primary source
   - D. Neither is a source until it has been published by a museum

   **Answer: B.** A primary source is created during the period studied, like the 1915 diary; a secondary source, like the 2010 book, retells or interprets the period from a distance. Option A ignores that the diary is from the moment. Option C reverses the categories. Option D invents a publication requirement that does not define a source.

4. **In historiography, what is the main reason the same well-documented event can be interpreted differently over time?**
   - A. New evidence, new questions, and new perspectives lead historians to reevaluate earlier interpretations
   - B. Historians are required to disagree with anyone who came before them
   - C. The facts of what happened physically change as years pass
   - D. Older interpretations are always discarded because they were dishonest

   **Answer: A.** Historiography describes how interpretations shift as archives open, new questions arise (often about overlooked people), and later historians bring present concerns. Option B invents a rule of mandatory disagreement. Option C is impossible; established facts do not change, their interpretation does. Option D wrongly assumes earlier work was dishonest rather than working from less evidence.

5. **Two historians read the same box of sources about a 1912 strike and reach different but well-supported conclusions. What does this best illustrate about the discipline?**
   - A. At least one of the historians must be fabricating evidence
   - B. History cannot produce reliable knowledge because answers vary
   - C. The sources should be ignored since they led to disagreement
   - D. Different questions and partial evidence can yield more than one defensible interpretation of the same past

   **Answer: D.** Because evidence is partial and each historian asks a different question, both can build defensible, evidence-based arguments, which is normal scholarship. Option A assumes disagreement means fabrication. Option B confuses interpretive range with unreliability. Option C would abandon the evidence that makes history possible.
