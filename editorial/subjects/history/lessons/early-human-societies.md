# Early Human Societies

## In 30 Seconds

For most of the human story there were no cities, no writing, and no farms. Anatomically modern humans evolved in Africa somewhere between about 300,000 and 200,000 years ago, then spread across the globe over tens of thousands of years as nomadic hunter-gatherers. Only around 12,000 years ago did people in several separate regions begin to farm. That shift to agriculture produced food surpluses, permanent villages, and social hierarchy, setting the stage for the first cities.

## Why This Matters

This is the ground floor of every history course. Almost everything that follows, states, writing, trade, warfare, inequality, rests on two prehistoric turning points: the global spread of one African species, and the independent invention of farming. Understanding them teaches you to reason with archaeological evidence, to state dates as honest ranges rather than false certainties, and to see that "civilization" was not inevitable or singular but emerged from choices made in many places. It also inoculates you against a common error: assuming the Fertile Crescent was the only cradle. Human beings crossed that threshold on several continents, on their own.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain where and approximately when anatomically modern humans evolved, and why the date is given as a range.
- Describe the out-of-Africa migration as a process spanning tens of thousands of years.
- Distinguish the Paleolithic foraging way of life from the Neolithic farming way of life.
- Analyze why agriculture is described as developing independently in multiple world regions.
- Evaluate how food surplus led to permanent settlement, specialization, and social hierarchy.

## The College Version

### Human origins and a deep timeline

Human prehistory is the vast stretch of the human past that occurred before writing, so we reconstruct it from bones, tools, campsites, and DNA rather than from documents. Our own species, Homo sapiens, was not the first human. Earlier members of the genus Homo, such as Homo erectus, emerged roughly two million years ago and had already spread out of Africa long before us; Neanderthals lived across Europe and western Asia for tens of thousands of years. Anatomically modern humans, the people whose skeletons look essentially like ours, evolved in Africa. The Smithsonian's Human Origins Program dates this emergence to about 300,000 years ago, a figure supported by fossils such as those from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco; some survey textbooks, including OpenStax World History, still cite an earlier estimate of roughly 200,000 years ago. Both are approximate, and the honest way to state it is as a range, about 300,000 to 200,000 years ago, that has moved earlier as new fossils have been found. This habit, giving a range and naming the source, is central to historical thinking about deep time.

### Out of Africa: a migration, not a single trip

Beginning roughly 100,000 years ago, according to OpenStax, groups of Homo sapiens moved out of Africa and, over tens of thousands of years, populated the rest of the planet. This was not one journey but a slow, uneven expansion of small populations following coastlines, rivers, and game. The approximate sequence is consistent across sources: humans reached southern and eastern Asia by around 70,000 years ago, Australia and New Guinea by at least 45,000 years ago, and Europe by about 40,000 years ago, where they overlapped with Neanderthals before the Neanderthals disappeared around 40,000 years ago. By roughly 25,000 years ago people had reached Siberia, and by about 15,000 years ago some groups had crossed into the Americas. The Smithsonian gives a compatible ordering of continents. The precise dates are debated and continue to shift with new finds, which is exactly why historians present them as approximate ranges rather than fixed points.

### The Paleolithic way of life

The long era before farming is called the Paleolithic, or Old Stone Age, named for its chipped stone tools. It lasted until roughly 12,000 years ago, when the last ice age ended. Paleolithic people were foragers: they lived by hunting animals and gathering wild plants, nuts, fruits, grains, honey, shellfish, and more. They moved with the seasons and the herds, so they were nomadic, living in small tight-knit bands that OpenStax describes as ranging from about fifteen to forty people. They made tools from stone, bone, wood, and ivory, sewed clothing from hides using bone needles by around 30,000 years ago, and sheltered in caves or in structures built from mammoth bones and skins. Crucially, they controlled fire; OpenStax notes that modern humans could start and control fires by at least about 125,000 years ago. They also produced art and signs of spirituality, including the famous cave paintings of Europe and engraved objects. Foraging bands are generally described as relatively egalitarian, with no permanent rulers and little accumulated wealth, because a mobile group cannot easily store or carry surplus.

### The Neolithic Revolution and its consequences

About 12,000 years ago, as the climate warmed at the end of the ice age, some human populations began to farm. This transition to agriculture and the domestication of animals is called the Neolithic Revolution, and the resulting New Stone Age is defined by ground stone tools and settled life. The single most important point is that it did not happen once in one place. Farming was invented independently, at different times, in several separate regions. In the Near East, the arc of fertile land sometimes called the Fertile Crescent, people domesticated wheat and barley and herded sheep and goats. Around 10,000 years ago, peoples along the Yangtze and Yellow Rivers in China grew rice, millet, and soybeans. In sub-Saharan Africa people cultivated sorghum, possibly as early as 6,000 years ago; in Mesoamerica they grew squash and later maize (corn); and in South America they grew potatoes around 5,000 years ago. Animals were domesticated on similar independent timetables, sheep and goats in the Near East, chickens in South Asia, horses in Central Asia, and llamas in Peru.

Farming produced a reliable food surplus, and that surplus changed everything. It could feed far larger populations, so numbers grew. It rewarded staying put near fields and storehouses, so people built permanent villages; large settlements existed by around 9,000 years ago. A surplus meant not everyone had to produce food, so some people specialized as potters, weavers, builders, or priests. And stored wealth could be owned unequally, so the relatively flat social structure of foraging bands gave way to hierarchies of rich and poor, rulers and ruled. Historians therefore describe farming as setting the stage for the first cities and civilizations, which arose in river valleys such as Mesopotamia and Egypt, and which later topics take up in detail. It is worth adding that this was a trade-off, not pure progress: skeletal evidence suggests early farmers often worked harder, ate a narrower diet, and suffered more disease than foragers did.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Prehistory** — The span of the human past before written records, studied through physical evidence like bones, tools, and settlements.
- **Homo sapiens** — The species of anatomically modern humans, which evolved in Africa roughly 300,000 to 200,000 years ago.
- **Hunter-gatherers (foragers)** — People who obtain food by hunting wild animals and collecting wild plants rather than by farming.
- **Nomadic** — Moving from place to place rather than living in one permanent settlement, typically to follow food and water.
- **Paleolithic Age** — The Old Stone Age, the long era of chipped stone tools and foraging that ended about 12,000 years ago.
- **Neolithic Revolution** — The transition, beginning about 12,000 years ago, from foraging to farming and the domestication of plants and animals.
- **Domestication** — The process of adapting wild plants or animals over generations to serve human needs, such as growing wheat or herding goats.
- **Fertile Crescent** — An arc of fertile land in the Near East where wheat, barley, sheep, and goats were among the first species domesticated.
- **Surplus** — Food produced beyond immediate needs, which can be stored and which allowed populations to grow and some people to specialize in non-food work.
- **Social hierarchy** — A ranked ordering of people by wealth, status, or power, which emerged as farming allowed unequal accumulation of stored surplus.

## Eli-10

For a really long time, people did not have farms, towns, or writing. Everyone lived in small groups and got food by hunting animals and picking wild plants, so they had to keep moving to wherever the food was. This whole time started in Africa, and slowly, over many thousands of years, people walked and paddled to every part of the world. Then, only about twelve thousand years ago, people in a few different places, all on their own, figured out how to grow plants and raise animals on purpose. Now food came to them instead of the other way around, so they could finally stay in one spot, build villages, and grow extra food. With extra food, some people could do other jobs, and some families ended up with more stuff and more power than others. That is how the very first towns and, later, cities got started.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a big family that has to follow a food truck all over the city to eat, so they can never unpack. Then they plant a garden that feeds them right at home. Now they can build a real house, invite others, and some family members can spend their days doing things other than chasing food, like fixing things or making rules.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** It makes farming sound like a clear upgrade, but it was really a trade-off: early farmers often worked longer hours, ate a less varied diet, and got sick more than foragers did. And a garden is a personal choice made in one afternoon, while the Neolithic Revolution unfolded slowly over thousands of years and happened separately in several parts of the world.

## Worked Example

Suppose archaeologists excavate an ancient site and find these things together: mud-brick houses rebuilt in the same spot for generations, pits full of charred wheat grains, clay storage jars, and a few graves with rich jewelry alongside many plain graves. How should you read this? First, the permanent rebuilt houses and storage pits point to a settled, not nomadic, community, so this is Neolithic rather than Paleolithic. Second, the stored wheat is a food surplus, evidence of farming, not just foraging. Third, the contrast between rich and plain graves signals social hierarchy: some people accumulated more wealth than others. Chaining these together, you can conclude that this community had crossed the agricultural threshold and was developing the surplus-based inequality that, elsewhere, would grow into the first cities. Notice that no single artifact proves this; the interpretation comes from how the evidence fits together.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking farming was invented once, in the Fertile Crescent, and spread everywhere from there.** Agriculture arose independently in several regions, the Near East, China, sub-Saharan Africa, Mesoamerica, and South America, at different times and with different crops and animals.
- **Assuming Homo sapiens is the only or first human species.** Earlier hominins like Homo erectus left Africa around two million years ago, and Neanderthals lived in Europe and western Asia; modern humans overlapped with and outlasted them.
- **Stating a single exact date for when modern humans appeared.** The date is approximate and contested; give a range, about 300,000 to 200,000 years ago, and note that new fossil finds have pushed it earlier.
- **Picturing the out-of-Africa migration as one deliberate journey.** It was a slow, unplanned expansion of many small populations over tens of thousands of years, reaching different continents at different times.
- **Treating the switch to farming as pure progress that made life easier.** Farming enabled surplus and cities but was a trade-off; evidence suggests early farmers often worked harder, ate a narrower diet, and had more disease than foragers.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Paleolithic foraging societies vs. Neolithic farming societies:** Foragers were nomadic, lived in small egalitarian bands, and ate wild foods; farmers were settled, grew and stored surplus, supported larger populations, and developed specialized roles and social hierarchy.
- **Domestication of plants vs. domestication of animals:** Both were part of the Neolithic Revolution, but plant domestication (e.g., wheat, rice, maize) provided storable staple calories, while animal domestication (e.g., sheep, goats, llamas) provided meat, milk, hides, and labor; the two often developed together but on their own regional timetables.
- **Established fact vs. historical interpretation:** That humans evolved in Africa and later farmed is well-established fact anchored to evidence; why farming spread and whether it improved life are matters of interpretation where scholars weigh trade-offs.

## Key Takeaway

Modern humans evolved in Africa roughly 300,000 to 200,000 years ago and spread worldwide as nomadic hunter-gatherers; only about 12,000 years ago did farming arise, independently, in several regions, and its food surplus produced the settlements, specialization, and hierarchy that made the first cities possible.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Where and approximately when did anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) first evolve?**
   - A. In the Fertile Crescent, about 10,000 years ago
   - B. In Africa, roughly 300,000 to 200,000 years ago
   - C. In Europe, about 40,000 years ago
   - D. In East Asia, about 70,000 years ago

   **Answer: B.** Homo sapiens evolved in Africa; the Smithsonian dates this to about 300,000 years ago and OpenStax to about 200,000, so a range is the honest answer. The Fertile Crescent (about 10,000 years ago) is where farming, not our species, began. Europe and East Asia are places humans reached during the later out-of-Africa migration, not where they originated.

2. **Which description best characterizes people during the Paleolithic Age?**
   - A. Small nomadic bands of hunter-gatherers who used stone tools and controlled fire
   - B. Permanent farming villages centered on domesticated wheat and barley
   - C. Large cities with writing systems and a ruling class
   - D. Mounted herders who domesticated horses on the central Asian steppe

   **Answer: A.** The Paleolithic (Old Stone Age) was defined by nomadic foraging bands who made stone tools and used fire. Permanent villages and domesticated grain belong to the later Neolithic; cities with writing came later still; and horse domestication and herding were Neolithic and later developments.

3. **Which statement best describes how agriculture began?**
   - A. It began only in the Fertile Crescent and spread everywhere else through migration
   - B. It began first in Mesoamerica and was carried from there to Asia and Africa
   - C. It began simultaneously worldwide about 300,000 years ago
   - D. It arose independently in several regions, including the Near East, China, and the Americas

   **Answer: D.** Farming was invented independently in multiple regions at different times, with different crops: wheat and barley in the Near East, rice and millet in China, sorghum in Africa, maize in Mesoamerica, and potatoes in South America. It was not a single Fertile Crescent event, did not originate in Mesoamerica, and did not happen simultaneously; 300,000 years ago refers to human origins, not farming.

4. **Archaeologists find a site with permanent mud-brick houses, storage pits full of grain, and graves that range from very rich to very plain. This evidence most likely reflects which of the following?**
   - A. A Paleolithic foraging band living before the end of the last ice age
   - B. The initial migration of Homo sapiens out of Africa
   - C. A Neolithic farming community where food surplus enabled settlement and social hierarchy
   - D. A nomadic herding group that deliberately avoided any permanent settlement

   **Answer: C.** Permanent houses and grain storage indicate settled farming with a surplus, and the range from rich to plain graves indicates social hierarchy, both hallmarks of a Neolithic community. Paleolithic foragers were nomadic; the out-of-Africa migration involved mobile populations; and a group avoiding settlement would not leave rebuilt houses and storage pits.

5. **Why do historians say the shift to farming set the stage for the first cities and civilizations?**
   - A. Because farming required every member of a community to work the fields full-time
   - B. Because a reliable food surplus freed some people from producing food, enabling specialized roles and larger, denser populations
   - C. Because agriculture eliminated social hierarchy and made communities fully egalitarian
   - D. Because domesticated animals removed the need for any permanent settlements

   **Answer: B.** A stored food surplus meant not everyone had to grow food, so some people could specialize in crafts, building, or religion, and populations could grow large and concentrated, the preconditions for cities. Farming did not require everyone to farm full-time; it increased rather than eliminated hierarchy; and domesticated animals were part of settled farming life.
