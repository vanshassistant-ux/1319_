# Westward Expansion

## In 30 Seconds

Across the 19th century the United States pushed its borders from the Mississippi to the Pacific. Boosters called this "Manifest Destiny" - a belief, not a fact, that Americans were meant to take the continent. Purchases, annexation, and the Mexican-American War moved the map. But the same expansion forced Native nations from their homelands: the Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears killed thousands and broke treaty after treaty.

## Why This Matters

Westward expansion set the physical shape of the modern United States and the terms of who counted as an American. Understanding it as an ideology - Manifest Destiny - rather than an inevitability trains you to separate what people believed from what actually happened, a core skill in history. It also explains present-day realities: the borders of the Southwest, the location of tribal nations and reservations, and unresolved questions about land, sovereignty, and broken treaties. Reading this period honestly, with the Native experience at the center, is a model for how to study any story of conquest and settlement.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain Manifest Destiny as an ideology used to justify expansion, and identify its racial and nationalist assumptions.
- Identify the major territorial acquisitions between 1803 and 1848 and how each was obtained.
- Describe the Mexican-American War and the terms of the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.
- Explain the Indian Removal Act and the Trail of Tears, including the human cost and the breaking of treaties.
- Analyze how a favorable court ruling (Worcester v. Georgia) failed to protect Native land when the executive refused to enforce it.

## The College Version

### Manifest Destiny: an ideology, not a destiny

In 1845 the journalist John L. O'Sullivan popularized the phrase "Manifest Destiny" in editorials urging the annexation of Texas and Oregon. The idea was that it was the God-given, obvious ("manifest") right and duty of the United States to expand across North America. It is essential to treat this as an ideology - a belief people used to justify what they wanted to do - and not as a fact or an actual destiny. Nothing about continental conquest was inevitable; it was chosen, argued for, and carried out by human decisions. The belief carried explicit racial and nationalist assumptions: its promoters framed expansion as the spread of White, Protestant, democratic civilization, which implied that the peoples already living on the land - Native nations and Mexican citizens - were obstacles to be removed or absorbed rather than rightful owners. Naming those assumptions is part of understanding the period accurately. Historians distinguish the rhetoric of Manifest Destiny from the concrete forces also driving expansion: hunger for farmland, commercial access to Pacific ports, land speculation, and the political struggle over whether new territory would be slave or free.

### How the map changed, 1803-1846

The continental United States was assembled in stages. The Louisiana Purchase of 1803 bought roughly 530 million acres from France for $15 million, doubling the country and setting the pattern of acquiring land far faster than it could be settled. Texas is the next hinge. American settlers in the Mexican state of Texas rebelled and won independence in 1836, creating the Republic of Texas; the United States annexed it by joint resolution passed March 1, 1845, admitting Texas on December 29, 1845. Because Mexico had never recognized Texan independence, annexation pushed the two countries toward war. In the Pacific Northwest, the United States and Britain had jointly occupied the Oregon Country; the Oregon Treaty of 1846 divided it at the 49th parallel, giving the United States the future states of Oregon, Washington, and Idaho without a war. Within three years, then, the country gained Texas by annexation and Oregon by negotiation - and was about to gain the Southwest by force.

### The Mexican-American War and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

War between the United States and Mexico ran from 1846 to 1848. Fighting began in the disputed zone along the Rio Grande in the spring of 1846, and Congress declared war in May. U.S. forces eventually occupied Mexico City, and the war ended with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo, signed in February 1848. Under the treaty Mexico ceded an enormous territory - the Mexican Cession - that became California, Nevada, and Utah, most of Arizona and New Mexico, and parts of Colorado and Wyoming; this was roughly half of Mexico's national territory. In return the United States paid Mexico $15 million and assumed $3.35 million in claims by U.S. citizens against Mexico. The treaty promised that Mexican citizens who remained in the ceded lands would keep their property and become U.S. citizens, but in practice many Mexican Americans lost land and rights in the decades that followed. In a single stroke the war completed the reach of the United States to the Pacific.

### The Gold Rush and mass settlement

Days before the treaty was signed, gold was found at Sutter's Mill in Coloma, California, on January 24, 1848. After President Polk confirmed the discovery in December 1848, migration exploded. The "forty-niners" - named for the peak year, 1849 - poured in from the eastern United States, Europe, Asia, Latin America, and Australia; more than 100,000 people arrived within about a year. The rush transformed California almost overnight, funneling it toward statehood in 1850 and accelerating settlement of the whole West. It also concentrated enormous pressure on the Native peoples of California, whose population collapsed under disease, violence, and displacement during these years. Mass settlement, in other words, was never only a story of opportunity; for the people already there it was a catastrophe.

### The cost to Native nations: removal, the Trail of Tears, and broken treaties

Expansion was built on the dispossession of Native American nations, and this belongs at the center of the story, not in a footnote. In 1830 Congress passed the Indian Removal Act, which authorized the forced removal of Native nations east of the Mississippi to "Indian Territory" in the West (present-day Oklahoma). The Cherokee resisted through the U.S. legal system and won: in Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Supreme Court affirmed Cherokee sovereignty against Georgia. But President Andrew Jackson's administration refused to enforce the ruling, showing that a favorable court decision could not protect Native land when the executive branch declined to act. Removal proceeded anyway. A small, unauthorized faction signed the Treaty of New Echota (1835) - about twenty men, none of them elected Cherokee officials - purporting to trade all Cherokee land east of the Mississippi for $5 million and western territory. The great majority of Cherokee rejected it, but the United States used it to justify removal. In 1838 and 1839 the U.S. Army forced more than 16,000 Cherokee from Tennessee, Alabama, North Carolina, and Georgia and marched them roughly a thousand miles west. Thousands died of exposure, disease, hunger, and exhaustion - by many estimates around a fifth of the Cherokee people. This forced march is remembered as the Trail of Tears. The Cherokee were not alone: the Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw, and Seminole were driven west as well, and across the century treaty after treaty with Native nations was made and then broken as settlers advanced. Told honestly, westward expansion is inseparable from this record of forced removal, treaty-breaking, and death.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Manifest Destiny** - The 19th-century belief, popularized in 1845, that the United States had a God-given right and duty to expand across North America; an ideology used to justify expansion, not a fact.
- **Annexation** - The formal act of a country incorporating another territory or state into itself, as when the United States annexed the Republic of Texas in 1845.
- **Mexican Cession** - The vast territory - including California and most of the modern Southwest - that Mexico gave up to the United States under the 1848 Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.
- **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** - The 1848 treaty that ended the Mexican-American War, transferring roughly half of Mexico's territory to the United States for $15 million plus assumed claims.
- **Indian Removal Act** - An 1830 U.S. law authorizing the forced removal of Native nations from lands east of the Mississippi River to territory in the West.
- **Trail of Tears** - The forced removal, chiefly in 1838-1839, of the Cherokee and other southeastern nations to Indian Territory, during which thousands died.
- **Indian Territory** - The federally designated western lands (in present-day Oklahoma) to which the United States forcibly relocated southeastern Native nations.
- **Worcester v. Georgia (1832)** - A Supreme Court decision affirming Cherokee sovereignty against the state of Georgia, which the Jackson administration then refused to enforce.
- **Forty-niners** - The migrants who rushed to California in 1849 after the 1848 discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill.

## Eli-10

In the 1800s the United States grew from a strip of land near the Atlantic all the way to the Pacific Ocean. Leaders sold this as "Manifest Destiny" - the idea that Americans were simply meant to have the whole continent. But an idea about what people deserve is not the same as the truth. To get the land, the country bought some (Louisiana, from France), added Texas and Oregon, and took the Southwest from Mexico in a war. Meanwhile, Native nations who had lived on the land for centuries were pushed off it. The government forced the Cherokee and their neighbors to walk hundreds of miles west, and thousands died on the way. So the same expansion that put new states on the map was, for Native peoples, a disaster.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a family declares it is their "destiny" to own the whole apartment building, then takes over one unit after another - buying one, arguing another away, and simply evicting the neighbors from the rest. Saying it was "meant to be" does not change the fact that real people were forced out of their homes.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy shrinks a continent to a building and leaves out the deaths, the broken treaties, and the scale of loss to Native nations. Evictions in a building are governed by courts that can protect tenants; here the Supreme Court actually ruled for the Cherokee and the government ignored it, which is worse than the analogy suggests.

## Worked Example

Put the acquisitions in order and label how each was obtained, then check the logic of Manifest Destiny against it. Louisiana (1803) - purchased from France. Texas (1845) - annexed after settlers revolted against Mexico. Oregon (1846) - negotiated with Britain at the 49th parallel. The Mexican Cession (1848) - taken by war and formalized in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo. Notice the pattern: two were bought or negotiated, but the largest single addition came through military force, and none of it was empty. If expansion were truly a "manifest" destiny, you would not need purchases, treaties, a war, and the forced removal of Native nations to accomplish it. The messy mechanics are the evidence that "destiny" was a justification laid over ordinary human choices.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating Manifest Destiny as a real historical force or an inevitable outcome.** It was an ideology - a belief people used to justify expansion. Describe what it claimed and who promoted it, not "what was destined to happen."
- **Telling westward expansion as a triumphant settler story with Native peoples as a side note.** Dispossession of Native nations is central to the period. The Indian Removal Act, the Trail of Tears, and broken treaties belong in the main narrative.
- **Assuming the Treaty of New Echota showed Cherokee consent to removal.** It was signed by about twenty men who held no elected authority; the great majority of Cherokee rejected it, yet the U.S. used it to justify removal.
- **Thinking Worcester v. Georgia protected the Cherokee because they won.** They won the ruling, but the Jackson administration refused to enforce it, so removal went ahead - a favorable court decision did not stop the loss of their land.
- **Confusing the 1848 gold discovery with the 1849 rush.** Gold was found at Sutter's Mill in January 1848; the mass migration of "forty-niners" came in 1849 after the discovery was widely confirmed.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Oregon settlement (1846) vs. Mexican Cession (1848):** Oregon was gained by negotiating a boundary with Britain at the 49th parallel; the Southwest was taken by war and formalized in the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo.
- **Manifest Destiny vs. the actual causes of expansion:** Manifest Destiny is the belief used to justify expansion; the actual drivers included land hunger, Pacific trade, speculation, and the slavery question - which the ideology masked.
- **Legal victory vs. political power:** In Worcester v. Georgia the Cherokee won in the Supreme Court, but with the executive refusing to enforce the ruling, the legal victory did not translate into protection on the ground.

## Key Takeaway

Westward expansion built the continental United States through purchase, annexation, and a war with Mexico - but "Manifest Destiny" was a justifying belief, not a destiny, and its cost was the forced removal, treaty-breaking, and death inflicted on Native nations, above all the Trail of Tears.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. In the context of 19th-century U.S. westward expansion, "Manifest Destiny" is best described as:** (recall)

- A. A belief used to justify U.S. expansion across the continent
- B. A treaty that fixed the border between the United States and Mexico
- C. A federal law that required settlers to move west
- D. An inevitable outcome that historians agree was destined to occur

**Answer: A.** Manifest Destiny was an ideology - a belief, popularized in 1845, that Americans had a God-given right to expand. It was not a treaty (Guadalupe Hidalgo was), not a law (the Indian Removal Act was), and not an inevitability: expansion resulted from human choices, purchases, and war.

**2. The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848) ended the Mexican-American War. What was its most significant territorial result?** (recall)

- A. Britain ceded the Oregon Country to the United States
- B. France sold the Louisiana Territory to the United States
- C. Mexico ceded roughly half its territory, including California and most of the modern Southwest
- D. The Republic of Texas was created as an independent nation

**Answer: C.** The treaty transferred the Mexican Cession - California, Nevada, Utah, and most of Arizona and New Mexico, plus parts of Colorado and Wyoming - roughly half of Mexico's territory. Oregon (1846) came from Britain, Louisiana (1803) from France, and Texas independence (1836) predated the treaty.

**3. The forced removal of the Cherokee and other southeastern nations in the late 1830s, during which thousands died, is known as:** (recall)

- A. The Louisiana Purchase
- B. The Oregon Treaty
- C. The California Gold Rush
- D. The Trail of Tears

**Answer: D.** The Trail of Tears was the 1838-1839 forced march of more than 16,000 Cherokee (and, in the broader removal, the Choctaw, Creek, Chickasaw, and Seminole) to Indian Territory, in which thousands died. The other choices are a land purchase, a boundary treaty, and a migration - not the removal.

**4. In Worcester v. Georgia (1832) the Supreme Court ruled in favor of Cherokee sovereignty, yet removal proceeded anyway. This outcome best illustrates that:** (analysis)

- A. The Cherokee had no legal standing in United States courts
- B. A favorable court ruling did not protect Native land when the executive branch refused to enforce it
- C. The Indian Removal Act had already been repealed by 1832
- D. Georgia had no interest in acquiring Cherokee land

**Answer: B.** The Cherokee won the ruling, so they clearly had standing (ruling out A), the Removal Act remained in force (ruling out C), and Georgia was actively pursuing the land (ruling out D). Removal proceeded because the Jackson administration declined to enforce the Court's decision - a legal win without executive backing did not stop the dispossession.

**5. A student argues that the 1849 Gold Rush accelerated the settlement and statehood of California. Which fact best supports this claim?** (application)

- A. Gold was discovered only after California had already become a state
- B. The Gold Rush occurred before the United States acquired California from Mexico
- C. Migration to California fell sharply in the year after the discovery
- D. More than 100,000 migrants arrived in California within about a year of the discovery

**Answer: D.** A surge of more than 100,000 migrants within about a year directly supports rapid settlement, and California entered the Union in 1850. The other options are false: gold was found (Jan. 1848) before statehood and just after the U.S. acquired California, and migration surged rather than fell.
