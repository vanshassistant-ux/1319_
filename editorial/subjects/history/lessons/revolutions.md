# The Age of Revolutions

## In 30 Seconds

Between roughly 1775 and 1848, a chain of revolutions swept the Atlantic world, toppling monarchies and colonial rule in the name of Enlightenment ideals: natural rights, popular sovereignty, and equality before the law. The American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions shared these roots yet diverged sharply over who was actually freed. Haiti alone abolished slavery outright and extended citizenship across race, while most of the others proclaimed liberty even as they excluded the enslaved, women, and the poor.

## Why This Matters

These revolutions built the political vocabulary the modern world still argues in: citizenship, constitutions, human rights, and the idea that governments answer to the governed rather than to kings. They also expose a lasting tension — announcing universal rights while denying them to many — that later abolition, suffrage, and independence movements would press against. Studying the revolutions together, rather than as separate national stories, shows how ideas, people, and news traveled across the Atlantic, and how the same slogans could produce very different outcomes depending on who held power and who was doing the demanding.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the "Age of Revolutions" and identify the Enlightenment ideas its revolutions shared.
- Sequence the major events and dates of the French Revolution, from the Estates-General to Napoleon.
- Explain what made the Haitian Revolution distinctive among the Atlantic revolutions.
- Distinguish how the American, French, Haitian, and Latin American revolutions differed in who gained rights.
- Analyze the contradiction between proclaimed liberty and the persistence of slavery and exclusion.

## The College Version

### Shared roots: why so many revolutions at once

Historians group the upheavals of the later 18th and early 19th centuries into an "Age of Revolutions" because they were connected, not coincidental. Across the Atlantic world, colonists and subjects drew on the same pool of Enlightenment ideas: that people hold natural rights no ruler may violate, that legitimate authority rests on the consent of the governed (popular sovereignty), and that law should treat people equally rather than preserving the inherited privileges of nobles, established churches, and monarchs. Print culture, trade, and war carried these ideas — and news of each revolt — quickly across oceans. The American Revolution (armed conflict 1775-1783) opened the sequence: colonists objected that Britain taxed them without representation, the Continental Congress adopted the Declaration of Independence in 1776, Britain surrendered at Yorktown in 1781, and the Treaty of Paris recognized independence in 1783. That success showed others that a colonial people could break from a European monarchy and write a new government from stated principles. This lesson treats the American case as the opening reference and hands its detail to the dedicated topic; the comparative weight here falls on France, Haiti, and Spanish America.

### The French Revolution: from reform to republic to Napoleon

Facing fiscal crisis, Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General in 1789 for the first time in generations. The Third Estate — commoners — broke away to form a National Assembly, and on August 26, 1789 it issued the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, proclaiming liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty. A constitutional monarchy followed in 1791, but war and radicalization pushed the revolution further: on September 21, 1792 the monarchy was abolished and a Republic proclaimed, and Louis XVI was executed on January 21, 1793. From September 1793 to July 1794 the Committee of Public Safety, associated with Robespierre, governed through the Reign of Terror, executing thousands (roughly sixteen to seventeen thousand by guillotine, with many more imprisoned) in the name of defending the Republic. The Terror ended when Robespierre himself was overthrown and executed on July 28, 1794. Years of instability followed until Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in the coup of 18 Brumaire on November 9, 1799, eventually crowning himself emperor. The French Revolution thus moved through monarchy, republic, terror, and empire — a reminder that revolutions rarely deliver a stable new order at once.

### The Haitian Revolution: the revolt that made a nation

Saint-Domingue, France's Caribbean sugar colony, was among the wealthiest in the world and rested on the brutal labor of roughly half a million enslaved people. In August 1791 they rose in a mass uprising. Toussaint Louverture, himself formerly enslaved, emerged as a gifted military and political leader; revolutionary France abolished slavery in 1794, and Toussaint came to govern the colony. When Napoleon sent an expedition in 1802 to restore French control and slavery, the colony's people resisted. Toussaint was captured and died in French captivity in 1803, but the struggle continued under Jean-Jacques Dessalines, who declared independence on January 1, 1804 and named the country Haiti. The Haitian Revolution is unique: it is the only large-scale slave revolt in history to succeed in founding a state, it produced the first independent Black republic, and it made Haiti the second independent nation in the Americas after the United States. Alone among these revolutions, it abolished slavery immediately and extended citizenship regardless of race — which is why it is often underemphasized in accounts centered on Europe and North America, and why it deserves equal weight.

### Latin American independence: creole leaders and a continent redrawn

Spanish America's break came in the early 19th century, triggered largely from outside. During the Peninsular War (1808-1814), Napoleon invaded Spain and detained its king, throwing the empire's authority into question. Local governing councils, or juntas, formed in cities such as Mexico City, Caracas, Buenos Aires, Santiago, and Bogota, giving colonists their first taste of self-rule. The independence movements were led mainly by creoles — American-born people of European descent — and unfolded roughly between 1808 and 1826. In the north, Simon Bolivar (1783-1830), called the Liberator, led campaigns that freed Venezuela (secured at the Battle of Carabobo in 1821), New Granada, and Ecuador, uniting them briefly as Gran Colombia, and went on to help liberate Peru and Bolivia, which was named after him in 1825. In the south, Jose de San Martin led the liberation of Argentina and Chile and campaigned in Peru. The decisive Battle of Ayacucho on December 9, 1824 effectively ended Spanish rule on the South American mainland. Portuguese Brazil took a different, comparatively bloodless path to independence in 1822.

### Liberty for whom? The contradiction at the core

The era's grandest claims sat beside its sharpest contradictions. In the new United States, many leading revolutionaries were slaveholders, and the Constitution's Three-Fifths Clause counted part of the enslaved population for representation while denying them freedom or a vote. In France, rights were extended unevenly and mostly to certain men; women were excluded, and after briefly ending it, France reestablished slavery in its colonies under Napoleon. In Spanish America, creole leaders often limited their original aims to authority for White men of Spanish descent, and Indigenous, enslaved, and mixed-race (casta) populations were largely excluded from the new political rights. Haiti is the exception that proves how radical full inclusion was: it abolished slavery and extended citizenship across racial lines from the start. Reading these revolutions side by side, the pattern is clear — the language of universal rights outran its application, and the gap between proclaimed liberty and lived exclusion became the unfinished work that later abolition and suffrage movements inherited.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Age of Revolutions** — The period of roughly 1775-1848 in which a connected wave of political revolutions across the Atlantic world overthrew monarchies and colonial rule in the name of Enlightenment ideals.
- **Popular sovereignty** — The principle that legitimate political authority comes from the people rather than from a monarch's inherited or divine right.
- **Natural rights** — Rights understood to belong to people simply by virtue of being human, which governments are expected to protect rather than grant.
- **Estates-General** — The French assembly of three orders (clergy, nobility, and commoners) summoned by Louis XVI in 1789, whose commoner delegates broke away to form the National Assembly.
- **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen** — The 1789 French statement of principles proclaiming liberty, equality, and popular sovereignty as the basis of legitimate government.
- **Reign of Terror** — The violent phase of the French Revolution (September 1793-July 1794) during which the Committee of Public Safety executed thousands in the name of defending the Republic.
- **Saint-Domingue** — France's Caribbean sugar colony, built on the labor of enslaved Africans, whose 1791 uprising led to the founding of independent Haiti in 1804.
- **Creole (Spanish American)** — In colonial Spanish America, a person born in the Americas of European (Spanish) descent; creole elites led most of the region's independence movements.
- **Peninsular War** — The 1808-1814 conflict in which Napoleon invaded Spain and detained its king, provoking the legitimacy crisis that triggered Spanish American independence movements.

## Eli-10

For a long time, kings ruled because they said they were born to. Then a big idea spread: that ordinary people have rights, and that a government is only fair if the people agree to it. Within about fifty years, people in the American colonies, France, Haiti, and Spanish America all fought to throw out kings or distant rulers using that same idea. But there was a catch. Many of the people shouting "freedom" still kept other people enslaved, or only gave the new rights to some men. The one place that freed everyone right away was Haiti, where enslaved people won their own freedom and started their own country.

## Eli's Analogy

It was like the same song catching on in different towns. Everyone was humming the same tune about freedom and rights, but each town changed the words to fit who was in charge there, so the song meant something different in each place.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy makes it sound cheerful and voluntary. In reality these were wars with huge loss of life, and the differences were not just musical taste but questions of who counted as a full human being — most cruelly, whether enslaved people would be freed. A catchy song spreading is harmless; these revolutions involved violence, and their exclusions caused real, lasting harm.

## Worked Example

Put the four revolutions on one timeline and ask a single question of each: after independence, who was legally free? American Revolution (1775-1783): a new republic, but slavery continued and only some men could vote. French Revolution (from 1789): a republic by 1792, yet rights went mainly to certain men, and France restored colonial slavery under Napoleon. Haitian Revolution (1791-1804): enslaved people themselves won, slavery was abolished, and citizenship crossed racial lines. Latin American wars (c. 1808-1826): independence led by creole elites, with Indigenous, enslaved, and mixed-race people largely still excluded. Same Enlightenment slogans, four different answers — and only Haiti's answer was "everyone." Comparing the outcomes, rather than just the ideals, is how historians measure what a revolution actually changed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming the French Revolution quickly produced a stable democracy.** It moved through a constitutional monarchy, a republic, the Reign of Terror, and then Napoleon's empire. Its course was turbulent and reversed direction more than once.
- **Treating the Haitian Revolution as a minor sequel to the French one.** It was the only large-scale slave revolt in history to found a state, created the first independent Black republic, and was the one revolution to abolish slavery and extend citizenship across race immediately.
- **Reading "liberty and equality" as meaning everyone gained rights.** Rights were usually restricted by race, sex, and property. The gap between the universal language and its narrow application is a defining feature of the era.
- **Dating the French Republic to 1789.** 1789 began the revolution while France was still a monarchy; the monarchy was abolished and the Republic proclaimed on September 21, 1792.
- **Thinking one leader freed all of Latin America.** Independence came through many movements. Simon Bolivar led campaigns in the north and Jose de San Martin in the south, and Brazil's path from Portugal was separate again.

## Compare / Contrast

- **French Revolution vs. Haitian Revolution:** Both invoked the same rights language, but the French Revolution was led largely by the middle class and did not free the enslaved in France's colonies, while the Haitian Revolution was fought and won by enslaved people themselves and abolished slavery outright.
- **American Revolution vs. Latin American wars of independence:** Both were colonial breaks from a European monarchy, but they came decades apart, had different leaders and triggers (the Latin American movements were set off by Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain), and both left slavery and racial exclusion largely intact at first.
- **The Reign of Terror vs. the French Revolution as a whole:** The Terror (1793-1794) was one violent phase, not the entire revolution; treating it as the whole story mistakes a period of about a year for a decade-long transformation.

## Key Takeaway

The Age of Revolutions spread one set of Enlightenment ideas — natural rights and popular sovereignty — across the Atlantic, but the revolutions differed most in who they actually freed. Haiti alone abolished slavery and extended citizenship across race, while the others proclaimed liberty and left large groups excluded.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which revolution is described as the only large-scale slave revolt in history to succeed in founding a state, producing the first independent Black republic?**
   - A. The American Revolution
   - B. The Haitian Revolution
   - C. The French Revolution
   - D. The Mexican War of Independence

   **Answer: B.** The Haitian Revolution (1791-1804) was the only large-scale slave revolt to found a state; enslaved people won their freedom and Dessalines declared independent Haiti on January 1, 1804, the first independent Black republic. The American and French Revolutions did not free the enslaved, and Mexico's independence was a creole-led movement, not a slave revolt.

2. **In the French Revolution, what happened on September 21, 1792?**
   - A. Louis XVI summoned the Estates-General
   - B. The Bastille was stormed
   - C. Napoleon seized power in a coup
   - D. The monarchy was abolished and the Republic proclaimed

   **Answer: D.** The monarchy was abolished and the French Republic proclaimed on September 21, 1792. The Estates-General met in 1789, the Bastille was stormed on July 14, 1789, and Napoleon's coup came on November 9, 1799 — so those options are all misdated relative to the founding of the Republic.

3. **Which statement best captures the shared intellectual roots of the Age of Revolutions?**
   - A. Enlightenment ideas of natural rights and popular sovereignty
   - B. A single revolutionary government coordinating each revolt
   - C. Factory owners seeking cheaper industrial labor
   - D. Religious wars between Catholics and Protestants

   **Answer: A.** The revolutions drew on shared Enlightenment ideas — natural rights and the consent of the governed — spread by print and trade across the Atlantic. There was no single coordinating government; industrial labor and Catholic-Protestant religious wars belong to different developments and centuries.

4. **A student notes that the U.S. Constitution's Three-Fifths Clause counted part of the enslaved population for representation, and that France reestablished colonial slavery under Napoleon. These facts best illustrate which theme of the era?**
   - A. That the revolutions had no lasting political effects
   - B. That Enlightenment ideas played no role in the revolutions
   - C. The contradiction between proclaimed universal rights and continued slavery and exclusion
   - D. That every revolution of the era abolished slavery immediately

   **Answer: C.** Both facts show the gap between the era's universal rights language and its narrow application — the defining contradiction of the period. The revolutions did have lasting effects and were shaped by Enlightenment ideas, so those options are wrong, and only Haiti abolished slavery immediately, which makes the last option false.

5. **The Spanish American wars of independence were set off most directly by which event?**
   - A. The storming of the Bastille in 1789
   - B. The Treaty of Paris of 1783
   - C. The Haitian declaration of independence in 1804
   - D. Napoleon's 1808 invasion of Spain, which detained the Spanish king

   **Answer: D.** Napoleon's invasion of Spain during the Peninsular War (1808-1814) detained the king and triggered a legitimacy crisis, prompting juntas across Spanish America and the independence movements led by creole elites. The Bastille, the Treaty of Paris, and Haiti's independence were part of the wider era but were not the direct trigger for Spanish America's break.
