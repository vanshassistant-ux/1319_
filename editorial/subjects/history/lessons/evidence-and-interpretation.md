# Evidence and Interpretation

## In 30 Seconds

In history, evidence is what the sources actually show; interpretation is the argument a historian builds to explain that evidence. The two are different jobs. A good interpretation is not just an opinion: it is supported by evidence and reasoning, checked against multiple sources, and can be tested against those sources. Yet reasonable historians can still disagree, because they weigh the same evidence differently or ask different questions of it.

## Why This Matters

Knowing the difference between evidence and interpretation is what separates studying history from memorizing it. It lets you read a textbook or a documentary as an argument someone built, not as a fixed list of facts, and to ask what evidence supports it. That habit transfers well beyond history: evaluating a news story, a scientific claim, or a legal case all require distinguishing what the record shows from the conclusions people draw. As new evidence appears, interpretations change, so understanding how they are built prepares you to follow, and eventually to join, those debates.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish evidence (what the sources show) from interpretation (the argument that explains it).
- Explain why a historical interpretation is more than an opinion and how it can be tested against sources.
- Define corroboration and apply it to check a claim against multiple independent sources.
- Explain how reasonable historians can disagree while still arguing from evidence.
- Evaluate two interpretations and judge which is better supported by the evidence.

## The College Version

### Evidence is not the same as interpretation

History is not just a stored list of facts about the past; it is the ongoing work of explaining that past. Historians draw a working distinction between two things. Evidence is what the surviving sources actually show: a letter's wording, a census figure, an object's shape, a photograph's contents. Interpretation is the argument a historian constructs to explain that evidence, to answer why events unfolded as they did and what they meant. As one open history handbook puts it, history is fundamentally about argument, interpretation, and consequence: to do history well you must use appropriate evidence, assess it properly, and then draw meaningful conclusions from it. So a date carved on a monument is evidence; the claim that the monument was built to project a ruler's power is interpretation. Both are essential, but they carry different kinds of certainty. Evidence can be verified by returning to the source. Interpretation is a judgment that goes beyond any single source, weaving many fragments into one coherent account.

### Why an interpretation is not just an opinion

Because interpretation involves judgment, students sometimes conclude that in history "everything is opinion" and one view is as good as another. That is not how the discipline works. An opinion answers to nothing outside itself; a historical interpretation must answer to the sources. A strong claim is supported by evidence and by reasoning that connects the evidence to the conclusion, and it can be tested: another historian can go to the same archives, weigh the same documents, and check whether the argument holds. The Library of Congress teaches students to treat a primary source by observing its details, then generating and testing hypotheses about it, and then asking further questions, which is exactly this loop of evidence and checkable inference. An interpretation that no evidence could ever confirm or contradict is not really a historical argument at all. This is why "because I feel it happened that way" fails as history, while "the tax records and three independent letters point this way" can succeed.

### Corroboration: checking a claim against independent sources

One of the main tools for testing an interpretation is corroboration: comparing details across multiple sources to see whether they agree. The idea comes from research on how historians actually read, associated with Sam Wineburg and the Stanford History Education Group, whose framework names sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, and close reading as core historical reading skills. Corroboration matters because every source is partial and shaped by its creator's position and purpose. A single pro-Confederate newspaper editorial is genuine evidence of Confederate attitudes, but its claims about Union troop movements cannot simply be accepted as fact; you would look for independent accounts. When several sources that do not depend on one another point the same way, a claim rests on firmer ground. When they conflict, the historian has to explain the conflict, deciding which account is more reliable and why, rather than pretending it is not there. Independence is what gives corroboration its force: two newspapers copying the same wire report are really one source, not two.

### Why reasonable historians still disagree

If interpretations are disciplined by evidence, why do experts still argue? Because the evidence is incomplete and the same body of evidence can support more than one careful reading. Historians disagree when they weigh the surviving sources differently, when they ask different questions of the past, or when new evidence comes to light. Edward Gibbon, writing his six-volume *History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* between 1776 and 1788, placed heavy blame on the rise of Christianity for weakening the empire. Later historians, examining more evidence and asking different questions, reassessed that thesis: many now emphasize a mix of factors such as fiscal strain, leadership, epidemics, and external pressure, while scholars of "late antiquity" reframe the period as transformation rather than simple collapse. None of this is mere opinion trading. Each side argues from evidence; they differ over which evidence weighs most and what question is worth asking. This is why historical understanding is described as tentative: it is the best account of the evidence we now have, open to revision when the evidence or the questions change.

### Telling a stronger interpretation from a weaker one

If more than one interpretation can be defensible, they are still not equal. A stronger interpretation accounts for more of the relevant evidence, rests on sources checked against one another, and follows logically from what those sources show. Crucially, it also acknowledges the evidence that cuts against it and explains why the argument still holds, rather than hiding inconvenient sources. A weaker interpretation leans on a single source, ignores or explains away contrary evidence, over-reaches beyond what the evidence can bear, or substitutes assertion for reasoning. So when you meet a historical claim, ask a short set of questions: What evidence supports this? Is that evidence corroborated by independent sources? Does the argument address the counter-evidence? Does the conclusion stay within what the evidence can support? These questions do not always yield one winner, but they reliably separate arguments that earn belief from those that do not.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Evidence** — The information a surviving source provides about the past, such as the words in a document, a figure in a record, or the contents of a photograph.
- **Interpretation** — The argument a historian builds to explain evidence, addressing why something happened and what it meant, going beyond any single source.
- **Corroboration** — Comparing details across multiple sources to see whether they agree, used to test whether a claim is reliable.
- **Independent sources** — Sources that do not derive from one another, so their agreement is meaningful rather than a single account repeated.
- **Historical inference** — A conclusion a historian reasons to from a body of evidence, rather than a fact stated directly in one source.
- **Primary source** — A record produced during the period under study, used as direct evidence of that period.
- **Counter-evidence** — Evidence that runs against an interpretation, which a strong argument must acknowledge and address.
- **Tentative conclusion** — A historical judgment held as the best current account of the evidence and open to revision if new evidence or questions arise.

## Eli-10

Imagine three friends describe the same lunchroom argument. Evidence is what each friend actually says and saw. Interpretation is your explanation of what really happened and why. You don't just pick your favorite story; you check where the accounts agree and where they clash, and you build the explanation that fits the most reliable details. If one friend guessed at something they couldn't see, you don't count that part as strongly. A good explanation covers the parts that don't fit and says why. Two careful people might still land on slightly different explanations, but an explanation that ignores half the accounts is weaker than one that fits them all.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like being a detective. The clues at the scene are the evidence; your theory of who did it is the interpretation. A good detective's theory fits the clues, is checked against several witnesses, and can be tested, rather than just being a hunch.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A detective usually expects one final "whodunit" that a confession could settle. History rarely offers a confession or a single provable answer; the sources are always incomplete, so historians often live with more than one defensible interpretation and revise them as new evidence appears.

## Worked Example

Suppose you ask why the Western Roman Empire declined. You gather evidence: tax and coinage records, accounts of epidemics, reports of invasions, and Edward Gibbon's famous eighteenth-century argument (published across six volumes from 1776 to 1788) that the rise of Christianity sapped the empire's strength. Turning evidence into interpretation, you notice Gibbon leans heavily on one factor. You corroborate: do independent sources support a single-cause story? The Eastern Roman Empire was also Christian yet survived for centuries, which is strong counter-evidence against Christianity as the decisive cause. A stronger interpretation therefore weighs several corroborated factors together, fiscal strain, leadership failures, disease, and external pressure, and explains why the single-cause reading falls short. Notice you did not declare Gibbon simply "wrong opinion"; you tested his argument against evidence and found a better-supported one.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating every historical interpretation as "just opinion," so all views seem equally valid.
  **Correction:** Interpretations answer to the sources: they can be supported, tested, and compared. One that fits and corroborates the evidence is genuinely better than one that does not.
- **Mistake:** Confusing evidence with interpretation, for example calling a historian's conclusion a "fact" from the source.
  **Correction:** Separate what the source directly shows (evidence) from the argument built on it (interpretation). The date on a document is evidence; why it was written is interpretation.
- **Mistake:** Counting repeated copies of one account as multiple corroborating sources.
  **Correction:** Corroboration requires independent sources. Two outlets reprinting the same report are one source; agreement only strengthens a claim when the sources do not depend on each other.
- **Mistake:** Judging an interpretation only by the evidence that supports it and ignoring what cuts against it.
  **Correction:** A strong interpretation acknowledges counter-evidence and explains why it still holds. Ignoring inconvenient sources is a mark of a weak argument, not a strong one.
- **Mistake:** Assuming that because historians disagree, no interpretation can be better than another.
  **Correction:** Disagreement usually reflects incomplete evidence and different questions, not the absence of standards. Interpretations are still ranked by how well they fit and corroborate the evidence.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Evidence** vs. **Interpretation** — Evidence is what a source shows and can be re-checked in the source; interpretation is the argument explaining that evidence and goes beyond any single source.
- **Historical interpretation** vs. **Personal opinion** — An interpretation is supported by evidence and reasoning and can be tested against the sources; an opinion answers to nothing outside itself.
- **Corroborated claim** vs. **Single-source claim** — A corroborated claim is confirmed by multiple independent sources; a single-source claim rests on one account and is more vulnerable to that source's bias or error.

## Key Takeaway

Evidence is what the sources show; interpretation is the tested argument built on it. Interpretations are not mere opinions, because they must fit the evidence, corroborate across independent sources, and address counter-evidence, yet reasonable historians can still disagree when they weigh evidence differently or ask different questions.

## Practice Question Bank

1. In historical work, what is the difference between evidence and interpretation?
   - A. Evidence is older than interpretation; interpretation only applies to recent events.
   - B. Evidence is what the sources show; interpretation is the argument a historian builds to explain it.
   - C. Evidence is written; interpretation is always spoken or visual.
   - D. There is no real difference; the two words mean the same thing.

   **Answer: B.** Evidence is what a surviving source actually shows and can be re-checked in that source. Interpretation is the argument a historian constructs to explain the evidence. Age, medium, and "same thing" all misstate the distinction.

2. Why is a well-made historical interpretation more than just an opinion?
   - A. Because it is written by someone with an advanced degree.
   - B. Because it is the view held by the largest number of people.
   - C. Because it has been repeated in many textbooks over time.
   - D. Because it is supported by evidence and reasoning and can be tested against the sources.

   **Answer: D.** An interpretation answers to the sources: it rests on evidence and reasoning and can be checked by returning to the documents. Credentials, popularity, and repetition do not make an argument sound; testable support against evidence does.

3. A student finds four websites that all state the same claim, but discovers they all copied it from one original press release. For corroboration, how many independent sources does the student actually have?
   - A. One, because the four accounts all trace back to a single source.
   - B. Four, because the claim appears in four separate places.
   - C. Two, because copies always count as half of the originals.
   - D. Zero, because online sources never count as evidence.

   **Answer: A.** Corroboration requires independent sources. Four copies of one press release derive from the same origin, so they function as a single source. Counting them as four (or two) mistakes repetition for independent agreement, and online sources are not automatically worthless.

4. Two historians study the same surviving records about the decline of the Western Roman Empire but reach different explanations. What best accounts for this disagreement?
   - A. One of them must be inventing evidence, since honest work always yields identical conclusions.
   - B. History has no standards, so any explanation is as good as any other.
   - C. They can weigh the same incomplete evidence differently or ask different questions of it, while both still argue from the sources.
   - D. Whichever historian is more famous is automatically correct.

   **Answer: C.** Reasonable historians disagree because evidence is incomplete and can support more than one careful reading; they may weigh sources differently or ask different questions. That does not mean fabrication, an absence of standards, or that fame settles the matter.

5. You are comparing two interpretations of the same event. Which feature most clearly marks the stronger one?
   - A. It tells a more dramatic and memorable story.
   - B. It accounts for the evidence, corroborates across independent sources, and addresses the evidence that runs against it.
   - C. It relies confidently on a single vivid source and does not mention any others.
   - D. It reaches the most surprising conclusion possible.

   **Answer: B.** A stronger interpretation fits more of the relevant evidence, is checked against independent sources, and openly addresses counter-evidence. Drama, surprise, and confident reliance on one source are not measures of soundness; leaning on a single source is in fact a mark of weakness.
