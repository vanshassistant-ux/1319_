# Ancient India

## In 30 Seconds

Ancient India covers the subcontinent's first cities and empires. The Indus Valley (Harappan) civilization built planned, well-drained brick cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, with a mature urban phase around c. 2600-1900 BCE and a script still undeciphered. Later came the Vedic period and its Sanskrit hymns, the varna social order, and new traditions including Buddhism and Jainism. The Maurya Empire (Ashoka) and the Gupta "golden age" of mathematics followed.

## Why This Matters

The Indian subcontinent produced one of the world's earliest urban civilizations and, later, ideas still in daily use — most famously the decimal place-value number system you use to write any figure. Studying ancient India corrects a Eurocentric habit of treating South Asia as peripheral: its cities, empires, and mathematics rank among the ancient world's major achievements. The period is also a case study in working with limited evidence, since the Indus script cannot be read and many dates are approximate. Understanding this foundation makes later South Asian history, and the global spread of Buddhism and of Indian mathematics, far easier to follow.

## Learning Objectives

- Describe the Indus Valley (Harappan) civilization, including its cities, urban planning, and undeciphered script, and place its mature phase in time.
- Explain the Vedic period, the Vedas, and the four-part varna social order, described factually and neutrally.
- Name the religious and philosophical traditions that emerged in ancient India — the development of Hinduism and the origins of Buddhism and Jainism — with their founders and approximate dates.
- Summarize the Maurya Empire and Ashoka, and the Gupta Empire's golden age of mathematics, including the decimal place-value system and the treatment of zero.
- Distinguish the Maurya and Gupta empires and place ancient India's major periods in chronological order using "c." dating.

## The College Version

### The Indus Valley (Harappan) civilization

The earliest cities of the subcontinent belonged to the Indus Valley Civilization, also called the Harappan civilization after Harappa, one of its first excavated sites. It developed along the Indus River and its tributaries in what is now Pakistan and northwestern India. Authorities give somewhat different spans — OpenStax dates it broadly to c. 2800-1800 BCE, and UNESCO describes the metropolis of Mohenjo-daro as flourishing roughly c. 2500-1500 BCE — while its mature urban phase is most commonly placed at about c. 2600-1900 BCE. All agree it was a Bronze Age culture of remarkable scale. Its two largest cities, Harappa and Mohenjo-daro, may each have held tens of thousands of people. What impresses archaeologists most is the planning: streets laid out on a grid and meeting at right angles, standardized fired and mud brick, a raised walled citadel above a lower residential town, and — unusually for the ancient world — citywide covered drains, wells, soak pits for sewage, and even indoor toilets connected to the drainage network. UNESCO calls Mohenjo-daro the most ancient planned city on the subcontinent. The Harappans traded as far as Mesopotamia, whose records call their land "Meluhha" and mention goods such as lapis lazuli and carnelian. They also left a script of roughly four hundred signs on seals and other small objects that has never been deciphered, so we cannot read their own words. Around c. 1900-1800 BCE the cities declined; the causes are debated and probably combined — climate and river changes, environmental strain, and shifting trade rather than a single catastrophe.

### The Vedic period, the Vedas, and the varna order

After the Indus cities faded, the centuries often called the Vedic period take their name from the Vedas, a large body of sacred hymns and ritual texts composed in early Sanskrit. They were first preserved by careful oral transmission across generations and only written down much later. The Rigveda is the oldest layer, generally dated to roughly the second half of the second millennium BCE, with the wider tradition continuing for centuries afterward. How Sanskrit-speaking, or "Indo-Aryan," culture arose in the subcontinent — through migration, exchange, or a mix — is genuinely debated among scholars, and this lesson does not settle it. Vedic society came to be described in terms of varna, a ranked scheme of four broad categories: Brahmins (priests and ritual specialists), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants, artisans, and farmers), and Shudras (laborers and servants). Some groups, later called "untouchables" or Dalits, were placed outside the four varnas altogether and subjected to severe social exclusion. It helps to distinguish the idealized varna categories of the texts from jati, the many local birth-groups that structured actual daily life, and from the far more complex system that developed over later centuries. This lesson describes the framework factually; the long history of caste, its documented harms, and its modern reforms and legal prohibitions of discrimination belong to later topics.

### New religious and philosophical traditions

The middle of the first millennium BCE was a time of intense religious questioning in the Ganges plain, and two traditions that began then still have tens of millions of followers. Buddhism traces to Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha ("awakened one"), traditionally dated c. 563-483 BCE, who taught a path out of suffering — the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path — as an alternative to Brahmanical ritual. Jainism looks to Mahavira, traditionally c. 599-527 BCE (some scholars prefer c. 539-467 BCE), remembered not as a founder but as the twenty-fourth and latest Tirthankara, or "ford-maker," of the current age; Jain teaching centers on ahimsa, non-violence toward all living things. Mahavira and the Buddha were near contemporaries. Meanwhile the Vedic tradition itself kept developing into the family of practices later grouped as Hinduism. This topic names these traditions, their founders, and their approximate dates; their beliefs and practices are treated in depth in the major world religions topic. What matters here is that ancient India was a seedbed of religious and philosophical ideas that spread far beyond the subcontinent — Buddhism in particular later reached much of Asia.

### The Maurya Empire and Ashoka

The first empire to unite most of the subcontinent was the Maurya Empire, founded by Chandragupta Maurya around c. 321 BCE with its capital at Pataliputra. Under his successors it grew to cover nearly all of present-day India, Pakistan, and Bangladesh. Its most famous ruler was Ashoka, who reigned c. 268-232 BCE. Early in his reign Ashoka waged a brutal war to conquer Kalinga, on the eastern coast, around c. 260 BCE; by his own account it killed roughly 100,000 people and displaced far more. Ashoka recorded profound remorse and turned toward Buddhist ethics, promoting dhamma — a public morality of non-violence, tolerance, and welfare. He is best known for the Edicts of Ashoka, messages carved on rocks and polished stone pillars across the empire; they are among the earliest deciphered writing from India and a rare case in which a ruler's own words survive. Through his patronage Buddhism spread widely, including missions abroad. The Maurya Empire weakened after Ashoka and finally collapsed around c. 185 BCE. Ashoka also illustrates reading a source in context: his edicts are both genuine evidence of his policies and a piece of royal self-presentation, so historians weigh them critically rather than taking them purely at face value.

### The Gupta Empire and a golden age of science

Centuries later the Gupta Empire (c. 320-550 CE), founded by a different ruler named Chandragupta — Chandragupta I (reigned c. 320-335 CE), not to be confused with the earlier Chandragupta Maurya — presided over a period often called a classical "golden age" of northern India. It saw flourishing art, literature, and especially mathematics and astronomy. Gupta-era mathematicians used a decimal, base-ten place-value system, in which a digit's value depends on its position — the same principle behind modern numerals. Aryabhata (c. 476-550 CE), working in this tradition, wrote the Aryabhatiya (c. 499 CE), used place-value calculation, gave a strikingly accurate value for pi, and argued that the Earth rotates on its axis. The full treatment of zero as a number — rules such as a number minus itself equals zero, and how zero behaves in arithmetic — appears a little later, in Brahmagupta's Brahmasphutasiddhanta of 628 CE, just after the Gupta imperial peak. This place-value system, with its zero, was later transmitted to the Islamic world and from there to Europe, becoming the numerals used worldwide today. Crediting these advances to "the Gupta golden age" is a useful shorthand, but the more precise story spans the Gupta and immediately post-Gupta centuries and several named mathematicians.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Indus Valley (Harappan) Civilization** — A Bronze Age urban culture of the Indus River basin (in today's Pakistan and northwestern India) whose mature phase ran roughly c. 2600-1900 BCE, known for planned brick cities and advanced drainage.
- **Mohenjo-daro** — One of the two largest Indus Valley cities (with Harappa), noted for its grid streets, citadel, and citywide drainage; a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1980.
- **Indus script** — The writing system of the Indus Valley Civilization, found on seals and small objects with about four hundred signs; it remains undeciphered.
- **Vedas** — A large body of sacred hymns and ritual texts composed in early Sanskrit and long transmitted orally; the Rigveda is the oldest layer.
- **Varna** — The idealized four-part ranking of Vedic society into Brahmins (priests), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (laborers and servants), with some groups placed outside it.
- **Maurya Empire** — The first empire to unite most of the subcontinent, founded by Chandragupta Maurya c. 321 BCE and lasting until c. 185 BCE; its best-known ruler was Ashoka.
- **Edicts of Ashoka** — Moral and administrative messages that the Maurya emperor Ashoka (r. c. 268-232 BCE) had carved on rocks and stone pillars across his empire, promoting dhamma.
- **Gupta Empire** — A north Indian empire (c. 320-550 CE) often described as a classical "golden age" of art, literature, mathematics, and astronomy.
- **Decimal place-value system** — A base-ten way of writing numbers in which a digit's value depends on its position; developed by Indian mathematicians and, with zero, later spread to the Islamic world and Europe.

## Eli-10

Think of ancient India as four big chapters. First, thousands of years ago, people along the Indus River built neat cities with straight streets and covered pipes to carry away dirty water — so clever that we still admire the plumbing, even though we cannot read their writing. Next came the Vedic time, when priests memorized long sacred songs called the Vedas and society was sorted into ranked groups called varnas. Then new ideas appeared, including Buddhism (from the Buddha) and Jainism (from Mahavira). After that, big empires rose: the Maurya Empire, whose emperor Ashoka carved messages about kindness onto rocks, and later the Gupta Empire, when mathematicians worked out the number system — the ones, tens, hundreds, and the zero — that the whole world writes with today.

## Eli's Analogy

The way we write numbers is like a set of parking spots in a row. The spot a digit sits in tells you how much it is worth — ones, tens, hundreds — and zero is the sign that says "this spot is empty, but keep counting." Indian mathematicians built exactly that parking system, and it later traveled everywhere.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** It is only about the number system, not all of ancient India, and it makes the discovery sound like one neat invention. In reality the place-value idea and the rules for zero came together gradually over the Gupta and following centuries, through several mathematicians such as Aryabhata and Brahmagupta, not in a single moment.

## Worked Example

Why does the decimal place-value system matter, and why is zero part of it? Write "two hundred five." With place value, each position means a power of ten: hundreds, tens, ones. You need something to show that the tens spot is empty, or 205 collapses into 25 and changes value entirely. That placeholder is zero. Gupta-era mathematicians such as Aryabhata (c. 476-550 CE) already calculated with a base-ten place-value system, and Brahmagupta, in 628 CE, wrote down explicit rules for zero as a number — for example, that a number minus itself is zero. Those two ideas together let anyone write any whole number with just ten symbols, the system the world uses now.

## Common Mistakes

- **Confusing Chandragupta Maurya, founder of the Maurya Empire (c. 321 BCE), with Chandragupta I of the Gupta dynasty (r. c. 320-335 CE).** They are different people separated by roughly 600 years and two different empires. The similar name is a coincidence; check the empire and the era (BCE vs CE).
- **Saying the Indus script has been "translated," or claiming we know what the Harappans called themselves.** The script is undeciphered. We describe the civilization from its archaeology — cities, drains, seals, trade goods — not from readable texts in its own language.
- **Crediting the "invention of zero" entirely to the Gupta emperors or to a single date.** Gupta-era mathematicians used a decimal place-value system, but the first known rules for zero as a number come from Brahmagupta in 628 CE, just after the Gupta peak. The advance was gradual and involved several mathematicians.
- **Treating the four varnas as identical to the full later caste system, or as fixed economic classes.** Varna is the idealized four-part scheme of the texts; actual society ran on many local birth-groups (jati), and the elaborate caste system developed over later centuries. Describe the framework factually and keep the later history separate.
- **Assuming the Buddha founded Hinduism, or mixing up the Buddha and Mahavira.** Hinduism grew out of the older Vedic tradition. The Buddha (Siddhartha Gautama) founded Buddhism; Mahavira is the central figure of Jainism. They were near contemporaries in the mid-first millennium BCE.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Maurya Empire (c. 321-185 BCE) vs. Gupta Empire (c. 320-550 CE):** The Maurya was the earlier, larger empire, famous for Ashoka and his edicts; the Gupta came roughly six centuries later and is remembered as a classical "golden age" of literature, art, and mathematics.
- **Indus script vs. Mesopotamian cuneiform:** Both are ancient writing systems, but cuneiform has been deciphered and can be read, while the Indus script remains undeciphered, so the Harappans left no readable words of their own.
- **Varna vs. jati:** Varna is the idealized four-category ranking described in the texts; jati refers to the many local birth-groups that actually organized daily life. The two are related but not the same, and neither is the whole later caste system.

## Key Takeaway

Ancient India runs from the planned, well-drained cities of the Indus Valley (mature phase c. 2600-1900 BCE), through the Vedic period and the rise of Buddhism and Jainism, to the Maurya Empire of Ashoka and the Gupta golden age that refined the decimal place-value number system the world still uses.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. The mature Indus Valley cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro are especially noted by archaeologists for which feature?** *(recall)*

- A. Massive stone pyramids built as royal tombs
- B. Planned street grids with standardized brick and citywide covered drainage
- C. A large standing navy recorded in their inscriptions
- D. Temples covered in fully deciphered royal hymns

**Answer: B.** The Indus cities are famous for orderly grid streets, standardized brick, and unusually advanced covered drains and sanitation. Pyramid tombs (A) are Egyptian, not Harappan; there are no inscriptions describing a navy (C) because the script is undeciphered; and (D) is impossible for the same reason — no Indus writing can be read.

**2. Which statement about the writing system of the Indus Valley Civilization is accurate?** *(recall)*

- A. It survives on seals and small objects but has never been deciphered
- B. It was deciphered in the nineteenth century as an early form of Sanskrit
- C. It was identical to Mesopotamian cuneiform and read the same way
- D. No evidence of any Indus writing has ever been found

**Answer: A.** The Indus script appears on seals and other small objects, has about four hundred signs, and remains undeciphered. It has not been read as Sanskrit or anything else (B), it is a distinct system rather than cuneiform (C), and physical evidence of the writing clearly exists (D).

**3. An emperor conquers a coastal kingdom in a war that kills and displaces enormous numbers of people, then expresses deep remorse, embraces Buddhist ethics, and has moral messages carved on rocks and pillars throughout his realm. Which ruler does this describe?** *(application)*

- A. Chandragupta I of the Gupta dynasty
- B. Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha
- C. Ashoka of the Maurya Empire
- D. Mahavira, the Jain teacher

**Answer: C.** This is Ashoka, whose war on Kalinga (c. 260 BCE) preceded his turn to Buddhist dhamma and his famous edicts carved on rock and stone pillars. Chandragupta I (A) was a Gupta founder centuries later; the Buddha (B) and Mahavira (D) were religious teachers, not conquering emperors.

**4. A student writes that Gupta-era India produced a fully worked-out set of rules for zero as a number. What is the most accurate correction?** *(analysis)*

- A. Zero as a number was a Mesopotamian invention with no Indian role at all
- B. Indian mathematicians never used a decimal place-value system
- C. The decimal system existed in India, but zero was never treated mathematically there
- D. Gupta-era mathematicians such as Aryabhata used a decimal place-value system, but the first known rules for zero as a number appear slightly later, with Brahmagupta in 628 CE

**Answer: D.** The place-value system was in use in the Gupta era (Aryabhata, c. 499 CE), but explicit rules for zero as a number come a little later, in Brahmagupta's work of 628 CE, just after the Gupta peak. Option A denies the well-documented Indian contribution; B and C are false, since Indian mathematicians both used place value and treated zero mathematically.

**5. Which statement most accurately describes the varna social structure of early India?** *(understanding)*

- A. It was a two-tier split between rulers and everyone else, with no separate priestly group
- B. It ranked society into four broad categories — Brahmins (priests), Kshatriyas (warriors and rulers), Vaishyas (merchants and farmers), and Shudras (laborers and servants) — with some groups placed outside it entirely
- C. It was purely an economic tax bracket that people freely changed each year
- D. It was invented by the Gupta emperors in the fourth century CE

**Answer: B.** Varna is the idealized four-part ranking of Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras, with groups later called "untouchables" or Dalits placed outside the four. It was not a two-tier system (A), not a freely chosen tax bracket (C), and long predates the Gupta emperors (D); it appears in the much earlier Vedic tradition.
