# The United States in World War I

## In 30 Seconds

The United States stayed neutral when World War I began in 1914, but German submarine attacks and the Zimmermann Telegram pushed it in, and Congress declared war in April 1917. America drafted millions, remade its economy, cracked down on dissent, and sent troops who helped win the war in 1918. President Wilson then pushed his Fourteen Points and a League of Nations, but the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, and the country turned back toward isolationism.

## Why This Matters

World War I was the moment the United States first threw its full weight into a European war and briefly tried to lead a new world order. How it entered, what it did at home, and why it retreated afterward set patterns that echoed for a century. The wartime crackdown on dissent tested the limits of free speech; the Great Migration reshaped American cities and the struggle for civil rights; and the Senate's rejection of the League of Nations helped push the country into the isolationism it would not fully abandon until World War II. Studying this period shows how a democracy mobilizes for total war and how it argues over its role in the world.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain why the United States began the war neutral and identify the specific events that eroded that neutrality.
- Describe the Zimmermann Telegram and the German submarine policy that led to the U.S. declaration of war in April 1917.
- Analyze how the United States mobilized for war at home, including the draft, war production, propaganda, and the suppression of dissent.
- Explain the significance of the Great Migration and the changing wartime workforce.
- Evaluate Wilson's Fourteen Points and explain why the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles and the League of Nations.

## The College Version

### Neutrality and its erosion, 1914-1917

When war engulfed Europe in the summer of 1914, most Americans wanted no part of it. President Woodrow Wilson proclaimed U.S. neutrality and urged citizens to be neutral in thought as well as in deed, and for nearly three years the country stayed officially out of the fighting that is covered in the companion topic on the global war. Neutrality proved hard to sustain, however, because American money and goods did not stay neutral. Trade with the Allies — Britain and France — surged while a British naval blockade choked off commerce with Germany, and U.S. banks extended large loans to the Allied governments. The nation's prosperity became tied to an Allied victory. Neutrality frayed fastest at sea. Germany used submarines, or U-boats, to attack shipping bound for Britain, and on 7 May 1915 a U-boat sank the British liner RMS Lusitania, killing nearly 1,200 people, including 128 Americans. Public outrage forced Germany to promise restraint, but the promise did not hold. Wilson won reelection in 1916 partly on the slogan that he had kept the country out of war, yet the underlying pressures were still building.

### The decision for war: submarines and the Zimmermann Telegram

Two events in early 1917 ended American neutrality. First, Germany announced that it would resume unrestricted submarine warfare, meaning its U-boats would sink any ship, including neutral American vessels, approaching the British Isles. German commanders gambled that they could starve Britain into surrender before the United States could make its power felt. Second came the Zimmermann Telegram. In January 1917, German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann sent a coded message to the German minister in Mexico proposing that, if the United States entered the war, Mexico ally with Germany; in return, Germany would help Mexico recover territory it had lost to the United States in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona. British intelligence intercepted and decoded the telegram and passed it to Washington, and its publication on 1 March 1917 stunned and enraged the American public. With U-boats already sinking American ships, Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war, framing it as a war to make the world safe for democracy. Congress declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917.

### Mobilizing a nation: the draft, war production, and financing

The United States was not ready for a modern war; its army was small and its economy geared for peace. Mobilization changed that within months. The Selective Service Act, signed on 18 May 1917, created a national draft, and roughly 24 million men eventually registered as the army swelled from a few hundred thousand to millions. To run a wartime economy, the government created powerful new agencies. The War Industries Board, led by financier Bernard Baruch, coordinated raw materials and production; the Food Administration, under future president Herbert Hoover, boosted output and promoted voluntary rationing with campaigns like "Meatless Mondays"; and other boards took control of fuel and the railroads. The government paid for the enormous effort largely by selling Liberty Bonds to ordinary citizens, turning war finance into a patriotic ritual. This was mobilization on a scale Americans had never seen, and it dramatically expanded the reach of the federal government into the economy and daily life.

### The home front: propaganda, dissent, and the Great Migration

Winning support for the war became its own campaign. The Committee on Public Information, headed by journalist George Creel, flooded the country with posters, pamphlets, films, and speakers to rally patriotism and demonize the enemy. Enthusiasm shaded into repression. The Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918 made it a crime to interfere with the draft or to speak against the government or the war effort; roughly 2,000 people were prosecuted, and the Socialist leader Eugene V. Debs was sentenced to ten years in prison for an anti-war speech. The wartime economy also set people in motion. As factories boomed and immigration from Europe dried up, hundreds of thousands of Black Americans left the rural South for jobs in Northern industrial cities, beginning the first great wave of the Great Migration and reshaping American society and politics. More than a million women entered the paid workforce, many in industrial jobs, and the momentum of women's wartime contribution helped carry the Nineteenth Amendment, guaranteeing women the vote, to ratification in 1920.

### Americans in the fight, 1918

American soldiers, nicknamed "doughboys," reached Europe slowly at first but arrived in force during 1918, at the moment the Allies most needed fresh troops. General John J. Pershing commanded the American Expeditionary Forces and insisted, over Allied objections, that they fight as a distinct American army rather than being parceled out to French and British units. U.S. forces helped blunt Germany's last great offensive in the spring and summer of 1918, at engagements such as Belleau Wood in June and the Second Battle of the Marne in July, and then took part in the massive Meuse-Argonne Offensive from late September until the war's end. About 116,000 American servicemembers died — roughly half in combat and half from disease, above all the 1918 influenza pandemic that swept through crowded camps — and around 200,000 were wounded. Exhausted and running out of options, Germany agreed to an armistice that took effect on 11 November 1918, ending the fighting. The American contribution came late but at a decisive moment, adding manpower and industrial weight that the Allies could not have matched alone.

### Wilson's peace and the Senate's rejection

Even before the war ended, Wilson set out a vision for the peace. In his Fourteen Points speech to Congress on 8 January 1918, he called for open diplomacy, freedom of the seas, reduced armaments, self-determination for peoples, and — as his centerpiece — a League of Nations to settle disputes and prevent future wars. At the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 Wilson won inclusion of the League Covenant in the Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919, but he had to compromise on most of his other points. Then came the hardest fight, at home. Under the Constitution the Senate must approve treaties, and Republican leader Henry Cabot Lodge and a bloc of "reservationists" objected especially to Article X of the League Covenant, which they feared could commit the United States to foreign wars without a vote of Congress. Wilson refused to accept Lodge's reservations and, after collapsing during a national speaking tour in the fall of 1919, would not compromise. The Senate rejected the treaty on 19 November 1919 and again on 19 March 1920. The United States never joined the League of Nations and turned back toward isolationism in the 1920s.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Neutrality:** A nation's official policy of not taking sides in a war; the United States held to it from 1914 until it declared war in April 1917.
- **Unrestricted submarine warfare:** Germany's policy of using U-boats to sink any ship, including neutral and civilian vessels, in the waters around Britain without warning.
- **Zimmermann Telegram:** A January 1917 coded German message proposing a Mexican alliance and the return of U.S. territory to Mexico, intercepted by Britain and used to rally American opinion for war.
- **Selective Service Act:** The 18 May 1917 law establishing a national military draft, under which about 24 million American men eventually registered.
- **Committee on Public Information:** The U.S. government agency under George Creel that ran mass propaganda to build support for the war.
- **Espionage and Sedition Acts:** Wartime laws (1917 and 1918) that criminalized interference with the draft and criticism of the government or war effort, used to prosecute dissenters.
- **Great Migration:** The large movement of Black Americans from the rural South to Northern cities, whose first major wave was driven by wartime industrial jobs and the effort to escape Jim Crow.
- **American Expeditionary Forces (AEF):** The U.S. army sent to Europe in 1917-1918 under General John J. Pershing, kept as a distinct American command.
- **Fourteen Points:** Wilson's 8 January 1918 program for a lasting peace, including self-determination, freedom of the seas, and a League of Nations.
- **League of Nations:** The international organization proposed by Wilson to settle disputes and prevent war; the U.S. Senate refused to join it.

## Eli-10

For almost three years the United States watched the war in Europe from the sidelines and sold supplies to both the winners it liked and stayed out of the fight. But German submarines kept sinking ships with Americans on board, and a secret German letter tried to get Mexico to attack the U.S. That was too much, so in 1917 America joined the war. At home, the country drafted soldiers, built weapons, sold war bonds, and even jailed people who spoke against the war. American troops arrived in 1918 and helped end it. Afterward the president wanted to start a club of nations to keep peace, but the Senate said no, so the country pulled back from the world again.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a neighbor who refuses to get involved in a street fight but keeps selling water to one side. When the other side starts throwing rocks through his own windows and tries to recruit his little brother against him, he finally jumps in, helps end the brawl, then proposes a neighborhood council to stop future fights.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy shrinks a war that killed millions into a schoolyard scuffle and leaves out the human cost, the crackdown on free speech at home, and the huge changes the war brought to American cities and workers. It also makes joining sound like a single clear moment, when it built up over years of pressure.

## Worked Example

Trace how the United States went from neutral to at war in just over two months in 1917, and notice how causes stacked up. In late January 1917 Germany announces it will resume unrestricted submarine warfare, so American merchant ships are again at risk. On 1 March the Zimmermann Telegram is published, revealing Germany's offer to help Mexico take back Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona; public anger spikes. Through March, U-boats sink several American ships with loss of life. On 2 April Wilson asks Congress to declare war "to make the world safe for democracy," and on 6 April Congress does. Now the historical thinking: no single event forced the decision. The submarine policy threatened American lives and commerce, the telegram made Germany look like a direct danger to U.S. soil, and years of economic ties to the Allies had already tilted the country. The trigger mattered because the underlying conditions were in place.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking the United States fought in World War I from the start in 1914.** The U.S. was neutral for nearly three years and did not declare war until 6 April 1917, more than halfway through the conflict.
- **Believing the sinking of the Lusitania (1915) directly caused the U.S. to enter the war.** The Lusitania outraged Americans but did not bring war; the U.S. entered in 1917 after Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram surfaced.
- **Assuming the Zimmermann Telegram was a German attack plan already underway.** It was a secret diplomatic proposal to Mexico, intercepted by British intelligence before it could lead to anything; its power was in swaying American opinion once published.
- **Thinking the United States joined the League of Nations because Wilson championed it.** Wilson pushed hard for the League, but the U.S. Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles in 1919 and 1920, so the United States never joined.
- **Assuming the wartime government protected free speech and civil liberties.** The Espionage and Sedition Acts criminalized anti-war speech; about 2,000 people were prosecuted, and Eugene Debs was imprisoned for a speech.

## Compare / Contrast

- **The sinking of the Lusitania (1915) vs. The resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram (1917):** The Lusitania inflamed opinion but Wilson kept the country neutral; the 1917 events came together to actually trigger the U.S. declaration of war.
- **The Fourteen Points vs. The Treaty of Versailles:** The Fourteen Points were Wilson's idealistic peace program; the Treaty of Versailles was the negotiated settlement that kept his League but compromised most of his other aims.
- **The global topic on World War I vs. This topic on the United States in World War I:** The global topic covers the war's causes, trenches, and overall course; this one focuses on American neutrality, entry, the home front, the 1918 contribution, and the peace debate.

## Key Takeaway

The United States entered World War I only in April 1917, after German submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram broke its neutrality; it mobilized society for total war, helped win the fight in 1918, and then rejected Wilson's League of Nations and retreated toward isolationism.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What was the Zimmermann Telegram?**
   - A. A German ultimatum demanding that the United States stop trading with Britain
   - B. A secret German proposal that Mexico ally with Germany in exchange for the return of U.S. territory
   - C. A message in which Wilson asked Congress to declare war on Germany
   - D. A British warning to the United States about German submarine locations

   **Answer: B.** The Zimmermann Telegram was a January 1917 coded message from German Foreign Secretary Arthur Zimmermann proposing that Mexico ally with Germany and, in return, recover territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona; British intelligence intercepted it, and its publication helped push the U.S. toward war. It was not an ultimatum about trade, not Wilson's war message, and not a British warning about submarines.

2. **Which German policy, resumed in early 1917, most directly threatened American lives and shipping and helped bring the United States into the war?**
   - A. Unrestricted submarine warfare
   - B. A naval blockade of American ports
   - C. Aerial bombing of American cities
   - D. An arms embargo against the United States

   **Answer: A.** In early 1917 Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare, meaning its U-boats would sink any ship near Britain, including neutral American vessels, which directly threatened U.S. lives and commerce. Germany did not blockade American ports, did not bomb American cities from the air, and did not impose an arms embargo on the United States.

3. **During the war, a Socialist leader gives a public speech urging young men to resist the draft and is sentenced to ten years in prison. Which laws made this prosecution possible?**
   - A. The Selective Service Act and the Liberty Loan Act
   - B. The Fourteen Points and the League Covenant
   - C. The Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918
   - D. The Nineteenth Amendment and the Food Administration rules

   **Answer: C.** The Espionage Act of 1917 and the Sedition Act of 1918 criminalized interfering with the draft and criticizing the government or war effort; under them roughly 2,000 people were prosecuted, and Eugene V. Debs received a ten-year sentence for an anti-war speech. The Selective Service and Liberty Loan Acts organized the draft and war finance, the Fourteen Points were a peace program, and the Nineteenth Amendment concerned voting rights.

4. **Why did the U.S. Senate ultimately reject the Treaty of Versailles and keep the United States out of the League of Nations?**
   - A. The treaty required the United States to pay reparations to Germany
   - B. Wilson opposed the League and asked the Senate to vote it down
   - C. The treaty had already expired before the Senate could vote on it
   - D. Reservationists led by Henry Cabot Lodge objected to Article X, and Wilson refused to compromise

   **Answer: D.** Republican leader Henry Cabot Lodge and the reservationists objected especially to Article X of the League Covenant, fearing it could commit the United States to foreign wars without congressional consent; Wilson refused to accept their reservations, and the Senate rejected the treaty in November 1919 and March 1920. The treaty did not require the U.S. to pay Germany, Wilson championed rather than opposed the League, and the treaty had not expired.

5. **The wartime demand for factory labor, combined with the desire to escape the Jim Crow South, drove which major demographic change in the United States?**
   - A. The return of large numbers of Americans to farming
   - B. The migration of hundreds of thousands of Black Americans to Northern cities
   - C. The mass immigration of new workers from Germany and Austria-Hungary
   - D. The movement of most industry from the North to the rural South

   **Answer: B.** Booming war industry and the halt in European immigration opened Northern factory jobs, and hundreds of thousands of Black Americans left the rural South for those cities in the first great wave of the Great Migration, also fleeing Jim Crow. Farming did not surge, wartime immigration from enemy nations did not occur, and industry remained concentrated in the North.
