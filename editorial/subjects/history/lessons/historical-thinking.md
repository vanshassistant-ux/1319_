# Historical Thinking

## In 30 Seconds

Historical thinking is what it means to reason like a historian instead of memorizing dates. You treat every account as something a person made for a reason, so you ask who wrote it, when, and why (sourcing), read it in its own time and place (contextualization), and check it against other sources (corroboration). The big idea: history is an evidence-based interpretation you can argue for, not a fixed list of facts to recite.

## Why This Matters

Almost every college course asks you to build a claim from imperfect evidence, and history is where that habit is taught directly. Learning to source, contextualize, and corroborate turns you from a passive receiver of facts into someone who can weigh conflicting accounts and defend a conclusion. These are also everyday survival skills: the same moves that test a Civil War letter test a viral post, a political ad, or a news story of uncertain origin. Employers and citizenship alike reward people who can ask where a claim comes from, judge whether it is trustworthy, and change their minds when better evidence appears.

## Learning Objectives

- Define historical thinking and distinguish it from memorizing dates and facts
- Explain the historical-reading skills of sourcing, contextualization, corroboration, and close reading, and attribute them to the Stanford History Education Group
- Distinguish established historical facts from interpretation, and explain why disciplined accounts can still differ
- Apply sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration to a specific document or scenario
- Analyze why comparing multiple sources produces a more trustworthy account than relying on one

## The College Version

### From memorizing the past to investigating it

Most people leave school history thinking the subject is a settled list of names, dates, and battles to be recalled on a test. Working historians experience it very differently. To them the past is not directly visible; it survives only in traces — letters, laws, photographs, ledgers, oral testimony, ruins — each made by a particular person or institution for a particular purpose. Historical thinking is the disciplined set of habits for turning those traces into a defensible account of what happened and what it meant. The cognitive scientist Sam Wineburg, who founded the Stanford History Education Group in 2002, put the gap bluntly: historians critically analyze documents and probe the motives of the people who made them, while students of history are too often "relegated to searching for facts." Closing that gap is the whole point of this lesson. Historical thinking does not throw out dates — you still need to know that the U.S. Constitution was drafted in 1787 — but it treats a date as the beginning of a question, not the end of one.

### The core moves: sourcing, contextualization, corroboration

The Stanford History Education Group's "Reading Like a Historian" curriculum organizes historical reading around a small set of skills, and three of them do most of the work. **Sourcing** means looking at a document's origin before its content: who created this, when, for whom, and why? A general's after-action report, a soldier's private diary, and a newspaper's front page can describe the same battle very differently, and knowing the author's position and purpose tells you how to weigh each. **Contextualization** means reading a source inside its own time and place rather than through present-day assumptions — recognizing that a word, a law, or a silence meant something specific in, say, 1850 that it does not mean now. **Corroboration** means never resting a conclusion on a single account: you compare sources, note where they agree, and pay special attention to where they diverge, because the disagreements often reveal bias, error, or a perspective you had missed. A fourth skill, **close reading**, runs underneath all three — attending carefully to a source's exact claims, word choices, and the evidence it offers for them.

### History as interpretation, not a fixed list of facts

The deepest idea in historical thinking is that history is interpretation built from evidence. That does not mean "anything goes." Historians separate established facts — the Battle of Hastings occurred in 1066; six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust — from interpretation, which asks why events happened, how they connected, and what they meant. On questions of interpretation, well-trained historians using the same documents can reach different, defensible conclusions, because they weigh causes differently or ask different questions. This is why a history essay makes an argument and marshals evidence rather than simply listing what happened. It also explains why historical accounts change over time: new sources surface, and later generations ask questions earlier ones ignored — recovering, for example, the experiences of enslaved, colonized, and indigenous people who were written out of older "master narratives." A claim is stronger not because an authority asserts it but because the evidence supports it and it survives comparison with rival accounts.

### Turning the moves into a routine

Historical thinking becomes practical when you make it a repeatable routine. The Library of Congress teaches a simple three-step version for any primary source: Observe (collect what you actually notice), Reflect (connect it to what you already know), and Question (decide what you still need to find out). The National Archives teaches a parallel progression — meet the document, observe its parts, make sense of it, then use it as historical evidence — and stresses that the steps stay the same for a photograph, a map, a cartoon, or a treaty. The value of a fixed routine is that it slows you down at exactly the moment you are tempted to accept a striking source at face value. In practice, working a source means asking of it: Who made this and why? What was going on when it was made? What does it actually claim, and in what words? And what do other sources say about the same thing? Answer those, and you are no longer memorizing history — you are doing it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Historical thinking** — The disciplined practice of reasoning about the past the way historians do — asking questions and building evidence-based interpretations from surviving sources — rather than memorizing facts.
- **Sourcing** — Considering a document's origin (who made it, when, for whom, and why) and judging its trustworthiness before relying on what it says.
- **Contextualization** — Understanding a source within the time, place, and circumstances in which it was created, rather than through present-day assumptions.
- **Corroboration** — Comparing a source against other sources to establish what is probable and to notice where accounts of the same event agree or diverge.
- **Close reading** — Careful attention to a source's exact claims, word choices, and the evidence and reasoning it offers to support them.
- **Primary source** — A record created by a participant or witness at the time under study, such as a letter, photograph, or official document.
- **Interpretation** — An evidence-based explanation of why past events happened and what they meant, which can reasonably differ among historians.
- **Fact vs. interpretation** — The distinction between what can be established as having happened (and when) and the arguable question of why it happened and what it signified.
- **Presentism** — Judging or reading the past solely by present-day standards and assumptions, which distorts what a source meant in its own time.

## Eli-10

Imagine three friends each tell you why they were late to a party. You do not just believe the first one — you notice who is talking and why they might shade the story, you remember what was going on that day, and you compare all three to figure out what probably really happened. Historians do exactly that with letters, photos, and records left from long ago. They ask who made something and why, picture the time it came from, and check it against other clues. History is less like a list of facts to memorize and more like being a detective who has to explain the past and show the evidence.

## Eli's Analogy

Doing history is like being a detective at a crime scene where every clue was left behind by someone who had their own reasons. You cannot just take one witness's word; you ask who each witness is, what they could see, and whether their stories line up.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A detective usually expects one true solution to convict a suspect, while historians accept that thoughtful people can reach different, defensible interpretations of the same evidence — and there is no confession or courtroom verdict to settle it for good.

## Worked Example

Suppose you are handed a printed 1850s advertisement offering a reward for the return of a person who escaped slavery. A memorizer notes the date and moves on. A historical thinker sources it first: who paid for this notice, and why? The enslaver, to reclaim someone as property and to warn others — so the ad is evidence of the slaveholder's aims, not a neutral report. Contextualization places it under the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, which legally compelled such recaptures, explaining why the notice could be printed openly. Close reading of its wording and the details it lists reveals what the enslaver knew and valued. Finally, corroboration sets it beside a very different source — a formerly enslaved person's narrative — to reconstruct the same system from the other side. One document, four moves, and a far richer, better-supported account than any date alone could give.

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing historical thinking means abandoning dates and facts.** You still need accurate facts and chronology; historical thinking uses them as the starting point for questions and interpretation, not as the finish line.
- **Treating a source as a neutral window onto the past.** Every source was made by someone for a purpose. Sourcing — asking who made it and why — comes before trusting what it says.
- **Concluding that because history is interpretation, all opinions about the past are equally valid.** Interpretations still have to fit the evidence and survive comparison with other sources; a claim contradicted by the record is simply weaker or wrong.
- **Judging a source only by today's assumptions (presentism).** Contextualization asks what a word, law, or action meant in its own time before you evaluate it, which is different from excusing injustice.
- **Trusting a single dramatic source because it feels convincing.** Corroboration requires checking it against other accounts; the places where sources disagree are often the most revealing.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Memorizing history vs. Historical thinking** — Memorizing treats the past as a fixed list of facts to recall; historical thinking treats it as an interpretation you build and defend from evidence.
- **Fact vs. Interpretation** — A fact is what can be established as having happened and when; an interpretation is the arguable claim about why it happened and what it meant.
- **Sourcing vs. Corroboration** — Sourcing evaluates one document by its origin and purpose; corroboration compares several documents against one another to test what is probable.

## Key Takeaway

Historical thinking means investigating the past like a historian — sourcing, contextualizing, and corroborating evidence to build an interpretation you can argue for — rather than memorizing a fixed list of dates and facts.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best captures what "historical thinking" means?**
   - A. Memorizing the correct dates and names so they can be recalled on a test
   - B. Reasoning like a historian by asking questions and building evidence-based interpretations of the past
   - C. Accepting the account given by the oldest surviving source as the most reliable
   - D. Deciding that because the past is gone, no claim about it can be judged better than another

   **Answer: B.** Historical thinking is the disciplined practice of interpreting evidence the way historians do. Memorizing dates (A) is the very thing it contrasts with; age alone does not make a source reliable (C); and although history is interpretive, claims are still judged against evidence, so "anything goes" (D) is wrong.

2. **The framework of sourcing, contextualization, and corroboration is most associated with which organization?**
   - A. The Stanford History Education Group ("Reading Like a Historian")
   - B. The United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO)
   - C. The Federal Reserve Board of Governors
   - D. The American Bar Association

   **Answer: A.** These historical-reading skills were popularized by the Stanford History Education Group's "Reading Like a Historian" curriculum, built on Sam Wineburg's research. UNESCO (B) works on heritage and education broadly but did not originate this framework, and the Federal Reserve (C) and American Bar Association (D) are unrelated to it.

3. **Before reading a Civil War letter's contents, a student first asks, "Who wrote this, when, and for what purpose?" Which historical-thinking skill is the student using?**
   - A. Corroboration
   - B. Contextualization
   - C. Sourcing
   - D. Continuity and change over time

   **Answer: C.** Asking who made a document, when, and why — before trusting its content — is sourcing. Corroboration (A) compares multiple sources; contextualization (B) situates a source in its era; and continuity and change over time (D) is a separate skill about patterns across periods.

4. **A historian reads a general's report, a private soldier's diary, and a newspaper article about the same battle, then notes where the three accounts agree and where they conflict. This best illustrates which skill?**
   - A. Close reading of a single document's word choices
   - B. Presentism
   - C. Sourcing a document by its author's purpose
   - D. Corroboration

   **Answer: D.** Comparing several sources to establish what is probable and to notice disparities is corroboration. Close reading (A) focuses on one document's language; presentism (B) is the error of judging the past by present standards; and sourcing (C) evaluates a single source's origin rather than comparing several.

5. **Two well-trained historians study the same documents about a revolution and reach different conclusions about its main cause. What does historical thinking say about this situation?**
   - A. One of them must be dishonest, because sound evidence can support only a single conclusion
   - B. This is normal, because history is interpretation and evidence can reasonably support more than one defensible account
   - C. Their disagreement proves that no reliable knowledge about the past is possible
   - D. Whichever historian holds the more senior academic position should be regarded as correct

   **Answer: B.** History is interpretive, so historians can weigh causes differently and still argue defensibly from the evidence. Honest disagreement does not require dishonesty (A) or prove that nothing can be known (C), and authority or seniority (D) does not settle a historical claim — evidence and reasoning do.
