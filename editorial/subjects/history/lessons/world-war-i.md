# World War I

## In 30 Seconds

World War I (1914-1918) was the first industrial-scale war fought across whole continents. Long-term pressures often summarized as MAIN — militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism — made Europe combustible, and the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 lit the fuse. Alliance obligations pulled the great powers in within weeks. The fighting settled into trench warfare and stalemate, killed roughly 9-10 million soldiers, and ended with the armistice of 11 November 1918 and the Treaty of Versailles.

## Why This Matters

World War I reshaped the modern world. It toppled four empires, redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, and introduced total war — the mobilization of entire societies and economies for the fight. Understanding it is the key to understanding the twentieth century, because the harsh peace imposed at Versailles helped create the grievances that led to World War II barely two decades later. The war also shows how a single incident can trigger catastrophe when deep rivalries and rigid alliances are already in place, a lesson in how crises escalate that still shapes how leaders and historians think about diplomacy, deterrence, and the limits of control.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the four long-term causes of World War I summarized as MAIN and distinguish them from the immediate trigger.
- Explain how the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand set off an alliance chain reaction in the summer of 1914.
- Describe trench warfare on the Western Front and the industrial weapons that produced stalemate and mass casualties.
- Define total war and explain how the conflict drew in colonies and multiple continents.
- Summarize the human cost of the war and explain why the Treaty of Versailles is widely linked to the causes of World War II.

## The College Version

### The road to war: militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism

By 1914 Europe had gone decades without a general war, but beneath the calm ran pressures that a common teaching device summarizes with the acronym MAIN: militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism. Militarism showed up in a naval arms race, as Germany built battleships to challenge British sea power and Britain answered in 1906 with HMS Dreadnought, a warship so advanced it made every other battleship obsolete and set off a fresh round of building. Alliances divided the great powers into two armed camps. On one side stood the Triple Entente of Russia, France, and Britain, drawn together after 1907 by shared distrust of German aims; on the other stood the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. Imperialism sharpened the rivalry, because Germany, unified only in 1871, wanted colonies and influence to match older empires and competed with Britain and France in Africa and the Pacific. Nationalism pulled in two directions at once: it made the great powers proud and combative, and it strained multiethnic empires from within, as subject peoples in Austria-Hungary and the declining Ottoman Empire pressed for independence. None of these forces made war inevitable, but together they meant that a crisis in one corner of Europe could pull in every major power. Historians still debate how to weigh these causes against one another, even though the sequence of events is not in dispute.

### The spark and the chain reaction

The trigger came on 28 June 1914, when Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary, was assassinated in Sarajevo by a Bosnian Serb nationalist. Blaming Serbia, Austria-Hungary declared war on it on 28 July 1914. From there the alliance system did exactly what it was built to do, but in the worst possible way. Serbia's ally Russia mobilized its army; Germany, allied to Austria-Hungary, declared war on Russia and then on Russia's ally France; and when German forces invaded neutral Belgium to strike at France, Britain entered the war as well. Within about a week a local quarrel in the Balkans had become a continental war. It is important to keep the trigger in proportion. The assassination was the spark, not the whole explanation; without the arms race, the rival alliances, the imperial competition, and the nationalist tensions built up over decades, the same shooting need not have led to a world war. The two sides took shape as the Allied Powers, led by France, Russia, and Britain, and the Central Powers, made up of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and later the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria. Italy, though a member of the pre-war Triple Alliance, stayed neutral in 1914 and joined the Allies in 1915.

### The Western Front: trenches, stalemate, and industrial weapons

Both sides expected a short, mobile war. Instead, after the Battle of the Marne in September 1914 halted the German advance near Paris, the armies dug in, and a system of trenches came to define the Western Front. Two lines of fortified trenches, separated by a cratered strip called no-man's-land and tangled with barbed wire, ran hundreds of miles from the North Sea to the Swiss border. The technology of the era favored defenders and made attacking murderous. Machine guns based on Hiram Maxim's 1884 design could fire four to six hundred rounds a minute, cutting down advancing infantry, while heavy artillery pounded the trenches and poison gases such as chlorine, mustard gas, and phosgene added a new terror. Mustard gas could burn the eyes, nose, and skin with injuries that took weeks to heal. Attempts to break the deadlock produced staggering losses for tiny gains: at Verdun and on the Somme in 1916, casualties ran into the hundreds of thousands, and the Somme saw tens of thousands of deaths on its first day alone. New weapons meant to restore movement — tanks, introduced by Britain in 1916, and aircraft used first for reconnaissance and then for combat — hinted at the future of warfare but did not end the stalemate during most of the war.

### Total war and a global conflict

World War I was a total war, which OpenStax defines as a war fought using all available resources, with no restrictions on weapons or their targets. That meant far more than soldiers fighting at the front. Governments took control of their economies, directed factories to produce munitions, rationed food, drafted millions of men, and mobilized civilians — including large numbers of women who entered wartime industry. Propaganda and censorship enlisted public opinion, and the distinction between soldier and civilian blurred as submarines attacked merchant shipping and cities came under aerial bombardment. The war was also genuinely global. Though the heaviest fighting was in Europe, the combatants were empires, and they drew troops, laborers, and raw materials from colonies across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, so that men from India, Africa, Australia, New Zealand, Canada, and elsewhere fought and died far from home. Campaigns were fought in the Middle East against the Ottoman Empire, in Africa, and at sea worldwide. Naval blockade and unrestricted submarine warfare turned the world's oceans and food supplies into weapons, one reason the war eventually helped draw the United States in on the Allied side in 1917.

### The human cost, the armistice, and Versailles

The scale of death was without precedent. Estimates attributed to the Imperial War Museum put military deaths at more than nine million, with some twenty-one million wounded, and civilian deaths close to ten million from combat, hunger, disease, and displacement. On top of this came the influenza pandemic of 1918, which spread rapidly among crowded, exhausted troops and then worldwide; the U.S. Centers for Disease Control estimates it killed at least fifty million people globally, and possibly far more. Exhausted and unable to continue, Germany agreed to an armistice that took effect on 11 November 1918, ending the fighting. The formal peace came the following year with the Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors at the Palace of Versailles. Its terms fell heavily on Germany: Article 231, the so-called War Guilt Clause, required Germany to accept responsibility for the war, and the treaty imposed large reparations, stripped Germany of territory and all overseas colonies, and sharply limited its armed forces. Many Germans regarded the settlement as a humiliation, and historians widely agree that the resentment it fed helped create the conditions for World War II two decades later, even as they debate how much blame the treaty itself deserves.

## Key Vocabulary

- **MAIN (causes of World War I):** A teaching acronym for the four long-term causes of the war: militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism.
- **Militarism:** The buildup of armed forces and the belief that military strength should be used to advance national interests, seen before 1914 in the Anglo-German naval arms race.
- **Triple Entente:** The pre-war alignment of Russia, France, and Britain, formed after 1907 out of shared distrust of German ambitions.
- **Central Powers:** The wartime coalition of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and later the Ottoman Empire and Bulgaria, opposed to the Allied Powers.
- **Trench warfare:** Combat from fortified ditches facing each other across no-man's-land, which produced years of stalemate on the Western Front.
- **No-man's-land:** The exposed, cratered strip of ground between opposing frontline trenches, typically strung with barbed wire and swept by machine-gun fire.
- **Total war:** A war fought using all available resources, with no restrictions on weapons or their targets, mobilizing entire economies and societies.
- **Armistice:** An agreement to stop fighting; the World War I armistice took effect on 11 November 1918 and effectively ended the war as a German surrender.
- **Treaty of Versailles:** The 1919 peace treaty that formally ended the war with Germany, imposing war guilt, reparations, territorial losses, and disarmament.
- **Article 231 (War Guilt Clause):** The clause of the Treaty of Versailles requiring Germany to accept responsibility for causing the war, resented widely within Germany.

## Eli-10

When something big happens, it is tempting to point at one reason. Picture a schoolyard where the big kids have spent years choosing sides, building up muscle, and daring each other. Everyone is tense, but nobody has thrown a punch. Then one small fight breaks out, and because everyone has promised to back up their friends, within days the whole yard is brawling. That is roughly how World War I started: the promises between countries turned one killing in a city called Sarajevo into a war across the world. Once it began, the soldiers dug long ditches called trenches and hid in them, because new machine guns and cannons made charging across open ground almost suicidal. So they were stuck, facing each other for years, and millions of people died. When it finally ended, the winners made the losing side, Germany, sign a punishing agreement. That deal left so many Germans angry that it helped set up an even bigger war twenty years later.

## Eli's Analogy

The tangle of alliances was like a row of climbers roped together on a mountain: when one slipped off the edge, the ropes yanked all the others down with him.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Climbers are tied together by accident and would cut the rope to save themselves; the great powers chose their alliances on purpose and honored them, and unlike a single slip, the war grew from many causes built up over decades, not one unlucky step.

## Worked Example

Trace how the war spread in the summer of 1914 to see the alliance system at work. On 28 June, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary is assassinated in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary blames Serbia and declares war on it on 28 July. Serbia's ally Russia begins mobilizing its huge army. Germany, bound to Austria-Hungary, declares war on Russia, and then on Russia's ally France. To attack France quickly, Germany invades neutral Belgium, which brings Britain into the war against Germany. In roughly one week, a political murder in one Balkan city has drawn in five great powers. Now notice the historical thinking: the assassination was the immediate trigger, but the underlying causes — the arms race, the two alliance blocs, imperial rivalry, and nationalism — are what turned a local crisis into a world war. Different choices in late July might have contained it, which is why historians treat the outcome as contingent rather than inevitable.

## Common Mistakes

- **Believing the assassination of Franz Ferdinand caused World War I by itself.** The assassination was the trigger, not the whole cause. The long-term MAIN pressures — militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism — made Europe ready to explode; the spark only mattered because of them.
- **Confusing the Triple Alliance with the Central Powers, or assuming Italy fought with Germany.** The pre-war Triple Alliance included Italy, but Italy stayed neutral in 1914 and joined the Allies in 1915. The wartime Central Powers were Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria.
- **Picturing World War I as a war of constant movement and quick battles.** After late 1914 the Western Front froze into trench warfare and stalemate. Machine guns and artillery favored defenders, so huge attacks often gained little ground at enormous human cost.
- **Thinking of it as a purely European war between soldiers.** It was a total war and a global one. Empires mobilized whole economies and civilian populations and drew troops and resources from colonies across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific.
- **Assuming the Treaty of Versailles simply ended the war and settled everything.** The armistice of 11 November 1918 stopped the fighting; the Treaty of Versailles (1919) set the terms. Its harshness toward Germany is widely seen as helping cause World War II, though historians debate how much.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Long-term causes (MAIN) vs. Immediate trigger:** Militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism built up over decades and made war possible; the assassination of Franz Ferdinand in June 1914 was the specific spark that set it off. The trigger needed the underlying causes to have any effect.
- **Triple Alliance vs. Central Powers:** The Triple Alliance was the pre-war bloc of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. The wartime Central Powers were Germany, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria; Italy did not join them and instead fought with the Allies from 1915.
- **Armistice of 11 November 1918 vs. Treaty of Versailles (1919):** The armistice was the ceasefire that stopped the fighting; the Treaty of Versailles was the formal peace signed on 28 June 1919 that dictated the terms imposed on Germany.

## Key Takeaway

World War I turned decades of militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism into a total, industrial war after a single assassination in 1914; four years of trench stalemate killed millions, and the harsh Treaty of Versailles that followed helped sow the seeds of World War II.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What was the immediate trigger that set off the outbreak of World War I in the summer of 1914?**
   - A. The sinking of the RMS Lusitania by a German submarine
   - B. The assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo
   - C. Germany's invasion of Poland
   - D. The signing of the Treaty of Versailles

   **Answer: B.** Franz Ferdinand, heir to Austria-Hungary, was assassinated in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914, the spark that set off the alliance chain reaction. The Lusitania sinking (1915) came later and did not start the war; the invasion of Poland began World War II in 1939; and the Treaty of Versailles (1919) ended World War I rather than starting it.

2. **The long-term causes of World War I are often summarized by the acronym MAIN. Which set of causes does MAIN stand for?**
   - A. Monarchy, autocracy, industry, and neutrality
   - B. Migration, agriculture, invention, and nationalism
   - C. Money, armies, isolation, and negotiation
   - D. Militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism

   **Answer: D.** MAIN is a standard teaching device for the four underlying causes: militarism, alliances, imperialism, and nationalism. The other options mix in factors that do not fit the acronym or the history, such as isolation and neutrality, which were not among the pressures that drove the powers toward war.

3. **Which countries made up the Triple Entente, one of the two armed camps in Europe before World War I?**
   - A. Russia, France, and Britain
   - B. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy
   - C. Germany, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria
   - D. Britain, France, and the United States

   **Answer: A.** The Triple Entente aligned Russia, France, and Britain after 1907. Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy formed the rival Triple Alliance; Germany, the Ottoman Empire, and Bulgaria were among the wartime Central Powers; and the United States did not join the Entente and entered the war only in 1917.

4. **A government drafts millions of soldiers, converts its factories to munitions production, rations food for civilians, and censors the press to sustain the war effort. This mobilization of an entire society best illustrates which concept?**
   - A. Trench warfare
   - B. Imperialism
   - C. Total war
   - D. Armistice

   **Answer: C.** Total war is a war fought using all available resources, with no restrictions on weapons or their targets, mobilizing whole economies and populations. Trench warfare describes the style of fighting on the Western Front, imperialism was a cause of the war rather than a way of waging it, and an armistice is an agreement to stop fighting.

5. **Historians frequently connect the Treaty of Versailles to the later outbreak of World War II. Which feature of the treaty best supports that connection?**
   - A. It created the Triple Entente that encircled Germany before 1914
   - B. It granted Germany new colonies and expanded its army
   - C. It ended the war before any armistice could be signed
   - D. It forced Germany to accept war guilt and pay heavy reparations, breeding lasting resentment

   **Answer: D.** Article 231 assigned Germany responsibility for the war, and the treaty imposed heavy reparations, territorial losses, and disarmament; the resulting resentment is widely seen as helping cause World War II. The Triple Entente predated the treaty; the treaty stripped rather than granted colonies and shrank Germany's army; and the armistice of 11 November 1918 came before the treaty, not after.
