# Classical Greece

## In 30 Seconds

Classical Greece is the world of the Greek city-states in the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, roughly from the end of the Persian Wars (479 BCE) to the death of Alexander the Great (323 BCE). Its independent poleis included democratic Athens and militarized Sparta. Athens ran a direct democracy — but only for adult male citizens, excluding women, enslaved people, and foreigners. The era's philosophy, drama, and history-writing shaped later Western thought, and Alexander's conquests spread Greek culture across the Near East.

## Why This Matters

Classical Greece is a reference point for later Western politics, philosophy, science, and art, so college courses across the humanities keep returning to it. Understanding it well means holding two things at once: crediting genuine innovations — direct democracy, systematic philosophy, critical history-writing, monumental architecture — while being honest that Athenian democracy excluded most of the people who lived there, and that equally sophisticated civilizations flourished at the same time in Persia, India, and China. That double vision is exactly the historical skill instructors want: distinguishing real achievement from myth, and describing a society's ideals and its exclusions in the same breath rather than choosing one.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the polis and describe the political contrast between Athens and Sparta.
- Explain how Athenian direct democracy worked and identify who it excluded.
- Place the Greco-Persian Wars and the Peloponnesian War in sequence with approximate dates and outcomes.
- Identify the major figures of Classical Greek philosophy, history-writing, and drama.
- Explain how the Hellenistic era spread Greek culture after Alexander the Great, and evaluate Greek achievement without triumphalism.

## The College Version

### The polis and the world of city-states

The Greek world of the Classical period — conventionally the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, from the end of the Persian Wars (479 BCE) to the death of Alexander the Great (323 BCE) — was not a single country. It was a patchwork of hundreds of independent city-states, each called a polis. A polis typically grew up around a fortified hill, or acropolis, where inhabitants could shelter in danger, and each had its own government, laws, and religious cults. Greeks shared a language, gods, and festivals, and thought of themselves as a common people distinct from non-Greek "barbarians," but they rarely united politically, and the poleis competed and warred with one another as often as they cooperated. This is why "ancient Greece" names a culture rather than a state, and why its politics is best studied one city at a time. The two most influential — because they are the best documented and because they became rivals — were Athens and Sparta, and their differences show how much variation the polis system contained.

### Athens and Sparta: democracy and oligarchy

Sparta was an oligarchy: real power lay with a small class of full citizens, the soldier-citizens, and the state was organized around military strength. Sparta was headed by two hereditary kings and governed with a council of elders and assemblies, but participation was narrow, and its economy depended on a large subjugated population of helots who farmed the land. Spartan boys were taken from their families at about age seven and raised by the state for a lifetime of military service. Athens took a different path. Over the sixth and fifth centuries it developed a democracy — literally "rule by the people" (demos) — in which all adult male citizens could take part in government directly. The foundations were laid by the reforms of Cleisthenes around 508 BCE, which reorganized citizens by locality into demes and ten tribes, weakening the old aristocratic clans, and created a Council of 500 (the boule) in which citizens had an equal right to speak. Neither system was "better" in the abstract; they were different answers to how a polis should be governed, and much of Classical history is the story of their rivalry.

### How Athenian democracy worked — and whom it excluded

Athenian democracy was direct, not representative: citizens did not elect legislators to act for them but gathered in person in the Assembly to debate and vote on laws, war, and public spending, with a majority of those present deciding. Many offices were filled by lot rather than election, on the principle that ordinary citizens could govern. This was a genuine innovation, and it is a real ancestor of later democratic thought. But it is essential to be exact about its limits. Full political participation was reserved for adult male citizens — a body of perhaps thirty to forty thousand men in the Classical period. Women of citizen families had no vote and little public role, though they could serve as priestesses. Enslaved people, who made up a large part of the population, had no political rights at all. And metics — free foreigners living in Athens, including many long-settled merchants and craftsmen — were excluded from citizenship no matter how long they stayed. Athenian democracy was therefore direct and, for its citizens, remarkably participatory, but it was far from universal: most adults in Athens could not vote. Describing both the innovation and the exclusion is not a contradiction; it is the accurate picture.

### Two defining wars

Two conflicts frame the period. First came the Greco-Persian Wars, when the vast Persian (Achaemenid) Empire tried to bring the mainland Greeks under its control. The Athenians turned back a Persian invasion at Marathon in 490 BCE; a decade later a small Spartan-led force made its famous stand at Thermopylae while the Greek fleet destroyed the Persian navy at Salamis (both 480 BCE), and the following year the Greeks won decisively on land at Plataea (479 BCE). (OpenStax dates the wars as a whole to 492-449 BCE.) After the victory, Athens organized the Delian League in 477 BCE, an alliance that it gradually turned into an empire. That growing power alarmed Sparta, and the two led opposing coalitions into the Peloponnesian War (431-404 BCE). Pericles, the leading Athenian statesman of the era, died in 429 BCE during a plague that ravaged the crowded city early in the war. A truce, the Peace of Nicias, paused the fighting in 421 BCE, but the war resumed and finally ended in 404 BCE when a defeated Athens surrendered to Sparta. Athens lost its empire, and no single polis held lasting dominance afterward.

### Culture, Alexander, and the Hellenistic spread

The same century produced an extraordinary cultural record. In philosophy, Socrates (c. 470-399 BCE) pressed Athenians to examine their assumptions and was tried and executed by the city in 399 BCE; his student Plato (c. 428-348 BCE) founded the Academy, and Plato's student Aristotle (384-322 BCE) wrote across logic, biology, ethics, and politics and later tutored Alexander. Greeks also invented critical history-writing: Herodotus, later called the "Father of History," chronicled the Persian Wars, and Thucydides wrote a sharply analytical account of the Peloponnesian War he had lived through. Drama flourished in the forms of tragedy and comedy, and Athens rebuilt its Acropolis — crowned by the Parthenon — under Pericles. Political independence, however, was ending. By 338 BCE the kingdom of Macedon under Philip II dominated Greece, and his son Alexander the Great (356-323 BCE) conquered the Persian Empire, winning at Issus (333 BCE) and Gaugamela (330 BCE) and pushing into central Asia and India before dying at Babylon in 323 BCE. His death opened the Hellenistic period (323-31 BCE), in which Greek language and culture blended with local traditions across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East. New Greek cities such as Alexandria in Egypt became centers of learning, and philosophies like Stoicism and Epicureanism spread. All of this deserves credit without triumphalism: at the very same time, Achaemenid Persia, the Maurya empire in India, and the states of Zhou-era China were building equally sophisticated societies, and the Greek story is one among several, not the single source of civilization.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Polis** — An independent, self-governing Greek city-state, usually centered on a fortified hill (acropolis), with its own government, laws, and religious cults.
- **Direct democracy** — A system in which citizens themselves debate and vote on laws and policy in person, rather than electing representatives to decide for them.
- **Oligarchy** — Rule by a small group; in Sparta, political power was held by a narrow class of full soldier-citizens rather than by the people at large.
- **Metic** — A free foreign-born resident of Athens who could live and work in the city but was barred from citizenship and the vote.
- **Boule** — The Athenian Council of 500, created by Cleisthenes' reforms, which prepared business for the Assembly and gave citizens an equal right to speak.
- **Delian League** — An alliance of Greek city-states formed in 477 BCE and led by Athens after the Persian Wars, which Athens gradually turned into an empire.
- **Hellenistic period** — The era from Alexander the Great's death (323 BCE) to the Roman conquest of the last Greek kingdom (31 BCE), when Greek culture spread across the Near East.
- **Helots** — A large subjugated population in Sparta who farmed the land for the citizen class and had no political rights, underpinning Sparta's military society.

## Eli-10

Ancient Greece was not one country. It was hundreds of separate towns-with-their-own-rules called city-states, and they often argued and fought. Athens let its adult male citizens vote directly on everything, all together, which was a big new idea — but women, enslaved people, and people born elsewhere were not allowed to vote, so most people there had no say. Sparta was run by a small group and trained its boys to be soldiers from age seven. The Greeks fought off the huge Persian Empire, then Athens and Sparta fought each other for almost thirty years and Sparta won. Greek thinkers, playwrights, and history-writers from this time still get read today. Later a king named Alexander conquered a giant empire and spread Greek language and ideas far to the east.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of Classical Greece like a league of separate small towns that all speak the same language and cheer at the same festivals but each have their own mayor and rulebook — sometimes teaming up against an outside threat, often feuding with each other.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Modern towns sit inside one country with shared national laws, while each polis was fully sovereign, could wage its own wars, and answered to no higher Greek government. And a friendly sports league understates how deadly the rivalries became — the Peloponnesian War lasted a generation and ended one city's power for good.

## Worked Example

Imagine you want to place Athens' three big moments in order and say who was in and out of power. Start with democracy: Cleisthenes' reforms around 508 BCE set up the Assembly and the Council of 500, so by the early fifth century adult male citizens governed directly. Next, external threat: the Persian Wars peak in 490 BCE (Marathon) and 480-479 BCE (Salamis and Plataea), and Athens' leadership of the resulting Delian League (477 BCE) grows into an empire. Then, internal war: that empire frightens Sparta, and the Peloponnesian War (431-404 BCE) ends with Athens' surrender. Now ask the exclusion question at any point: could a metic merchant or a citizen's wife vote? No — only adult male citizens could. Laying the events on a timeline and separately checking who held rights keeps the achievement and its limits both in view.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Talking about "ancient Greece" as if it were a single unified country or empire.
  **Correction:** It was a culture shared across hundreds of independent poleis. They shared language and gods but had separate governments and frequently fought one another.
- **Mistake:** Describing Athenian democracy as if everyone could vote.
  **Correction:** Only adult male citizens could participate. Women, enslaved people, and metics (free foreigners) were all excluded, so most adults in Athens had no vote.
- **Mistake:** Confusing the Persian Wars with the Peloponnesian War, or reversing who fought whom.
  **Correction:** In the Persian Wars the Greek city-states resisted the Persian Empire; in the Peloponnesian War Athens and Sparta fought each other. Sparta won the latter, in 404 BCE.
- **Mistake:** Assuming Greece was uniquely advanced and the sole origin of civilization.
  **Correction:** Avoid triumphalism. Persia, Maurya India, and Zhou-era China were equally sophisticated at the same time; Greek contributions are one important thread, not the whole story.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Athens vs. Sparta:** Athens was a direct democracy in which adult male citizens voted in person; Sparta was a militarized oligarchy run by a small class of soldier-citizens and two kings.
- **Greco-Persian Wars vs. Peloponnesian War:** The Persian Wars (early fifth century BCE) pitted allied Greek poleis against the Persian Empire; the Peloponnesian War (431-404 BCE) was Greek-against-Greek, Athens versus Sparta.
- **Classical period vs. Hellenistic period:** The Classical period (to 323 BCE) centers on independent poleis like Athens; the Hellenistic period (323-31 BCE) follows Alexander, spreading Greek culture across large kingdoms in the Near East.

## Key Takeaway

Classical Greece was a world of independent city-states whose achievements in democracy, philosophy, and the arts were real and lasting — but Athenian democracy reached only adult male citizens, and equally sophisticated civilizations thrived elsewhere at the same time, so the era deserves credit without triumphalism.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In the Greek world of the Classical period, what was a polis?**
   - A. A large regional empire ruled by a single hereditary monarch
   - B. An independent, self-governing city-state
   - C. A pan-Hellenic parliament that governed all Greeks together
   - D. A four-yearly religious festival held at Olympia

   **Answer: B.** A polis was an independent, self-governing city-state, usually centered on an acropolis, with its own government and cults. The poleis were not unified under one monarch (A) and had no common government (C); the Olympic festival (D) is a different thing entirely.

2. **Which statement best captures the political contrast between Athens and Sparta in the Classical period?**
   - A. Both were direct democracies open to all residents, including women and enslaved people
   - B. Athens was ruled by two hereditary kings, while Sparta was governed by a mass citizen assembly
   - C. Athens was a democracy in which adult male citizens participated directly, while Sparta was an oligarchy dominated by an elite of soldier-citizens
   - D. Sparta was a democracy and Athens an empire with no citizen government at all

   **Answer: C.** Athens developed direct democracy for its adult male citizens; Sparta was an oligarchy centered on its soldier-citizen class and headed by two kings. Option A is false, option B reverses the two cities (the kings were Spartan), and option D is simply inverted.

3. **Four adults live in democratic Athens: a native adult male citizen, a citizen's wife, an enslaved worker, and a metic (a free foreign-born merchant). Under the rules of Athenian democracy, who could vote in the Assembly?**
   - A. All four, because Athens was a direct democracy
   - B. The citizen and his wife, but not the enslaved worker or the metic
   - C. The citizen and the metic, since both were free adults
   - D. Only the native adult male citizen

   **Answer: D.** Athenian democracy limited political participation to adult male citizens, so only that one person could vote. Women of citizen families could not vote (ruling out B), and metics were barred from citizenship regardless of how long they lived in Athens (ruling out C).

4. **How did the Peloponnesian War (431-404 BCE) end?**
   - A. Athens surrendered to Sparta, which won the war
   - B. Athens defeated Sparta and absorbed it into the Athenian empire
   - C. The Persian Empire conquered both Athens and Sparta
   - D. The two sides signed a lasting peace that left Athens the dominant power

   **Answer: A.** The war ended in 404 BCE with Athens' surrender to Sparta. Option B reverses the outcome, option C confuses it with the earlier Persian Wars, and option D mistakes the temporary Peace of Nicias (421 BCE) for a final settlement.

5. **Which event conventionally marks the beginning of the Hellenistic period, when Greek culture spread widely across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East?**
   - A. The Athenian victory over Persia at the Battle of Marathon
   - B. The reforms of Cleisthenes that founded Athenian democracy
   - C. The death of Alexander the Great in 323 BCE
   - D. The outbreak of the Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta

   **Answer: C.** The Hellenistic period is dated from Alexander's death in 323 BCE to the Roman conquest of the last Greek kingdom in 31 BCE. Marathon (490 BCE) and Cleisthenes (c. 508 BCE) belong to the earlier Classical period, and the Peloponnesian War (431 BCE) began an intra-Greek conflict, not the Hellenistic era.
