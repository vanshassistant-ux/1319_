# Social Psychology

## In 30 Seconds

Social psychology is the study of how other people shape our thoughts, feelings, and behavior — through their actual presence, the ones we imagine, and the ones we only carry in mind. Its central claim is the power of the situation: context steers us more than we usually notice. Its big topics are attribution, attitudes, conformity, obedience, and group behavior. Classic studies by Solomon Asch, Stanley Milgram, and Philip Zimbardo showed ordinary people bending under social pressure. We are social animals with social minds.

## Why This Matters

Social psychology sits at the meeting point of the individual and everyone else. Academically, it explains how the mind works in company — which is where minds actually work most of the time — and it connects to personality, emotion, and learning. Practically, it helps you read your own behavior: why you act differently in a crowd, why a team decision went wrong, why a first impression of a rude coworker may be a misread of a bad day. Forward-looking, the same principles show up in teamwork, public health campaigns, online communities, and leadership. The honest value is the biggest one: knowing that situations shape us is the first step toward choosing situations that bring out the best in us.

## The College Version

### What social psychology is

Social psychology is the study of how other people shape what we think, feel, and do. The working definition used here comes from Gordon Allport, carried in the APA Dictionary of Psychology: social psychology is the study of how an individual's thoughts, feelings, and actions are affected by the actual, imagined, or symbolically represented presence of other people. Actual presence is a friend in the room; imagined presence is the audience you picture while rehearsing alone; symbolized presence is the standard you carry, like a coach's voice on the last lap. OpenStax Psychology 2e: social psychology examines how people affect one another, and it looks at the power of the situation. The Noba Project's introduction module investigates the same ground: the ways other people affect our thoughts, feelings, and behaviors. Three authoritative sources, one object of study.

### The big topics

The field organizes around a handful of big questions. Attribution concerns inferences about the causes of behavior — the APA Dictionary: an inference regarding the cause of a person's behavior or an interpersonal event. Original example: Priya's coworker snaps at a customer, and Priya's first thought is rudeness; the coworker had just learned her father was hospitalized — the situational explanation never occurred to Priya. Attitudes are relatively enduring evaluations of objects, people, groups, issues, or concepts, ranging from negative to positive, per the APA Dictionary. Original example: Ravi quietly dislikes cilantro, and the evaluation colors every menu he reads, guiding his orders. Conformity is the adjustment of opinions, judgments, or actions to match other people or group standards; it has its own sibling lesson, so we name it here. Original example: at a concert, Delia claps along with the encore though she found the song merely fine. Obedience is behavior in compliance with a direct command, often from a person in authority, per the APA Dictionary. Original example: a new hire keeps the outdated spreadsheet template because the manager told her to, though a better one exists. Group behavior is what happens when two or more interdependent people influence one another through roles, norms, and shared goals (the APA Dictionary's social group). Original example: four students split a presentation into speaker, slide maker, timer, and fact-checker — a result none would have produced alone.

### The situation idea

The core argument of social psychology is that context shapes behavior powerfully. OpenStax is blunt: people will change their behavior to align with the social situation at hand. The field's signature demonstration of how much we underrate this is the fundamental attribution error, named by Ross (1977): overestimating character and underestimating situation when explaining behavior. Original example: at his cousin's wedding, Daniel — famously quiet in the office — delivers the toast with a confidence no colleague has ever seen: the celebration, the microphone, the room expecting a speech drew out behavior his personality alone would not predict. The honest note: the power of situations is real but not absolute. Reviews find the error varies with context and culture — stronger in individualistic societies, as OpenStax and the APA Dictionary both note. Situations steer us; they do not erase us.

### Attitudes: evaluations that guide — sometimes

An attitude is a relatively enduring evaluation of an object, person, group, issue, or concept on a negative-to-positive scale, often assumed to grow out of beliefs, emotions, and past behaviors (APA Dictionary). The natural assumption is that attitudes guide behavior — and often they do. But the honest note from the research: attitudes do not always predict action. The Noba Project's social cognition module explains that explicit attitudes, the ones we can report, can diverge from implicit attitudes — automatic evaluations beyond awareness — and both can lose to the situation. Original example: Mila tells a survey interviewer she strongly supports recycling, but late for a train she drops a plastic bottle into a general trash bin — no recycling bin is nearby. Her stated attitude and her action parted ways — not dishonesty, but the situation outrunning the evaluation.

### The classic studies

Three landmark studies anchor the field's history, each earning one line here as an attributed historical study. Solomon Asch's line-judgment studies of the 1950s showed that people often give an obviously wrong answer to match a unanimous group — conformity in action. Stanley Milgram's obedience research at Yale (1963) showed participants following an experimenter's instructions in what they believed was a learning study, further than most people predicted, and it raised lasting ethical questions about research with human participants. Philip Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment (1971) assigned student volunteers to guard and prisoner roles in a mock prison, showing the power of social roles, norms, and scripts. All three are described in OpenStax Psychology 2e (sections 12.2 and 12.4), and the Asch and Milgram studies appear in the Noba Project's conformity and obedience module. What they share is a finding that surprised their era: ordinary people, in ordinary situations, can behave in ways their friends would swear they never would.

### The honest framing

The reality check that ties the lesson together: we are social animals with social minds. Being shaped by others is not a flaw in our design — it is the design. The same machinery that produces conformity also produces teamwork, cooperation, and kindness, and the same sensitivity to authority keeps classrooms and surgical teams functioning. When your own behavior surprises you, or someone else's does, look at the context before you write the verdict on character. The situation is part of the story — often the bigger part.

## Key Vocabulary

- **social psychology** — The study of how an individual's thoughts, feelings, and actions are affected by the actual, imagined, or symbolically represented presence of other people.
- **power of the situation** — The idea that context — roles, norms, and the presence of others — shapes behavior powerfully, often more than personality alone predicts.
- **attribution** — An inference about the cause of a person's behavior or an interpersonal event.
- **fundamental attribution error** — The tendency to overestimate a person's traits and underestimate the situation when explaining their behavior.
- **attitude** — A relatively enduring evaluation of an object, person, group, issue, or concept, ranging from negative to positive.
- **explicit attitude** — An evaluation a person can deliberately report when asked.
- **implicit attitude** — An automatic evaluation that can operate without awareness and may differ from what a person reports.
- **obedience** — Behavior in compliance with a direct command, typically issued by a person in a position of authority.
- **conformity** — The adjustment of one's opinions, judgments, or actions to match other people or group standards.
- **social group** — Two or more interdependent individuals who influence one another through roles, norms, and shared goals.

## Eli-10

**Simplified explanation.** Social psychology is the study of how other people get inside your head. Not just the people in the room: the ones you imagine, and the ones you have never met whose standards you have absorbed. The field's big lesson is that your behavior is not only yours. The situation — who is watching, what role you are playing, what everyone else is doing — steers you more than you notice. That is why the same person can be patient at home and sharp at the office, and why a confident speaker can go quiet in a meeting. The classic studies by Asch, Milgram, and Zimbardo made this point by showing ordinary people doing surprising things under social pressure.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The river only pushes one way; people push back. We choose situations, shape them, and sometimes swim hard against the current — a person under pressure still has agency. The same current moves different swimmers differently, so situations steer personality rather than erase it. And rivers have no intentions, while situations are made by people with expectations, which means they can be negotiated, questioned, and changed.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a swimmer in a river. The swimmer brings skill, strength, and habits — that is the person. The river brings current, banks, and weather — that is the situation. Two equally strong swimmers can end up in completely different places because the rivers differ, and a strong swimmer can be carried along by a current they never noticed. Social psychology is the science of the river: it maps the currents of roles, norms, and other people that move us.

## Worked Example

Nadia usually studies alone, reading in silence. For the final exam she joins a study group at the library. Omar, the group's strongest student, announces a plan: each person explains one chapter aloud while the others listen. Nadia finds herself explaining the memory chapter out loud, matching the group's format — behavior she never shows at home. When Omar declares that forgetting is mostly a matter of time passing, Nadia, who read the interference research, hears herself nod along before she quietly corrects him. Same person, different situation: the group's norms, roles, and a confident leader changed how she studied and even what she said before she thought it through. Later she tells a friend she is a group person — a trait explanation for behavior the situation produced.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Social psychology is just common sense about people."** — Common sense explains events after the fact; social psychology tests its claims with systematic research. The classic studies produced results most people did not predict — that is why they became classics.
- **"If someone behaves badly, the explanation is simply who they are."** — That is the fundamental attribution error. It undercounts the situation: the same person behaves very differently in different contexts, as the wedding-toast example shows.
- **"If I say I believe something, my behavior will follow."** — Attitudes are real, but they do not always predict action. Explicit and implicit attitudes can diverge, and situations intervene, as Mila's recycling example shows.
- **"The classic studies prove people are weak-willed."** — They demonstrate the power of situations, not a verdict on human character. Later research also finds these effects vary across cultures and contexts, so the honest lesson is about context, not about weakness.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Conformity vs. obedience:** Conformity is adjusting to match a group's standards, often with no one commanding anything; obedience is complying with a direct command from authority. You conform to a clapping crowd; you obey a supervisor's instruction.
- **Attitude vs. behavior:** An attitude is an evaluation; behavior is an action. Attitudes often guide behavior but do not always predict it, because situations and implicit attitudes intervene.
- **Dispositional explanation vs. situational explanation:** A dispositional explanation credits or blames a person's traits; a situational explanation points to context, roles, and pressures. The fundamental attribution error is the habit of choosing the first when the second fits better.

## Key Takeaway

Social psychology studies how other people — present, imagined, or symbolized — shape our thoughts, feelings, and behavior; its core lesson is that situations steer us powerfully, and its honest promise is that noticing that power is the first step to using it well.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **The working definition used in this lesson — social psychology as the study of how an individual's thoughts, feelings, and actions are affected by the actual, imagined, or symbolically represented presence of other people — is attributed to which psychologist?**
   - A. Gordon Allport
   - B. Solomon Asch
   - C. Stanley Milgram
   - D. Philip Zimbardo

   **Answer: A.** The APA Dictionary of Psychology carries this as the definition of social psychology as stated by Gordon W. Allport. Asch studied conformity, Milgram studied obedience, and Zimbardo ran the Stanford prison experiment — all famous researchers, but none of them authored the working definition this lesson uses.

2. **Which scenario is social psychology best suited to study?**
   - A. How a person's heart rate changes during a morning run.
   - B. How a student's answer choices shift after hearing several classmates answer first.
   - C. Which brain regions activate during deep sleep.
   - D. How a child's height changes across the school years.

   **Answer: B.** Social psychology examines how other people affect thoughts, feelings, and behavior, so a student changing answers under the influence of classmates is squarely in its territory. Heart rate, sleep brain activity, and growth are physiology and development, not social influence.

3. **Renata snaps at a client, and her coworker Omar thinks, "She is just rude." Omar has not learned that Renata received upsetting news minutes earlier. Which concept does Omar's thinking best illustrate?**
   - A. Obedience to authority.
   - B. The attitude-behavior gap.
   - C. The fundamental attribution error.
   - D. Conformity to group norms.

   **Answer: C.** Omar explains Renata's behavior with a trait — rudeness — while ignoring the situation. That is the fundamental attribution error: overestimating personal character and underestimating context when explaining another person's behavior. Obedience, the attitude-behavior gap, and conformity do not fit the scenario.

4. **Mila tells a survey she strongly supports recycling, yet when she is late for a train and no recycling bin is nearby, she drops a plastic bottle into the general trash. What does this episode best illustrate?**
   - A. Obedience to a direct command.
   - B. The fundamental attribution error.
   - C. Conformity with a group's standards.
   - D. That attitudes do not always predict behavior.

   **Answer: D.** Mila's stated attitude is pro-recycling, but the situation — rushing, no bin in reach — produced different behavior. This is the honest note of the attitudes research: explicit attitudes can diverge from action. The episode involves no command, no misattribution of causes, and no group standard to match.

5. **In the school play, a normally shy student is cast as a loud, commanding general. By the final rehearsal she is striding across the stage and bellowing her lines. Which explanation best fits the lesson's account of this change?**
   - A. The power of the situation: the role, the script, and the stage expectations shaped her behavior.
   - B. Her attitude toward acting changed permanently and now controls her behavior in every setting.
   - C. She obeyed a direct command to change her underlying personality.
   - D. She committed the fundamental attribution error about herself.

   **Answer: A.** The lesson's core argument is that situations — roles, norms, scripts — shape behavior powerfully, which is exactly what Zimbardo's Stanford prison experiment demonstrated with assigned roles. Option B overstates attitude-behavior consistency, C mistakes role-playing for obedience, and D applies the wrong concept: the fundamental attribution error is about explaining others, not about what the situation produced.
