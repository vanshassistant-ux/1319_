# Psychological Disorders

## In 30 Seconds

A psychological disorder is a pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving that causes distress or impaired functioning — the working definition this lesson uses, close to how MedlinePlus and the DSM describe mental disorders. Where the line falls is debated, and experts keep refining the manual they use. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, published by the American Psychiatric Association, is the main classification tool in the United States — a tool, not a truth machine. Stigma, misunderstanding that harms people, is a real problem. This lesson is education, not diagnosis.

## Why This Matters

Mental health is not a niche topic: more than one in five U.S. adults live with a mental illness in a given year, so most people know someone touched by these conditions — or are that someone themselves. Academically, psychological disorders sit where biology, thought, and culture meet; the same mind that learns and remembers can also suffer. Practically, this lesson gives you an accurate frame for what a diagnosis is and is not, so you can talk about mental health without fear-mongering, spot stigma when you see it, and read health claims more carefully. It also lays the groundwork for the next topic in this sequence: how treatment works.

## Learning Objectives

- Define psychological disorder using the working definition — a pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving that causes distress or impaired functioning — and name the sources that anchor it.
- Explain the medical model's core idea in one line: psychological disorders treated as health conditions, analogous to biological problems.
- Distinguish the anxiety, mood, psychotic, and personality categories of disorders, each with an original fictional example.
- Describe the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders — what it is, who publishes it, and why it is a tool rather than a truth machine.
- Analyze how stigma operates and why misunderstanding harms people with psychological disorders.
- Apply the honest framing that a diagnosis describes a pattern of symptoms and is not a verdict on a person.

## The College Version

### What counts as a psychological disorder

The working definition used in this lesson: a psychological disorder is a pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving that causes distress or impaired functioning. It is not invented here. MedlinePlus describes mental disorders as conditions that affect thinking, feeling, mood, and behavior and can impair the ability to function each day; the DSM-5 definition quoted in OpenStax adds significant distress in social, occupational, or other important activities. Both agree on the spine: distress, impaired functioning, or both. Original example: Amir, a fictional student, has slept badly for three months, stopped answering calls from friends, and watched his grades collapse. Thinking (constant dread), feeling (misery), behaving (withdrawal): real distress and impaired functioning — it meets the working definition. The honest note: definitions are debated. Experts struggle to agree on which thoughts, feelings, and behaviors truly indicate psychopathology — the study of psychological disorders, their symptoms, causes, and treatment — and uncomfortable states like first-date nerves can fall in the normal range.

### The medical model: disorders as health conditions

The medical model is one influential way of thinking about psychological disorders, and it fits in one line: mental and emotional problems are analogous to biological problems — with detectable causes and specific treatments (APA Dictionary of Psychology). Stated simply, the model treats a psychological disorder as a health condition to be understood, named, and addressed — not as a moral failing or weak character. The honest limits matter: the APA Dictionary's mental disorder entry lists physiological, genetic, chemical, social, and other factors among the causes, so biology, psychology, and life experience all contribute; the model is a starting point, not the whole story. Original example: when a doctor says a patient has diabetes, no one hears a character judgment; the medical model holds that a depression diagnosis deserves the same treatment.

### Four categories, four original examples

Anxiety disorders are characterized by excessive and persistent fear and anxiety plus related behavioral disturbances (APA, 2013). Original example: Hana, a fictional graduate, feels crushing dread at the thought of attending her own graduation and avoids crowded places now. Mood disorders are characterized by severe disturbances in mood and emotions — most often depression, but also mania and elation. Original example: Marcus, a fictional mechanic, spends weeks feeling hollow and uninterested in the engine projects he once loved. Psychotic disorders, such as schizophrenia, involve major disturbances in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior, including hallucinations and delusions. Original example: Elena, a fictional librarian, hears voices no one else hears and believes strangers are plotting against her. One myth cleared: schizophrenia is not split personality (OpenStax). Personality disorders involve a personality style that differs markedly from cultural expectations, is pervasive and inflexible, and causes distress or impairment. Original example: Dev, a fictional bookkeeper, has for years read friendly remarks as hidden attacks and pushed people away before they could get close. These four families are a map, not a menu: real people rarely fit one neat box, and disorders often co-occur — comorbidity is common.

### The DSM: a tool, not a truth machine

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders — the DSM — is the classification system used by most mental health professionals in the United States, published by the American Psychiatric Association. The first edition appeared in 1952; DSM-5 followed in 2013 and its text revision, DSM-5-TR, in 2022 (APA Dictionary). The manual exists so professionals share a common language: a diagnosis labels a set of symptoms, supporting communication and, ultimately, treatment decisions. The honest note: the DSM is a tool, not a truth machine. Its categories are expert judgments that have changed — the first two editions listed homosexuality as a disorder until the APA voted to remove it in 1973 (OpenStax) — and researchers continue to critique the overlap between diagnoses. A manual written, revised, and used by people: useful, and worth treating as such.

### Stigma: when misunderstanding harms

The APA Dictionary of Psychology defines stigma as the negative social attitude attached to a characteristic of an individual that may be regarded as a mental, physical, or social deficiency; it implies social disapproval and can lead unfairly to discrimination against and exclusion of the individual. In plainer words: misunderstanding psychological disorders causes real harm. Original example: at a fictional office, a manager quietly moves a colleague who once mentioned a depression diagnosis off a major project, 'just to be safe' — although her work has always been on time. No evidence, no missed deadline — a label, and a quiet exclusion: stigma in action. The honest note is simple: misunderstanding harms people. A diagnosis describes a pattern; it is not a summary of a person's worth, and the person behind the label is the same colleague, neighbor, or friend as before.

### The honest note: education, not diagnosis

This lesson is education, not diagnosis. Reading descriptions of symptom patterns does not qualify anyone to diagnose; only trained professionals, comparing a person's experience against established criteria, can do that. The categories here are for orientation, the way learning the names of constellations helps you look at the sky without claiming to navigate by them. The honest framing that closes the lesson: a diagnosis is a description, not a verdict. It names a pattern of suffering so it can be talked about, studied, and addressed; it does not define who a person is, what they are capable of, or what their future holds. If you or someone you care about is struggling, the right next step is a conversation with a qualified professional — the subject of the next topic in this sequence.

## Key Vocabulary

- **psychological disorder** — A pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving that causes distress or impaired functioning; the working definition used in this lesson, anchored in how MedlinePlus and the DSM describe mental disorders.
- **psychopathology** — The study of psychological disorders, including their symptoms, their causes, and their treatment, per OpenStax Psychology 2e.
- **medical model** — The concept, defined in the APA Dictionary, that mental and emotional problems are analogous to biological problems, with detectable causes and specific treatments.
- **DSM** — The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, the classification system used by most mental health professionals in the United States and published by the American Psychiatric Association.
- **anxiety disorders** — A category characterized by excessive and persistent fear and anxiety plus related disturbances in behavior, such as avoidance.
- **mood disorders** — A category characterized by severe disturbances in mood and emotions, ranging from the extreme sadness of depression to the extreme elation of mania.
- **psychotic disorders** — A category, including schizophrenia, marked by major disturbances in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior, such as hallucinations and delusions.
- **personality disorders** — Long-standing, pervasive, and inflexible personality styles that differ markedly from cultural expectations and cause distress or impairment.
- **stigma** — The negative social attitude attached to a characteristic, implying social disapproval and leading unfairly to discrimination against and exclusion of the individual, per the APA Dictionary.
- **comorbidity** — The co-occurrence of two or more disorders in the same person, which research shows is common among psychological disorders.

## Eli-10

**Simplified explanation.** Think of the mind as machinery for thinking, feeling, and behaving. A psychological disorder is when that machinery goes off course in a way that hurts the person — causing distress — and gets in the way of daily life — impairing functioning. Where exactly 'off course' begins is a judgment experts debate, so definitions are honest approximations, not engraved truths. The DSM is the big manual experts use to describe and compare disorders, and it gets revised because expert judgment improves and changes. One more thing matters: a person with a disorder is a person first, and the cruelest part of these conditions is often the misunderstanding around them — stigma that makes people ashamed to ask for help. This lesson teaches you to see clearly; it does not teach you to diagnose.

**The analogy.** A diagnosis is like a label on a jar: the label tells you what is inside — a pattern of symptoms — not what the jar is worth. Stigma is what happens when people judge the jar by a rumor about the label instead of by the person holding it.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The comparison breaks down because a jar's label is fixed and simple, while a diagnosis is a professional judgment that can be revised, debated, and even changed by new editions of the manual. A jar has no feelings and no story; a person does, so a label can never capture a whole life. And a jar's label is applied by a machine, while a diagnosis is made by trained people who must weigh evidence with care.

## Worked Example

Consider Tomás, a fictional barista. For two months, his chest tightens whenever a line forms at the counter, he dreads opening the shop, and last week he called in sick to avoid a busy Saturday. Walk through the working definition: his pattern involves feeling (fear), thinking (dread of the line), and behaving (avoidance), and it is causing distress and impairing his work — so the pattern meets the lesson's working definition of a psychological disorder. Which family? Persistent, excessive fear and avoidance fit the description of the anxiety disorders. Now the honest framing: even this careful walkthrough does not diagnose Tomás — a professional comparing his experience against the DSM's criteria would make that call. What this lesson contributes is the accurate vocabulary to describe the pattern, and the reminder that Tomás is a person with a condition, not a case file.

## Common Mistakes

- **A psychological disorder is just being unusual or different from other people.** Unusual alone does not make a disorder. The working definition requires distress or impaired functioning, and where the line falls is debated — first-date nerves are uncomfortable but normal, while a pattern that disrupts daily life meets the test.
- **The DSM is a complete, unchanging list of all mental illnesses, straight from nature.** The DSM is a classification tool written by experts and published by the American Psychiatric Association. Its categories have changed over time — homosexuality was removed in 1973 — so it is best treated as a useful tool, not a truth machine.
- **Schizophrenia means split personality.** It does not. As OpenStax stresses, schizophrenia involves major disturbances in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior, including hallucinations and delusions; split personality is a different, dissociative condition.
- **After reading this lesson, I can diagnose someone from their symptoms.** This lesson is education, not diagnosis. Only qualified professionals, comparing a person's experience against established criteria, can diagnose — and the honest next step for someone struggling is a conversation with a professional.
- **People with psychological disorders are dangerous, and it is their own fault.** Stigma works exactly this way: a negative attitude attached to a label, leading to unfair treatment. Disorders are health conditions to be understood and addressed, not character verdicts, and misunderstanding — not the person — is the problem.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Ordinary distress, like first-date nerves or homesickness, vs. a psychological disorder:** Both involve uncomfortable feelings, but a disorder is a pattern that causes significant distress or impaired functioning, while ordinary distress falls in the normal range — and experts debate exactly where the line sits.
- **The medical model vs. a broader multi-factor view:** The medical model treats disorders as health conditions analogous to biological problems, with detectable causes and specific treatments; a broader view, reflected in the APA Dictionary's mental disorder entry, adds psychological and social factors to the mix.
- **A DSM diagnosis vs. a verdict on a person:** A diagnosis labels a pattern of symptoms so professionals share a common language; it describes a condition the person has, while a verdict would judge the person's worth — which no diagnosis does.

## Key Takeaway

A psychological disorder is a pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving that causes distress or impaired functioning — a description of a condition, not a verdict on a person, and a topic that calls for understanding rather than stigma.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **According to the working definition in this lesson, what makes a pattern of thinking, feeling, or behaving a psychological disorder?**
   - It is unusual or rarely seen in other people
   - It causes distress or impaired functioning
   - It is difficult for others to understand
   - It has lasted for more than one month

   **Answer: B.** The working definition requires distress or impaired functioning, following MedlinePlus's description of mental disorders and the DSM-5 definition quoted in OpenStax. Unusualness alone is not enough, and neither is duration by itself; the test is the harm the pattern does.

2. **The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM) is best described as which of the following?**
   - A list of treatments published by the World Health Organization
   - A set of laws about how mental health care is delivered
   - A classification system published by the American Psychiatric Association that gives professionals a common language
   - A therapy method developed by clinical psychologists

   **Answer: C.** The DSM is a classification system published by the American Psychiatric Association, used by most mental health professionals in the United States, as OpenStax reports; its first edition appeared in 1952. It is not a treatment list, a set of laws, or a therapy method.

3. **Hana, a fictional graduate, feels crushing dread at the thought of attending her own graduation ceremony and has started avoiding crowded places. Based on the lesson's description of the four categories, which family does her pattern of excessive fear and avoidance best illustrate?**
   - Anxiety disorders
   - Mood disorders
   - Psychotic disorders
   - Personality disorders

   **Answer: A.** Anxiety disorders are characterized by excessive and persistent fear and anxiety plus related behavioral disturbances like avoidance, per OpenStax — which matches Hana's dread and avoidance. Mood disorders center on severe mood disruption, psychotic disorders on disturbed thought and perception, and personality disorders on long-standing inflexible styles.

4. **At a fictional workplace, a manager quietly moves a colleague who once mentioned a depression diagnosis off a major project 'just to be safe,' even though her work has always been on time. Which concept best describes what is happening?**
   - The medical model
   - Comorbidity
   - The working definition of a psychological disorder
   - Stigma

   **Answer: D.** The APA Dictionary defines stigma as a negative social attitude attached to a characteristic that can lead unfairly to discrimination and exclusion — exactly what the manager's unfounded reassignment does. The medical model frames disorders as health conditions, comorbidity is the co-occurrence of disorders, and the working definition concerns distress or impaired functioning.

5. **Marisol, a fictional student, learns that a classmate received a diagnosis and worries that the label now 'sums up' the whole person. Based on the honest framing in this lesson, which response is most accurate?**
   - A diagnosis is a verdict on a person's character
   - A diagnosis explains exactly why the disorder developed
   - A diagnosis describes a pattern of symptoms — a condition, not the whole person
   - A diagnosis is permanent and can never be revised

   **Answer: C.** The honest framing holds that a diagnosis is a description of a symptom pattern, not a verdict on the person. It does not settle the cause, and because manuals and judgments change, diagnoses can be revised — so both the verdict reading and the permanence reading are wrong.
