# Race and Media

## In 30 Seconds

Race and media studies examines how racial groups appear in film and television: who shows up, in what kinds of roles, and how often they are absent. Scholars have documented recurring stereotypes, typecasting, and the near-invisibility of some groups in early U.S. cinema, alongside the independent films made in response. Describing these patterns is not the same as claiming what any group is really like; the patterns are features of media texts, and their effects remain matters of interpretation.

## Why This Matters

Race-and-media analysis turns vague impressions into checkable claims. It teaches viewers to distinguish a pattern repeated across many films and shows from a single unusual portrayal, and to separate what media show from what any group of people is actually like. The vocabulary is practical: it clarifies public debates about casting, awards, and who directs and writes the stories audiences see, and it makes data such as the UCLA Hollywood Diversity Report legible. The questions also look forward, because media industries keep changing — new platforms, new funding, new filmmakers — and the same analytic tools apply to whatever comes next.

## Learning Objectives

- Define stereotyping, typecasting, and symbolic absence as documented patterns in U.S. film and television.
- Explain why repeated patterns of representation matter beyond any single portrayal.
- Describe documented findings on on-screen and behind-the-camera racial diversity, including the UCLA Hollywood Diversity Report series.
- Distinguish describing a pattern of representation from making a claim about any group's nature.
- Analyze a contemporary casting or cultural-appropriation debate by identifying and attributing the competing positions.

## The College Version

### Patterns of racial representation in U.S. film and television

Early U.S. cinema inherited practices from popular theater, and in landmark productions Black characters were routinely played by white performers in blackface. Film scholars point to The Birth of a Nation (1915) and The Jazz Singer (1927) as prominent examples; the practice drew organized protest from Black audiences and declined during the 1930s. Alongside those casting practices, scholars have catalogued recurring stock character types that appeared across dozens of films: the loyal servant, the comic simpleton, the tragic mixed-race character, and the menacing Black male. These are documented patterns in the texts, not descriptions of real people.

Typecasting followed as an industry practice: a performer could be repeatedly assigned to the same narrow type, as when one actor was billed and marketed under a single comic label and appeared in that role in film after film before stepping away, frustrated by unequal billing and pay. Absence was a pattern too. Communication scholar George Gerbner argued in 1976 that appearing in the fictional world signals social existence, while not appearing signals symbolic annihilation. Independent filmmakers answered with their own cinema: from the 1910s, Black filmmakers such as Oscar Micheaux produced dozens of race films with their own stars, directors, and theaters, offering stories aimed at Black audiences rather than at the stereotypes of the mainstream industry.

### Why patterns matter beyond individual portrayals

A single odd portrayal is a curiosity; a pattern repeated across many texts is something else. Stereotypes matter in media analysis partly because of repetition: when the same simplified type recurs in film after film, it can become shorthand that audiences recognize before any individual story begins. The documented patterns also renew themselves across eras — the 1970s blaxploitation cycle, and modern types such as the magical helper, the threatening figure, and the white savior — which shows the same structures being adapted rather than disappearing.

Media also carry cultural authority: they reach mass audiences and circulate for decades, so scholars argue that the presence, absence, and role patterns they repeat can shape what audiences consider normal, expected, or even real. That is why analysts study the pattern, not just the individual film: the question is what a body of texts, taken together, keeps telling viewers. This is an interpretive argument from scholarship, and its strength depends on the evidence — how many texts, how consistent the roles, and what the surrounding context shows.

### The politics of who tells stories

Who controls production shapes what gets made, and documented industry data show persistent gaps. The UCLA Hollywood Diversity Report 2025 found that Black, Indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC) were 44.3 percent of the U.S. population in 2024 but held 25.2 percent of theatrical film leads, 20.2 percent of directing credits, 12.5 percent of writing credits, and 32.8 percent of total acting roles in top films — underrepresented in every arena the report examined. The report, part of an annual UCLA series, also argues the gaps matter commercially: it reports that films with casts that were 41 to 50 percent BIPOC posted the highest median global box office receipts in 2024, a finding the authors read as evidence that diverse audiences prefer diverse content, and one that remains an interpretation rather than a settled law.

The politics of storytelling also appear in recognition and control. The #OscarsSoWhite campaign of 2015-16 called on the Motion Picture Academy after two consecutive years in which all twenty acting nominations went to white actors. And the history of Hollywood absorbing independent Black filmmaking — the early race films, and later the blaxploitation cycle — shows that when studios took over a genre, its direction and control changed. University research centers, including USC's Annenberg Inclusion Initiative, continue to track these patterns with original data.

### Debates, and how to discuss them carefully

Contemporary debates about casting and cultural borrowing are real and contested. Critics have objected when roles adapted from one culture's stories are played by actors from another background — sometimes described with the contested term whitewashing — and commentators disagree about where borrowing, homage, or exchange becomes cultural appropriation. Studios and defenders have framed such decisions as creative choices or market realities. A careful analysis attributes the positions rather than settling the argument.

Blaxploitation offers a documented example: the NAACP coined the term as a criticism of stereotypes it said exploited Black audiences, while filmmakers defended the cycle as a reclamation of Black imagery. One distinction keeps the whole discussion honest: describing a pattern of representation is an analysis of media texts, and it is not a claim about what any group of real people is like. A stereotype is a representation, not evidence about the group it depicts. That is the discipline of race-and-media study: describing what media do, and leaving claims about groups to evidence about groups.

## Key Vocabulary

- **stereotype:** A repeated, simplified character type that recurs across many texts and can come to stand for a whole group in audience understanding.
- **typecasting:** The industry practice of repeatedly assigning an actor to the same narrow character type.
- **symbolic annihilation:** George Gerbner's term for the cultural insignificance conveyed when a group is absent from or marginalized within media.
- **race film:** An independent U.S. film made by Black filmmakers for Black audiences in the early twentieth century, offering an alternative to mainstream studio depictions.
- **blaxploitation:** A 1970s cycle of films with Black leads and Black-oriented stories; the NAACP coined the term as a criticism of the films' stereotypes and their treatment of Black audiences.
- **whitewashing:** A contested term critics use when roles based on characters from one cultural background are played by actors from a different background.
- **cultural appropriation:** A contested term for the adoption of elements of one culture by members of another, often more powerful, culture; scholars and commentators disagree about where borrowing becomes exploitation.
- **behind-the-camera diversity:** The demographic composition of directors, writers, and other production roles, studied because creative control shapes what stories get told.

## Eli-10

Race and media is the study of who appears in movies and television, what kinds of roles they play, and who gets to make the creative decisions. A century ago, U.S. movies often showed Black characters played by white actors in makeup, and the same simplified character types appeared again and again: the loyal servant, the comic fool, the dangerous man. Scholars also study absences, because some groups barely appeared at all. Independent Black filmmakers built their own movie industry in response, and later filmmakers kept creating alternatives to the mainstream patterns. Today researchers count who is on screen and who directs and writes, and their numbers show gaps that are still being studied and debated.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a school play performed every year for a decade, where the same two students always play the hero and the villain and everyone else stands in the background. After a while, the audience expects those roles and reads any new performer through them, even though the performers are different people each year.

The analogy has limits. A school play reaches a few hundred people, while media reach millions and are made by industries with budgets, markets, and conventions. Media patterns are the work of many decisions — casting, writing, funding, marketing — so they are studied as institutional patterns, not one person's habit. And media texts circulate for decades, so their patterns can outlive the moment they were made.

## Worked Example

Imagine a student analyzing a twelve-season hospital drama. She counts who appears: characters from one background show up mostly as orderlies or comic relief and never in lead or authority roles, and none of the show's directors share that background. Her claim should read: across twelve seasons, this show assigns characters from this background a narrow range of roles, and no director from that background was hired. That sentence describes a documented pattern in the texts and the crew data. She should not write that this proves people from that background belong in those jobs. The first claim is analysis of media; the second converts a media pattern into a claim about real people.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a stereotype as evidence about real people.** A stereotype is a representation found in media texts; describing it analyzes the texts, and claims about any group require evidence about the group, not about the portrayals.
- **Judging representation from a single film.** Documented patterns gain significance through repetition across many texts, so count the cases and analyze the pattern before interpreting.
- **Presenting contested debates as settled.** Attributed positions from critics, scholars, studios, and defenders are the material of analysis; say who argues what and where the disagreement lies.
- **Confusing on-screen presence with behind-the-camera control.** Characters are not the same as storytellers; track leads and roles separately from directors and writers, as the UCLA report does.
- **Assuming an absence is simply an accident.** Absence and marginalization are describable patterns that scholars interpret — for example, Gerbner's symbolic annihilation — rather than facts about anyone's intent.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stereotype | Typecasting | A stereotype is a repeated simplified character type in the texts; typecasting is the industry practice of repeatedly assigning actors to such types. |
| On-screen representation | Behind-the-camera control | On-screen representation is who appears and in what roles; behind-the-camera control is who directs, writes, and decides, which shapes what stories get made. |
| Describing a representation pattern | Claiming what a group is like | Describing a pattern analyzes media texts; claiming what a group is like makes a claim about real people that requires evidence beyond the media. |

## Key Takeaway

Racial representation is studied as patterns — who appears, in what roles, and who tells the stories — and describing those patterns is not the same as making claims about any group's nature.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In media studies, what is a stereotype in the context of racial representation?**  
   A. A repeated, simplified character type that recurs across many texts and can come to stand for a whole group in audience understanding  
   B. A camera technique that exaggerates a character's physical size  
   C. A legal classification used by the film industry  
   D. A unique character who appears in exactly one film  
   **Answer:** A. A stereotype is a recurring, simplified character type that scholars study as a pattern in media texts; the other options name different things.

2. **A studio adapts a graphic novel whose characters come from a culture modeled on a real-world region, and critics object that none of the lead actors share that heritage, while the studio calls the casting a creative decision. What is the most careful description?**  
   A. The studio's defense proves the critics' objections are invalid  
   B. The critics' objections prove the adaptation is legally prohibited  
   C. The exchange is a contested casting debate; the objections and the defense are attributed positions, not settled facts  
   D. Casting choices have no documented connection to debates about representation  
   **Answer:** C. Casting debates are documented and contested, so analysis attributes positions to each side rather than declaring a winner.

3. **According to the UCLA Hollywood Diversity Report 2025, Black, Indigenous, and people of color were 44.3 percent of the U.S. population in 2024. What did the report find about their share of theatrical film leads?**  
   A. People of color held a majority of lead roles  
   B. Lead roles matched the population share exactly  
   C. The report did not measure lead roles  
   D. People of color held about one quarter of lead roles, below their population share  
   **Answer:** D. The report found people of color held 25.2 percent of theatrical film leads in 2024, below their 44.3 percent population share.

4. **A media scholar observes that across a decade of top-grossing films, characters from one background appear only as comic relief or not at all. Which concept from communication research best matches this observation?**  
   A. Color grading  
   B. Symbolic annihilation  
   C. Continuity editing  
   D. Diegetic sound  
   **Answer:** B. Gerbner's phrase symbolic annihilation describes the insignificance conveyed when a group is absent from or marginalized within media.

5. **A student writes: 'In early U.S. cinema, Black characters were often played by white actors in blackface, and film scholars have catalogued recurring stock types such as the loyal servant and the comic simpleton.' Which statement best evaluates this sentence?**  
   A. It describes documented patterns of representation without claiming the portrayals reveal what any real-world group is like  
   B. It proves that every early U.S. film used blackface  
   C. It claims the stock types accurately described real people  
   D. It proves how all audience members responded  
   **Answer:** A. The sentence reports documented practices and scholarly catalogues — analysis of media texts, not claims about real people.
