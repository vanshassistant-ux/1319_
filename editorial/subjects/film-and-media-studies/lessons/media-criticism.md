# Media Criticism

## In 30 Seconds

Media criticism is the practice of analyzing and evaluating media works: how they are made, what they mean, and how well they work. It differs from reviewing. A review is timely consumer guidance — should you see this now? — while criticism is sustained analysis of meaning and value. Critics describe what they observe, analyze how the parts relate, interpret what the work says, and evaluate it with reasons. Strong criticism is an argued judgment, not a verdict, and it is only as good as its evidence.

## Why This Matters

Criticism sharpens how we consume media. It gives us vocabulary for describing what a film or series does, questions for examining craft and meaning, and reasons for preferring one work over another. It also makes us better readers of reviews, ratings, and online hot takes: we can tell when a claim is supported by evidence and when it is merely taste dressed up as fact. Because critics help guide attention and public debate, understanding their practice improves our own judgment — whether we are writing an essay about a film, discussing a show with friends, or deciding which critical voices to trust. The habit of describing, analyzing, interpreting, and evaluating with reasons transfers to every medium we encounter.

## Learning Objectives

- Define media criticism as sustained analysis and evaluation of a work's meaning, form, and value.
- Distinguish criticism from review by purpose, timing, and audience.
- Identify the objects of criticism: formal craft, narrative, representation, ideology, and audience effects.
- Name and attribute the major critical approaches: formalist, ideological, feminist, and audience-oriented.
- Apply a description-analysis-interpretation-evaluation method to a described media work.
- Evaluate a critical claim's fairness, evidence, and disclosed conflicts of interest.

## The College Version

### Criticism and review are different practices

Media criticism is the sustained study, interpretation, and evaluation of a media work: what it means, how its structure and style produce that meaning, and how it compares with other works in its genre or history. The University of Vermont's film-studies guide describes criticism as offering interpretation of meaning, analysis of structure and style, judgment of worth by comparison with other films, and an estimation of likely effect on viewers. A movie review, by contrast, is consumer-oriented. Published around a film's release, it emphasizes recommendation over analysis: it explains the premise, weighs merits and flaws, and often ends in a star rating. Criticism may appear years after a release and is usually longer and more complex. Reviews can contain real analysis, and criticism often reaches a verdict, but their jobs differ. A review answers, "Should you spend your evening on this now?" Criticism asks, "How does this work, and what is it worth?"

### What critics examine

Good criticism says which object it is addressing. Formal craft covers how cinematography, editing, sound, performance, and design shape the work; MIT's viewing guide offers a checklist — camera distance and movement, editing and transitions, lighting, mise-en-scène, sound, storytelling — as a starting inventory. Narrative concerns how a work organizes and discloses story information. Representation concerns how a work portrays groups, identities, and social roles. Ideology concerns the values, assumptions, and power relations embedded in the work; media messages can range from overt statements to vague expressions of cultural values, and cultures are not monolithic. Audience effects concern what a work might do for or to viewers — a question researchers genuinely disagree about. Keeping these objects separate keeps claims honest: a statement about editing is not automatically a statement about ideology, and a claim about a work's values is not a claim about what viewers actually felt.

### Frameworks critics use

Formalist criticism attends closely to form and craft — how images, editing, and structure create meaning. Its roots run deep: André Bazin and the writers of Cahiers du Cinéma, including later directors François Truffaut and Jean-Luc Godard, and, in later academic film studies, David Bordwell and Kristin Thompson. Ideological criticism examines the values and power relations embedded in a work and asks whose interests its worldview serves. Such readings are contested: some critics praised Birth of a Nation's narrative complexity while others were outraged by its openly racist attitudes, and some critics have labeled strongly argued documentaries propagandistic. Feminist criticism, informed by feminist film theory, asks how a work represents gender and what assumptions about viewers it carries. Audience-oriented criticism studies how viewers interpret and respond to works; it reflects research that audiences are not passive — the direct-effects model of the 1920s and 1930s was challenged by studies such as the 1940 People's Choice research. These are lenses, not verdicts: a framework explains the questions a critic asks, not the answers.

### The critic's toolkit: a four-stage method

Description reports what is observable: the shots, cuts, sounds, and events, stated as neutrally as possible. Analysis identifies how the parts relate: how shot lengths interact, how a sound bridges a cut, how a scene answers a question the opening raised. Interpretation proposes what the work means — what its patterns suggest about its subject, characters, or world. Evaluation judges the work with reasons: whether the choices serve the meaning described, and how the work compares with others like it. These stages mirror the guide's four components: interpretation of meaning, analysis of structure and style, judgment of worth, and estimation of effect on viewers. Consider an invented short film, Northbound, about a night-shift courier. The critic notes the long takes and the recurring radio static (description); observes that every cut away from the courier lands on a phone notification (analysis); argues the film treats waiting as labor (interpretation); and judges that the restraint suits the theme, though the final scene under-explains the choice (evaluation). Each stage must be checkable: another viewer should be able to confirm the description while still disagreeing with the evaluation.

### The role and limits of criticism

Critics guide attention and debate. Reviews appear before most people have seen a film, and research finds that critics' judgments can influence a film's early box-office performance. But criticism does not control outcomes: films that receive a lousy critical reception can still do extremely well when marketing creates enough hype. Its limits follow from its nature. Taste is not universal, and a critic's judgment is shaped by values, experience, and context. Two thoughtful critics can disagree about the same pacing without either lacking evidence. Criticism is best treated as an invitation to think, not a final verdict.

### The ethics of criticism

Because criticism is argument, its ethics are the ethics of honest argument. The Society of Professional Journalists organizes professional ethics around four principles: seek truth and report it, minimize harm, act independently, and be accountable. For a critic these become practical duties. Fairness means representing the work accurately — describing what is actually on screen, not what you assumed — and engaging the strongest version of what the work attempts. Independence means disclosing conflicts: if you worked on the film, if the studio advertises in your outlet, or if you are close to the director, say so, because an undisclosed relationship makes judgment suspect. Accountability means standing behind your reasons and correcting errors. A critic who engages the work honestly can be wrong; a critic who engages something else — a disliked genre, an imagined audience, a grudge — has stopped doing criticism.

## Key Vocabulary

- **media criticism:** The sustained analysis and evaluation of a media work's meaning, form, and value, argued with evidence rather than delivered as a verdict.
- **review:** Timely, consumer-oriented writing about a new release that weighs a work's merits and flaws to help readers decide whether to see it.
- **analysis:** The stage of criticism that identifies how a work's parts relate, such as how shots, sounds, and scenes connect.
- **interpretation:** A reasoned proposal about what a work means, based on its observable patterns and its context.
- **evaluation:** A judged assessment of a work's worth, supported by reasons and by comparison with similar works.
- **formalist criticism:** An approach that examines how a work's form and craft — images, editing, sound, structure — create meaning.
- **ideological criticism:** An approach that examines the values, assumptions, and power relations embedded in a media work.
- **feminist criticism:** An approach informed by feminist theory that asks how a work represents gender and what assumptions about viewers it carries.
- **audience-oriented criticism:** An approach that studies how viewers interpret, respond to, and make use of media works.
- **conflict of interest:** A personal or professional relationship that could unfairly influence a critic's judgment and should be disclosed.

## Eli-10

Media criticism is thinking carefully about a movie or show and explaining your thinking. You look at what you actually see and hear, notice how the pieces fit together, say what you think it means, and then say whether it works — with reasons. A review tells people whether a new film is worth seeing this week. Criticism digs deeper and can happen any time, about anything on screen. You do not need to be an expert to start; you need to pay attention, take notes, and be willing to change your mind when the evidence points elsewhere.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of two guides at a museum. One stands at the entrance of a new exhibition and tells visitors whether it is worth their ticket — that is a reviewer. The other walks with you through the rooms, points out how the lighting shapes each piece, connects the pieces to each other and to the artist's other work, and explains what the exhibition is trying to do — that is a critic. Both are useful; they are doing different jobs.

The analogy has limits. Media works are watched over time, with sound, editing, and narrative, not hung in rooms. And a film critic's walk-through is written in words, so critics must choose what to describe and may miss what matters to you. Criticism is one careful reading, never the only one.

## Worked Example

Invented example: The Last Bus, a 98-minute drama about a night bus driver. A critic applies the four-stage method. Description: the film holds long takes of the empty bus, the driver's radio crackles between stops, and there is no music. Analysis: every time the driver reaches for the radio, the film cuts to a passenger's face, and the pattern repeats each night of the week shown. Interpretation: the film suggests that connection on a night shift is fragile and mostly imagined. Evaluation: the restraint serves the loneliness at the film's center, though the final scene, in which the driver resigns without explanation, asks more of viewers than the earlier pattern prepares them for. Each claim can be checked against the film itself.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling any piece of writing about media "criticism" when it is actually a review.** Check the job: a review is timely consumer guidance; criticism is sustained analysis of meaning and value. The same person can write both.
- **Skipping description and jumping straight to a verdict.** State what is observable first. A judgment without a checkable description is an opinion without evidence.
- **Treating personal dislike as analysis.** Dislike is a starting point, not an argument. Identify what the work does and why you respond that way before evaluating it.
- **Treating disagreement with a critic as proof that the critic is wrong.** Criticism is argued interpretation; two critics can observe the same pacing and evaluate it differently. Assess the reasons, not just the conclusion.
- **Assuming claims about ideology or audience effects are facts about the work or its viewers.** Ideological readings are interpretations, and effects claims are contested. Attribute them as such and keep them separate from what is observable.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Criticism | Review | Criticism offers sustained analysis of meaning and value and can appear any time; a review is timely consumer guidance around a release, emphasizing recommendation over analysis. |
| Analysis | Interpretation | Analysis explains how parts of a work relate; interpretation proposes what the work means, building on that analysis. |
| Formalist criticism | Ideological criticism | Formalist criticism examines how form and craft create meaning; ideological criticism examines the values and power relations embedded in a work. |

## Key Takeaway

Media criticism is argued analysis of meaning and value: describe what is observable, analyze how parts relate, interpret what the work means, and evaluate with reasons — honestly, and with conflicts disclosed.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best captures the working distinction between criticism and review?**
   A. A review offers sustained analysis of a film's place in cinema history
   B. Criticism is consumer guidance about whether to spend money on a new release
   C. Criticism offers sustained analysis of meaning and value, while a review offers timely consumer guidance
   D. A review is always longer and more complex than criticism
   **Answer:** C. Criticism studies a work's meaning, structure, and value, while a review is consumer-oriented guidance published around a release.

2. **A film scholar publishes an essay arguing that a director's long takes change the meaning of a 1980s drama, thirty years after its release. This work is best described as:**
   A. A review, because it discusses a film that has been released
   B. Criticism, because it offers sustained interpretation and analysis rather than purchase advice
   C. Advertising, because it appears long after the release date
   D. Audience research, because it measures how viewers responded
   **Answer:** B. Sustained interpretation of structure and meaning published years after release fits criticism, which is longer, more analytic, and not tied to a film's opening.

3. **A critic writes: "The film cuts away from the courier exactly when a phone notification arrives, and this pattern repeats eleven times." Which stage of the critic's toolkit does that sentence mainly represent?**
   A. Description, because it reports an observable pattern in the editing
   B. Evaluation, because it judges whether the film succeeds
   C. Reviewing, because it recommends the film to readers
   D. Ideology, because it reveals the film's values
   **Answer:** A. Reporting what is observable — the repeated cut to a notification — is description, the first stage of the method.

4. **One critic praises a comedy's chaotic pacing as deliberate satire; another calls the same pacing sloppy editing. What does this disagreement best illustrate?**
   A. That one of the critics is fabricating evidence
   B. That reviews are always more accurate than criticism
   C. That the film has no observable editing at all
   D. That evaluation is argued interpretation shaped by taste and context
   **Answer:** D. Both critics can observe the same pacing and still evaluate it differently, which is why criticism is argument rather than verdict.

5. **A critic who worked as an unpaid consultant on a documentary reviews the film without mentioning that work. Which ethical principle of professional journalism does this most directly violate?**
   A. Seek truth and report it, because the critic should have interviewed the director
   B. Minimize harm, because documentaries should not be reviewed
   C. Act independently, because the undisclosed relationship is a conflict of interest
   D. Be accountable, because the critic must apologize for the film's quality
   **Answer:** C. Independence requires critics to avoid undisclosed relationships that could color judgment; a consultant reviewing the film they consulted on is a conflict of interest that should be disclosed.
