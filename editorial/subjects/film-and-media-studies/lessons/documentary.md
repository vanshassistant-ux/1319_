# Documentary

## In 30 Seconds

Documentary is nonfiction filmmaking: a film that draws on real people, events, and recorded material and makes a claim about the world. Camera-made images carry special weight because they record what stood before the lens, yet selection, staging, and editing shape every documentary, so nonfiction film is interpretation as much as record. Bill Nichols' modes — expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, performative — offer a vocabulary for describing those choices, and representing real people raises questions of consent, harm, and power.

## Why This Matters

Documentaries are where many viewers meet real-world issues on screen, and they carry unusual authority: audiences trust a film that shows "actual footage." Understanding how that authority is built — and where it breaks down — lets you watch nonfiction critically instead of either believing everything or dismissing everything. The same tools apply to true-crime series, nature films, personal video essays, and social-media clips that claim to show reality. For makers, the mode vocabulary and the ethics questions are practical: they shape how you plan interviews, handle consent, and decide how visible you are in your own film.

## Learning Objectives

- Define documentary as nonfiction filmmaking that shapes and interprets factual material.
- Explain why indexical images, actual footage, and testimony give documentaries a claim to the real, and why selection, staging, and editing complicate that claim.
- Distinguish the five Nichols modes — expository, observational, participatory, reflexive, and performative — as an analytic typology.
- Apply the mode vocabulary to a described nonfiction film and identify the strategy in use.
- Analyze the ethical questions of consent, harm, and power raised by representing real people.
- Distinguish documentary from fiction film and from news reporting, and place a work on the spectrum from observational to essayistic.

## The College Version

### The documentary claim to the real

Documentary is commonly defined as nonfiction filmmaking: a motion picture that shapes and interprets factual material, typically for education, information, or entertainment. The label is recent relative to the practice — John Grierson, a Scottish educator, adapted the word "documentary" from the French "documentaire" in the mid-1920s — yet nonfiction-style films had been made since the earliest years of cinema. What gives documentary its distinctive authority is its tie to the real. A film image is indexical: it is produced by light reflecting off whatever actually stood before the camera, so the image works as a trace of that moment. Documentaries build on this by using actual footage, recordings of real events; testimony, people speaking about their own experience; and archival material. This is why the documentary tradition has contributed so much to realism in film: viewers reasonably treat "actual footage" as evidence that something happened.

### Why the claim is complicated: selection, staging, editing

The same facts that give documentary its power also limit it. A camera records only what a filmmaker chooses to point it at, and a finished film is built from selections: which hours of footage survive, which interviews appear, in what order, with what music. Filmmakers may also stage or arrange events for the camera. Even the most hands-off approaches are constructed. Cinema vérité — the French "truth cinema" of the 1960s — aimed at naturalness by recording people in everyday situations with authentic dialogue, yet its own method involved taping conversations first, then filming visuals to fit, then choosing material in the cutting room. Selection and editing are not flaws in documentary; they are the craft. The consequence is that a documentary is an interpretation of the real world, not a transparent copy of it. The image says "this happened"; the film as a whole says "this is what it means."

### Nichols' modes: an analytic typology

To describe how nonfiction films handle the gap between record and interpretation, the film scholar Bill Nichols developed a typology of documentary modes — recurring strategies a film uses to position itself toward its subject and audience. Five modes are especially useful. Expository documentaries speak directly to the viewer, often through authoritative commentary (the "voice-of-God" narrator) that advances an explicit argument; images typically illustrate the argument. Observational documentaries try to watch subjects with minimal intervention, letting long takes of unstaged behavior carry the film. Participatory documentaries make the filmmaker's presence visible — interviews, questions, shared scenes — acknowledging that filming can change what happens. Reflexive documentaries draw attention to their own construction, asking viewers to notice that they are watching a representation. Performative documentaries foreground subjective, personal experience, often blending testimony, emotion, and experiment. The modes are an analytic typology, not a quality ranking: a fine film can be expository, and an observational film is not automatically more truthful. Many documentaries mix modes, and the labels are best used to describe strategies you can point to.

### The ethics of representing real people

Because documentaries represent real people rather than actors, they raise ethical questions that fiction mostly avoids. Consent: does the subject understand how they will appear, and can they meaningfully agree? Harm: could the finished film damage a subject's safety, privacy, or reputation, even if the footage is accurate? Power: the filmmaker controls the camera, the editing, and the story, and that asymmetry is part of the participatory mode itself — the filmmaker keeps the camera and, with it, a degree of power and control over events. A film can be factually true and still do real harm, so documentary practice treats informed consent and care for subjects as professional responsibilities rather than formalities.

### Fiction, news, and the documentary spectrum

Documentary sits between two neighbors. Fiction film invents its world: actors, sets, and scripted events make no promise that what you see happened. News reporting works under journalistic conventions of timeliness, verification, and neutral reporting; it aims to report, though its standards are a sibling topic. Documentary typically runs longer than a news report and openly shapes and interprets its material — which is why a strongly argued documentary can draw criticism that it has crossed from persuasion toward propaganda, a contested line and a sibling topic. Within documentary itself, approaches form a spectrum. At one end, observational work tries to minimize the filmmaker's footprint and let reality unfold. In the middle, expository and participatory films organize and argue. At the far end, essayistic documentary is openly personal: the filmmaker's reflection, memory, and argument are as much the subject as the world is. The spectrum helps you ask, of any nonfiction film, where it sits and what it asks you to trust.

## Key Vocabulary

- **documentary:** Nonfiction filmmaking that shapes and interprets factual material about the real world.
- **indexical image:** A camera-made picture produced by light reflecting off what stood before the lens, so the image records that moment as a trace.
- **actual footage:** Recorded images of real events, used in a documentary as evidence that something happened.
- **testimony:** Spoken accounts by real people about their own experience, used as evidence in a nonfiction film.
- **expository mode:** A documentary strategy that addresses the viewer directly through commentary and advances an explicit argument.
- **observational mode:** A documentary strategy that aims to watch subjects with minimal intervention by the filmmaker.
- **participatory mode:** A documentary strategy in which the filmmaker's presence and interaction with subjects are visible on screen.
- **reflexive mode:** A documentary strategy that draws attention to the film's own construction as a representation.
- **performative mode:** A documentary strategy that foregrounds subjective, personal experience.
- **essayistic documentary:** A personal nonfiction film in which the filmmaker's reflection and argument shape the subject.

## Eli-10

A documentary is a movie made from real life. The camera records what was really in front of it, and real people tell their stories, so a documentary can show you something true — a river flooding, a team practicing before dawn, a neighbor describing how her family came to this town. But making a documentary means choosing. Someone decides which moments to film, which interviews to keep, which order to put things in, and whether to appear on camera or hide behind the camera. Those choices turn raw reality into a story with a point of view. Knowing that does not mean documentaries are fake. It means they are made, and asking who made which choice is part of watching them. Different documentaries make different choices: some just watch, some explain, some let the filmmaker join in, and some show you the machinery of filmmaking itself.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a documentary as a photograph album of a family trip, assembled by one person. The camera recorded what actually happened — the same beach, the same faces — but the album's owner picked which photos to keep, arranged them in an order, wrote the captions, and left themselves out of most pictures. The album is a true record and also a story with an opinion.

The analogy has limits. An album is static and belongs to one person's memory. A documentary moves, has timing and sound, and is often collaborative, and its subjects are real people with their own stake in how they appear. The camera can also change what people do in ways a photo album cannot, which is why consent, harm, and the filmmaker's power matter in documentary in ways they never do for a scrapbook.

## Worked Example

Imagine a short documentary about the night shift at a city bus depot. The expository version opens with a narrator: "Every night, two hundred buses are checked, cleaned, and refueled before sunrise." Interviews with a supervisor and a voice-over carry the argument that the depot keeps the city moving. The observational version shows the same depot with no narration: a mechanic walking a flashlight along a tire, a driver pouring coffee, real time passing without comment. The participatory version includes the director asking questions on camera, and in one scene the crew's jokes stop when the camera arrives — visible evidence that filming changed the moment. The same place, three different claims about what "the depot" is. Naming the mode is the first step; explaining what each version asks you to trust is the analysis.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating "documentary" as a guarantee of truth.** Camera-made images are traces of what was recorded, but the finished film is shaped by selection, staging, and editing; ask what the film chose to include rather than assuming it shows "everything."
- **Using the modes as a ranking, assuming observational means honest and expository means biased.** The modes are analytic labels for recognizable strategies; any mode can be used carefully or carelessly, and a film can mix modes.
- **Calling a documentary propaganda whenever it argues a point.** Argument is a normal part of nonfiction film; the contested line between persuasion and propaganda is a question to analyze, not a label to apply to any opinionated film.
- **Treating documentary subjects as characters in a story rather than real people.** Documentary subjects have stakes in how they appear; consent, potential harm, and the filmmaker's power are part of the ethics of the form.
- **Confusing documentary with news reporting.** News works under journalistic conventions of timeliness and verification; documentary typically runs longer, shapes and interprets material, and may openly argue a position.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Documentary film vs. Fiction film:** Documentary draws on real people and recorded events and claims a tie to the actual world; fiction invents its world with actors and scripted events, though both are shaped by filmmaking choices.
- **Documentary vs. News reporting:** News works under journalistic conventions of timeliness, verification, and neutral reporting; documentary works at greater length and openly shapes and interprets its material, and may argue a position.
- **Observational documentary vs. Essayistic documentary:** Observational work minimizes the filmmaker's footprint and lets unstaged behavior carry the film; essayistic work puts the filmmaker's reflection, memory, and argument at the center of the subject.

## Key Takeaway

Documentary earns its authority from recording the real world, but every nonfiction film is shaped by selection, staging, and editing — so watch for the choices, not just the footage.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which person is credited with adapting the term "documentary" in the mid-1920s from the French word documentaire?**
   - A. John Grierson
   - B. Robert Flaherty
   - C. Dziga Vertov
   - D. Jean-Luc Godard
   - **Answer: A.** Britannica credits the Scottish educator John Grierson with adapting "documentary" from the French "documentaire" in the mid-1920s. Flaherty made early nonfiction films but is not credited with coining the term, and the other two names are filmmakers unconnected with the coinage.

2. **A film shows a director on camera asking a shopkeeper questions, and the shopkeeper's answers change as the crew's presence becomes obvious. Which Nichols mode does this best illustrate?**
   - A. Expository
   - B. Observational
   - C. Participatory
   - D. Reflexive
   - **Answer: C.** Participatory documentaries make the filmmaker's presence visible and acknowledge that filming can affect events, so the crew's visible influence on the shopkeeper fits the mode. Expository films argue through commentary, observational films minimize the filmmaker's footprint, and reflexive films draw attention to their own construction.

3. **Which statement best captures both the power and the limits of documentary images?**
   - A. They are immune to bias because cameras record mechanically.
   - B. They are no different from animation because both are constructed.
   - C. They carry weight as traces of what stood before the camera, yet selection, staging, and editing shape every film.
   - D. They are reliable evidence only when filmed in color.
   - **Answer: C.** Camera-made images are indexical traces of what was recorded, which is why documentaries carry authority, but every documentary is also shaped by selection, staging, and editing, so it remains an interpretation. The other options deny one side of that tension.

4. **A filmmaker wants viewers to see a community center exactly as it is — no narration, no interviews, no visible crew. Which approach best matches that goal?**
   - A. Expository
   - B. Performative
   - C. Essayistic
   - D. Observational
   - **Answer: D.** Observational documentaries aim to watch subjects with minimal intervention, so no narration, interviews, or visible crew fits the mode. Expository films add commentary, while performative and essayistic films foreground personal presence and reflection.

5. **A documentary about a student's recovery includes footage the student later says made her life harder and that she did not understand she was agreeing to. Which ethical consideration does this raise most directly?**
   - A. The need for higher ticket prices
   - B. The risk of harm to real subjects and the limits of consent
   - C. The rule that documentaries must be entertaining
   - D. The requirement that every subject be paid
   - **Answer: B.** Representing real people raises questions of consent — whether the subject understood how they would appear — and of harm, because an accurate film can still damage someone's safety or reputation. The other options are not ethical considerations raised by this situation.
