# Shot Types

## In 30 Seconds

Shot types are labels for the scale and framing of a subject in a moving-image shot. Common labels include establishing or extreme wide shot, long shot, full shot, medium shot, close-up, and extreme close-up. Their boundaries are conventions, not universal measurements. A shot type tells you how much of a subject or setting is visible; it does not automatically tell you what the shot means. Describe the scale and surrounding sequence before interpreting its possible effect.

## Why This Matters

Shot-type vocabulary gives viewers a compact way to notice changes in visual information. A wide view may establish a space or show relationships among people, while a close view may make a face, object, or gesture easier to examine. Comparing shot scale across a sequence can show how attention shifts without treating any one size as a fixed emotional code. These habits help with close reading, media literacy, storyboarding, and conversations among people making or analyzing moving images.

## Learning Objectives

- Define shot types as conventions for describing the scale and framing of visible material.
- Identify common labels from establishing or wide views through close-ups and extreme close-ups.
- Explain why shot-type labels have flexible boundaries rather than exact universal measurements.
- Distinguish shot size from camera angle, camera movement, lens choice, and editing.
- Analyze how a change in shot scale can redirect attention across a sequence.

## The College Version

### Shot types describe scale, not a fixed meaning

A shot is one continuous camera capture of action. A shot type describes the scale of a person, object, or setting as it appears within that capture. It is a practical vocabulary for answering a basic question: how much does the viewer see? MIT OpenCourseWare's viewing guide names long or establishing shots and close-ups among the camera distances worth noticing. The University of Arkansas open textbook lists a related set of labels: extreme close-up, close-up, medium close-up, medium shot, medium long shot, long shot, and extreme long shot. These labels help a crew communicate and help an analyst compare images.

The labels are conventions, not a universal ruler. Different production traditions, textbooks, aspect ratios, and subjects can change where one label seems to end and another begins. A person framed from the waist up is often called a medium shot, while the Arkansas text describes a medium long shot as typically showing a character from around the knees up. Those examples are useful reference points, not requirements to impose on every image. When a boundary is uncertain, describe the visible scale precisely: “the frame contains the figure from waist to head, with little surrounding space.”

Shot type does not determine meaning by itself. A close-up can make a facial expression legible, isolate an object, or withhold the larger setting. A very wide view can establish a location, map the positions of several people, or make a single figure small relative to the environment. What an image does depends on what preceded it, what follows it, what is heard, what has been established in the story, and what the frame includes or excludes. Labels enable observation; they do not serve as a dictionary of permanent emotional translations.

### A flexible family of common shot-scale labels

An **establishing shot** commonly supplies orientation to a setting, although an establishing function can be performed at several scales. It may be a very wide view that shows a building, landscape, street, or room before more focused action begins. An **extreme long shot** or extreme wide shot typically makes figures small relative to the surrounding space. A **long shot** or wide shot may still show a full person while retaining substantial environment. These distinctions are relative: a wide view of a small interior cannot contain the same amount of visible space as a wide view of a city square.

A **full shot** usually shows a person's body from head to foot, with some environment. A **medium long shot** often frames a person from about the knees upward. A **medium shot** commonly frames from around the waist upward. A **medium close-up** moves nearer, often around the chest or shoulders. A **close-up** makes a face, hand, or important object occupy a larger part of the frame. An **extreme close-up** isolates a small detail such as an eye, mouth, key, or written word. These descriptions describe scale; they should not be treated as strict body-part rules. A close-up of an object and a close-up of a face provide different kinds of information even though both are close views.

Ask what becomes newly visible or newly hidden at this scale. A full shot may reveal a character's gesture and clothing while leaving facial detail less clear. A close-up may make a glance or an object legible while removing other people and the setting from view. A sequence can move between scales to orient viewers, concentrate attention, compare people, delay information, or establish a relationship between a figure and a place. Evidence for such a claim lies in the transition and its context, not in the label alone.

### Shot size is related to, but different from, other camera choices

Shot size describes apparent scale in the frame. It is not the same as **camera angle**, which describes the camera's relation above, below, or at the level of the subject. It is not **camera movement**, which changes framing over time through pans, tilts, tracking moves, or other motion. It is not **editing**, which selects and orders separate shots. A close-up can be held still, reached through a moving camera, or introduced by a cut; those are different formal facts that can work together.

Shot size is also not a complete description of lens choice. The Arkansas text notes that focal length affects angle of view and magnification, while framing depends on what has been included within the image. A filmmaker can change the apparent scale of a subject by moving the camera, changing focal length, or changing both. At an introductory level, accurately describe the onscreen result without claiming to know a lens from the finished image alone. Say “the figure fills more of the frame” rather than guessing a precise technical setup that the image cannot verify.

Mise-en-scène and sound matter too. A close view of an object may emphasize it only because the story and soundtrack have prepared the viewer to notice it. A wide shot may reveal a meaningful spatial pattern because of how performers, props, and setting were arranged before the camera. Keep categories distinct enough to notice each contribution and connected enough to explain the sequence's overall effect.

### How to analyze a change of shot scale

Start with a short sequence. Identify each shot's scale in plain language, using conventional labels only when they clarify the observation. Then record what information each shot provides: location, body position, facial detail, an object's condition, distance between people, or an offscreen area that is no longer visible. Compare the transition. Does the cut or camera move cause a wider view to become closer? Does a close view withhold the larger setting until a later reveal? Does the sequence alternate comparable scales for two people, or give one person a closer view than the other?

Imagine a scene in which two students wait outside an office. The sequence begins with a wide view that shows them at opposite ends of a hallway. A medium shot places both students around a closed door. A close-up then isolates one student's hand turning a folded note. The analysis can state that the scales first orient the viewer to the distance between the students, then bring them into the same local space, then direct attention to the note. It cannot conclude from scale alone that either student is dishonest or that all viewers will experience the scene as suspenseful. Further interpretation should draw on dialogue, performance, narrative information, and the rest of the sequence.

Rewatching helps. MIT's guide recommends writing and sketching while watching so that changes in composition and movement can be tracked. On a second viewing, check whether the close-up returns, whether the wide setting later changes its meaning, and whether sound or editing alters the apparent significance of the scale. This process makes a claim accountable: it connects what the viewer can see to a qualified explanation of how attention is organized.

## Key Vocabulary

- **shot type:** A conventional label describing the visible scale and framing within a camera shot.
- **establishing shot:** A shot that orients viewers to a setting, situation, or spatial relationship.
- **extreme long shot:** A very wide view in which a figure may appear small relative to surrounding space.
- **long shot:** A wide framing that commonly shows a full figure along with substantial environment.
- **medium shot:** A middle-distance framing often showing a person from around the waist upward.
- **close-up:** A framing that makes a face, object, or detail occupy a large part of the image.
- **extreme close-up:** A very tight framing that isolates a small facial feature, object, or detail.
- **scale:** The apparent size of a person, object, or setting within a frame.

## Eli-10

Shot types are names for how close the movie lets you get. Imagine looking at a playground through different windows. One window shows the whole playground. Another shows one child from head to toe. A smaller window shows only the child's face, and the smallest one shows one hand holding a note. Each window gives you different information. The close window does not automatically mean danger or sadness, and the wide one does not automatically mean loneliness. First notice what you can see at each distance. Then look at the order of the windows to explain where the movie guides your attention.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like changing the crop on a photograph of a picnic. The full picture shows the park and every person. A tighter crop shows one person's expression. A tiny crop shows only a dropped invitation. The subject has not changed, but each crop makes different evidence easier to notice.

The analogy has limits. A film shot is not just a cropped still image. Camera movement, lenses, focus, sound, editing, and the earlier story all shape how a shot works. A label such as close-up describes scale, not a complete meaning.

## Worked Example

A scene opens on an extreme long shot of a bus stop beside an empty road. The next shot is a medium shot of two people waiting several steps apart. A close-up shows one person's cracked phone screen as a bus passes without stopping. Record the scales and visible information before interpreting. The first image provides the larger location, the medium shot makes the spacing between the people clear, and the close-up directs attention to the phone. A reasonable claim is that the sequence moves from place and relationship to a relevant object. The shot sizes alone do not prove why the phone matters or require one emotional response.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating shot-type labels as exact measurements that apply identically in every production.** Use labels as conventions and describe actual visible scale when a boundary is uncertain.
- **Assuming a close-up or wide shot has one permanent emotional meaning.** Explain a shot's possible effect from sequence, narrative context, and other formal elements.
- **Confusing shot scale with angle, movement, lens choice, or editing.** State separately how large the subject appears, where the camera is, whether it moves, and how shots are ordered.
- **Naming a shot size without identifying what becomes visible or hidden.** Connect scale to specific information such as setting, body position, expression, object detail, or spatial relation.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shot type | Camera angle | Shot type describes apparent scale in the frame; camera angle describes the camera's position relative to the subject. |
| Establishing shot | Extreme long shot | Establishing describes an orienting function; extreme long describes a very wide scale. One shot can perform both roles. |
| Close-up | Extreme close-up | A close-up makes a face or object prominent; an extreme close-up isolates an even smaller detail or feature. |

## Key Takeaway

Shot types describe how much of a subject or setting viewers see within a frame. Use their flexible labels to compare visual information across a sequence, then make a qualified claim about attention and meaning instead of assigning a fixed emotion to a shot size.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What do shot-type labels primarily describe?**  
   A. The legal ownership of a film  
   B. The apparent scale and framing of visible material in a shot  
   C. The order in which shots are edited  
   D. The sound level of dialogue  
   **Answer:** B.

2. **Which statement most accurately describes an establishing shot?**  
   A. It always shows a face from very close range  
   B. It is a fixed camera movement from left to right  
   C. It orients viewers to a setting, situation, or spatial relationship and can occur at more than one scale  
   D. It guarantees that the next scene will occur at the same location  
   **Answer:** C.

3. **A sequence moves from a wide classroom view to a medium shot of two students at a desk and then a close-up of a note. What is the strongest first analysis?**  
   A. The close-up proves the note has one universal meaning  
   B. The scales progressively make the room, the students' relation, and then the note easier to examine  
   C. The camera must have used one precise lens throughout  
   D. The wide view means every viewer must feel lonely  
   **Answer:** B.

4. **Which observation distinguishes shot size from camera angle?**  
   A. Shot size asks how large a subject appears in the frame, while angle asks where the camera is positioned relative to that subject  
   B. Shot size and angle are interchangeable names for editing  
   C. Shot size describes sound, while angle describes costume  
   D. A close-up can never be filmed from above or below  
   **Answer:** A.

5. **A figure appears very small against a large landscape. Which description is most careful?**  
   A. It is necessarily an establishing shot and proves the figure is powerless  
   B. It must be a medium shot because the landscape is visible  
   C. It may be an extreme long or very wide view; its likely effect should be interpreted from the sequence rather than from scale alone  
   D. It cannot be analyzed until the production budget is known  
   **Answer:** C.
