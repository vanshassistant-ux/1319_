# News Media

## In 30 Seconds

News is the reporting of recent, significant events. Journalism is the practice of gathering, assessing, creating, and presenting that news for the public. A news story passes through reporting, sourcing, verification, and editing before it reaches you, and it follows professional norms of accuracy, fairness, independence, and accountability. News differs from opinion, entertainment, and propaganda in its aim: to give people verified information they can use, rather than to persuade, amuse, or advocate.

## Why This Matters

Understanding how news is produced changes how you read, watch, and share it. You can tell a news report from an opinion column, notice which events an outlet chose to cover and why, and ask whether a claim was verified before it was published. That matters beyond journalism courses: elections, public health, and local decisions all run on information citizens can trust. The norms of accuracy, fairness, independence, and accountability are also the vocabulary used to evaluate any source, from a city newspaper to a social media feed, and to understand why the news media are so often criticized, and defended, on those same terms.

## Learning Objectives

- Define news as reports of recent, significant events and journalism as the practice of gathering, assessing, creating, and presenting news.
- Distinguish news reports from opinion, entertainment, and propaganda by purpose and labeling.
- Explain the conventional news values of timeliness, significance, proximity, conflict, and human interest, and apply them to a story-selection scenario.
- Explain the history of objectivity as a professional norm and summarize the main critiques of it as a contested debate.
- Describe the news production process from reporting and sourcing through verification and editing, including fact-checking and corrections as norms.
- Analyze a described newsroom decision using the professional norms of accuracy, fairness, independence, and accountability.

## The College Version

### What news is, and what it is not

A working definition used widely in media studies is that news consists of reports of recent, significant events. The American Press Institute defines journalism itself as the activity of gathering, assessing, creating, and presenting news and information, and the product of those activities. What makes journalism different from the rest of the media universe, in the institute's account, is its purpose: to provide people with verified information they can use to make better decisions. That purpose is what separates a news report from its near neighbors. An opinion piece argues a viewpoint, and professional codes require that advocacy and commentary be labeled and kept distinct from straight reporting. An entertainment program aims to amuse. Advertising aims to sell, and propaganda aims to persuade audiences toward a cause or viewpoint. None of those is news, although all of them can appear in the same newspaper, website, or broadcast.

### News values: how editors decide what counts

Because not every recent event can be covered, newsrooms rely on conventional criteria called news values. Journalism training manuals typically test a candidate story by asking whether it is new, whether it is unusual, whether it is interesting or significant, and whether it is about people. The textbook list adds more precise labels: timeliness (how recently the event happened), significance (how much it affects the audience), proximity (how close to home it is), conflict (disputes between people, groups, or institutions), and human interest (stories that touch people's lives even when their broader significance is modest). The News Manual, a widely used free training text, describes closeness and personal impact as factors that make a story stronger, and notes that the classic saying "man bites dog" captures the appeal of the unusual. These are conventions, not laws: different outlets weigh them differently, and a story that is big in one community may be small in another.

### Objectivity: a contested norm with a history

Objectivity is the professional norm most associated with modern journalism, and it is also one of the most contested. Its history matters for understanding what the term was designed to do. Through most of the nineteenth century, many American newspapers were openly partisan. That changed in stages: in 1896, Adolph Ochs bought the failing New York Times and announced it would be clean, dignified, trustworthy, and impartial, and the paper became a model of a neutral reporting voice. Later in the same period, journalists spoke of realism rather than objectivity, the idea that if reporters dug out facts and arranged them, truth would reveal itself. In the 1920s, that confidence broke down. Walter Lippmann and Charles Merz published an influential 1919 critique concluding that cultural assumptions had distorted the New York Times' coverage of the Russian Revolution, and Lippmann argued that journalism needed a common method of testing information. As the American Press Institute summarizes the history, drawing on Bill Kovach and Tom Rosenstiel's The Elements of Journalism, the original concept was that the method is objective, not the journalist: reporters were expected to be biased, and objectivity meant a transparent discipline of verification that would keep those biases from undermining accuracy. The critique runs in several directions. The University of Minnesota open textbook reports wider acceptance that reporting always occurs through a lens of personal experience, culture, and background. Bias research, such as the contested 2005 study by political scientists Tim Groseclose and Jeffrey Milyo that found measurable slant in most of the outlets it examined, feeds the debate. And Kovach and Rosenstiel warn that a neutral voice without verification is a veneer that can make a one-sided report look objective. None of these positions is settled; this lesson presents them as a debate rather than a conclusion.

### How a story gets made: reporting, sourcing, verification, editing

A news story moves from event to publication through recognizable stages. Reporting is the gathering: attending meetings, observing scenes, requesting documents. Sourcing supplies the information: journalists interview people, use original sources when possible, identify sources clearly, and reserve anonymity for sources who might face harm. Verification is the check: codes of ethics require verifying information before releasing it and note that neither speed nor format excuses inaccuracy. Editing is the shaping: editors work with reporters to verify sources and double-check facts, cut and reorganize copy, and place the most important facts first in the inverted pyramid structure that grew out of the telegraph era. Fact-checking and corrections are norms, not afterthoughts. The Society of Professional Journalists' code directs journalists to gather, update, and correct information throughout the life of a story and to acknowledge mistakes and correct them promptly and prominently. News organizations have published apologies for flawed coverage, and fabrication is treated as a career-ending betrayal: the New York Times dismissed reporter Jayson Blair in 2003 and The New Republic fired Stephen Glass in 1998 after fabrication was discovered. The ethics that govern the process are usually summarized as accuracy, fairness, independence, and accountability. The Society of Professional Journalists names them as seek truth and report it, minimize harm, act independently, and be accountable and transparent. The "vital information premise," as the open textbook calls it, grounds these norms in the idea that news media exist to give citizens the accurate and reliable information they need to function in a democracy, including a watchdog role over government and business.

### The news ecosystem today

Modern news flows through a layered ecosystem. Local news organizations cover their own communities, often in a consensus style that serves as a forum for local voices, while national outlets cover events of wider reach, and wire services such as the Associated Press gather reporting and distribute it to member newsrooms. The Associated Press was founded in 1846 as a cooperative of five New York papers that shared the cost of telegraph-delivered news, and the wire model still underpins much of what local outlets publish. The delivery cycle has accelerated in stages: radio carried the first live breaking-news coverage of a presidential election in 1916, the assassination of President Kennedy in 1963 is widely described as the moment round-the-clock television news began, and the internet has made instant digital distribution and news aggregation the default. That speed creates a documented tension between being first and being right: with less time for fact-checking, organizations lean on verification and corrections as the safeguards. The ecosystem is described here in general terms; the economics of ownership and the tools of audience evaluation belong to their own lessons.

## Key Vocabulary

- **news:** Reports of recent, significant events, aimed at informing rather than persuading or entertaining.
- **journalism:** The practice of gathering, assessing, creating, and presenting news and information for the public.
- **news values:** Conventional criteria editors use to judge which events deserve coverage, including timeliness, significance, proximity, conflict, and human interest.
- **objectivity:** A professional norm that calls for a consistent method of testing information rather than claiming the journalist is free of bias; its meaning is contested.
- **verification:** Checking facts and claims against evidence and additional sources before they are published.
- **wire service:** An organization that gathers news and distributes it to many member newsrooms, such as the Associated Press.
- **inverted pyramid:** A story structure that places the most important facts first, followed by supporting detail in order of importance.
- **editorial:** An opinion piece expressing a publication's or writer's view, labeled and separated from news reports; an op-ed is an outside writer's opinion piece.
- **corrections:** Published notices that acknowledge and fix errors in earlier reporting, treated as a professional obligation.
- **watchdog journalism:** Reporting that investigates the actions of government, business, and other institutions on behalf of the public.

## Eli-10

News is information about what just happened and why it matters. Reporters find out what happened, check it, and write it down so you can decide things for yourself. Not everything you see in a news feed is news: a column argues a point of view, a game show entertains, and an ad tries to sell you something. Editors pick stories using familiar tests: is it new, does it matter, is it close to home, is there a conflict, does it touch people's lives? Behind the scenes, a story is checked more than once: sources are named or explained, facts are double-checked, mistakes get corrected. That checking is what separates journalism from rumor. People disagree about whether any report can be fully neutral, and that debate is part of how journalism works too. The answer is not settled, and careful readers keep asking.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of news as a tray of samples at a market: someone has already tasted, checked, and labeled each one, and the label tells you what it is, a sample, an opinion, or an advertisement. Your job is to choose what to try, not to assume everything on the tray is the same thing.

Where the analogy breaks down: a market label is printed by one seller, while a news story is built by many people, reporters, editors, and sources, and its quality is not guaranteed by any single label. A news organization can still get things wrong, which is why corrections and accountability matter.

## Worked Example

A city council votes to raise water rates. A reporter attends the meeting, records the vote, and collects the budget document (reporting). She interviews two residents and a council member and names them in the story (sourcing). She compares the new rate with the old one in the budget to confirm the numbers (verification). Her editor asks her to check one figure with the city clerk and cuts a long quote (editing). The story leads with the rate increase and its effective date, the most important facts first, and the station runs a separately labeled editorial supporting the increase. When a reader finds a wrong date, the outlet publishes a correction.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating every item in a news feed as news.** A feed mixes news, opinion, entertainment, and advertising; check what a piece is labeled and what evidence it offers.
- **Thinking objectivity means a journalist has no beliefs.** The norm, as its historians describe it, calls for a consistent method of testing information; whether any report can be fully neutral is an open debate.
- **Trusting a story because it sounds calm and neutral.** A neutral voice without verification can mask a one-sided report; ask about sources and evidence instead.
- **Assuming corrections mean an outlet is failing.** Prompt, prominent corrections are a professional norm and a sign of accountability, not a sign of collapse.
- **Confusing news values with personal interest.** News values are conventional professional criteria such as timeliness, significance, proximity, conflict, and human interest; a personal fascination is not the same test.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| News report | Opinion piece | A news report presents verified facts with attributed sources; an opinion piece argues a viewpoint and is labeled separately from news. |
| News | Propaganda | News aims to inform with verified information; propaganda aims to persuade audiences toward a cause or viewpoint, and is covered in its own lesson. |
| Wire service | News aggregator | A wire service gathers original reporting and distributes it to member outlets; an aggregator compiles headlines from many outlets without producing its own reporting. |
| Local news | National news | Local news focuses on a community's own events and concerns, often in a consensus style; national news covers events of wider reach, often via wire and network reporting. |
| Timeliness | Significance | Timeliness asks how recent an event is; significance asks how much it affects the audience. |

## Key Takeaway

News is reports of recent, significant events, produced through reporting, sourcing, verification, and editing and held to norms of accuracy, fairness, independence, and accountability; objectivity is a contested norm, not a settled fact.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best fits the working definition of news used in this lesson?**  
   A. Content designed mainly to entertain a large audience  
   B. Reports of recent, significant events  
   C. Any opinion expressed by a prominent person  
   D. Advertising that promotes a product or cause  
   **Answer:** B. News is working defined as reports of recent, significant events; entertainment, opinion, and advertising pursue different aims.

2. **A local station leads its evening bulletin with a fire at a downtown warehouse, reports on a city council vote on water rates, and skips a crash in a town three hundred miles away. Which pair of conventional news values best explains the selection?**  
   A. Timeliness and proximity  
   B. Conflict and human interest  
   C. Significance and entertainment value  
   D. Oddity and opinion  
   **Answer:** A. The fire is recent and close to home, and the distant crash scores low on timeliness for this audience and on proximity; conflict and human interest do not explain skipping the distant crash, and entertainment value and opinion are not conventional news values.

3. **The Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics organizes ethical journalism around four principles. Which set names them?**  
   A. Speed, Sensation, Loyalty, Profit  
   B. Truth, Power, Wealth, Fame  
   C. Seek Truth and Report It; Minimize Harm; Act Independently; Be Accountable and Transparent  
   D. Accuracy, Advocacy, Authority, Advertising  
   **Answer:** C. The SPJ code's four principles are Seek Truth and Report It, Minimize Harm, Act Independently, and Be Accountable and Transparent.

4. **A reporter files a story built on a single unnamed source and writes it in a calm, neutral voice without checking other evidence. Which critique from the objectivity debates applies most directly?**  
   A. The neutral voice proves the story is impartial  
   B. The story should be reclassified as propaganda because all reporting is biased  
   C. The reporter should add more descriptive language to signal balance  
   D. A neutral voice without a discipline of verification can create the appearance of objectivity for an unverified report  
   **Answer:** D. The critique associated with Kovach and Rosenstiel and the American Press Institute holds that the neutral voice is a device, not proof of impartiality, and that without verification it can cover an unverified or one-sided report.

5. **A morning article misstated a school's opening date, and readers have pointed out the error. Which response best matches the correction norms this lesson teaches?**  
   A. Leave the text unchanged so the error draws less attention  
   B. Delete the article without any explanation  
   C. Wait for the school to issue a statement before doing anything  
   D. Acknowledge the mistake and correct it promptly and prominently, explaining the change  
   **Answer:** D. Professional norms require acknowledging mistakes and correcting them promptly and prominently, with a clear explanation; hiding, silently deleting, or waiting for an outside statement is not the norm.
