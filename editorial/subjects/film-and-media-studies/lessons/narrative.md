# Narrative

## In 30 Seconds

Narrative is how a film or video organizes and reveals story information. It includes what happens, what viewers know, what is withheld, and when events are shown. A story may follow a character pursuing a goal through obstacles, but no single template fits every work. Map the events and the order in which viewers learn them before explaining how that arrangement shapes questions, expectations, or connections.

## Why This Matters

Narrative analysis helps viewers distinguish a summary of events from an explanation of how a work tells those events. It clarifies why a scene arrives before another, why a camera stays with one character, why an important fact is delayed, or how a flashback changes what a viewer knows. Plot, performance, editing, image, and sound all contribute to storytelling together rather than narrative being only a list of events.

## Learning Objectives

- Define narrative as the organization and disclosure of story information in a moving-image work.
- Distinguish plot presentation from the broader story relationships and chronology a viewer can infer.
- Identify goals, obstacles, cause-and-consequence links, restricted narration, and broader narration in a described sequence.
- Explain how changes in temporal order can alter when viewers receive information.
- Make a qualified claim about a narrative pattern using specific sequence evidence.

## The College Version

### Narrative organizes events and the information viewers receive

Narrative is not merely a list of events. It is the organization and disclosure of story information: what a work shows, what it withholds, what it repeats, and when viewers are invited to connect those pieces. MIT's viewing guide frames narrative analysis as asking how a story is told and how knowledge is given or withheld. The University of Arkansas open textbook connects narrative structure with characters, goals, obstacles, and the many film-form choices that carry a story.

A useful starting map records events in two ways. First, write the plot presentation: the order in which the film shows events. Second, write the inferred story chronology: the order in which those events appear to happen in the fictional world. The two may match, but they do not have to. A film can show a character opening a locked drawer before showing the earlier conversation that explains why the drawer matters. The presentation order creates a question; the later scene changes the viewer's understanding.

Narrative also depends on how information is delivered. A close view, a cutaway, an overheard line, a point-of-view shot, a musical cue, or a withheld reaction can all change what viewers know at a given moment. These choices overlap with cinematography, editing, sound, and performance, but the narrative question is specific: what information becomes available, to whom, and when?

### Goals, obstacles, and causes provide one useful pattern

Many narratives can be described through a central character or group pursuing a goal while encountering obstacles. The University of Arkansas text presents protagonist, goal, and obstacles as a compact way to recognize a common story pattern. It is useful because it prompts precise questions: What does the character seek? What prevents progress? What decision or event changes the next step? It is not a requirement that every film, episode, documentary, or experimental work organize itself this way.

Cause and consequence matter more than a generic label such as beginning, middle, or end. A character's decision to hide a message can cause another character to search for it, which can cause a later confrontation. To analyze the structure, identify the linked actions and the information that makes each action understandable. Avoid assuming that an obstacle must be a villain or that a protagonist must be admirable. An obstacle can be an environment, a limitation, a social arrangement, competing goals, or missing knowledge.

A plot summary states that events happened. Narrative analysis asks how their arrangement makes a viewer connect them. A scene may establish a goal explicitly through dialogue, or reveal it gradually through repeated behavior and choices. A careful claim remains tied to what the work shows rather than forcing every story into a rigid formula.

### Restriction and ordering shape what viewers can know

Narration may remain comparatively restricted around one character's experience. Viewers then learn information mainly when that character can see, hear, discover, or infer it. Another work may provide a broader range of information by showing multiple locations, following several characters, or revealing something one character does not know. These are differences in the distribution of knowledge, not proof that one approach is more sophisticated.

Temporal order is another narrative choice. A flashback presents an earlier event after a later one has already appeared; a flashforward presents a later event before the sequence returns to an earlier point. The underlying chronology may stay the same, while the order of presentation changes what questions viewers ask. For example, opening with a character reading an apology and then returning to the previous day's disagreement can give the disagreement a different significance than if it were shown first.

When analyzing such a structure, separate observation from inference. State the event order, identify which information is delayed or newly supplied, and compare what viewers could reasonably know before and after the shift. Do not call every non-linear sequence confusing or exciting by definition. The effect depends on the clarity of cues, the surrounding scenes, and the specific relation between events.

### A method for tracing a narrative sequence

Choose a short sequence and make an information chart. In one column, list what viewers see or hear. In a second, note what a central character knows. In a third, note what viewers may know that the character does not. Then mark any revealed goal, obstacle, decision, or new fact. This chart makes it possible to distinguish a change in plot from a change in knowledge.

Imagine a sequence that begins with a teenager arriving at a closed community center. The teenager checks a phone, finds a note taped to the door, and walks toward a nearby bus stop. The next scene shows an earlier conversation in which a friend promised to meet at the center. The visible events establish a goal and obstacle; the later scene supplies context for the note. A supportable claim is that the delayed conversation changes how viewers understand the teenager's search without changing the event chronology.

Finally, connect the narrative pattern to form. Did a cut delay the context? Did a close view hide part of the note? Did a sound heard before a door opens provide information about an offscreen space? Answering these questions produces a film analysis rather than a plot recap.

## Key Vocabulary

- **narrative:** The organization and disclosure of story information through events, characters, and moving-image form.
- **story:** The broader chronology and relationships that viewers infer from the events a work presents.
- **plot:** The selected events and order through which a work presents story information to viewers.
- **protagonist:** A central character or force whose pursuit, choices, or situation helps organize a narrative.
- **obstacle:** A condition, conflict, limitation, or competing goal that complicates movement toward an objective.
- **restricted narration:** A storytelling arrangement that limits viewer knowledge mainly to what one central character can access.
- **flashback:** A presentation of an earlier event after the narrative has already moved to a later point.
- **flashforward:** A presentation of a later event before the narrative returns to an earlier point in chronology.

## Eli-10

Narrative is the way a movie or video gives you a story piece by piece. It chooses what you see first, what you learn later, and what questions you have while watching. You might see a student find a broken key before you learn why the key matters. Or you might stay with one character and only know what that person knows. List what happened and when you learned about it. Then look for goals, problems, choices, and new clues.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a friend telling you about a surprising school day. They could start with the big surprise, then explain what happened earlier, or tell each event in order. The day itself did not change, but the order changes what you wonder about.

The analogy has limits. A film can show close-ups, move between places, use music, and let viewers see things no character sees. Those visual and sound choices help control information in ways an ordinary spoken story may not.

## Worked Example

Imagine a scene that begins with Kai arriving at a darkened art room after school. Kai sees an empty display stand and opens a message saying, 'Meet me at the side entrance.' The next scene shows an earlier moment when Kai promised to bring a sculpture to the display. List the presented order and then the inferred chronological order. A supportable claim is that the later scene supplies information that changes how viewers understand the empty stand and message. It does not prove who sent the message or require a single emotional reaction.

## Common Mistakes

- **Summarizing events without explaining how information is ordered or withheld.** Map what viewers learn in each scene and identify how the presentation changes the understanding of events.
- **Treating every story as a mandatory three-act structure.** Use goals, obstacles, and causal links as questions for analysis, not as a formula every work must satisfy.
- **Confusing story chronology with plot presentation.** Make separate lists for the inferred event order and the order in which the work gives events to viewers.
- **Calling a flashback automatically confusing or exciting.** Describe what information arrives later and compare it with the questions established by the earlier presentation.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Story | Plot | Story concerns inferred chronology and relationships; plot is the selected event order through which the work presents that information. |
| Restricted narration | Broader narration | Restricted narration stays near one character's access to information; broader narration provides viewers with information from multiple characters or locations. |
| Flashback | Flashforward | A flashback presents an earlier event later in the telling; a flashforward presents a later event before returning to an earlier point. |

## Key Takeaway

Narrative organizes what happens and when viewers learn it. Trace events, knowledge, goals, obstacles, and ordering before making a qualified claim about how a sequence tells its story.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which question is most central to narrative analysis?**  
   A. Which camera brand recorded the image?  
   B. How are story information and events organized, revealed, or withheld for viewers?  
   C. Which costume requires the most fabric?  
   D. How many lights were used on set?  
   **Answer:** B. Narrative analysis focuses on how a work tells and distributes story information.

2. **A film opens with a firefighter receiving an apology, then later shows the earlier argument that prompted it. What is the strongest description?**  
   A. The later scene functions as a flashback that supplies earlier context  
   B. The sequence proves the firefighter is the antagonist  
   C. The apology is nondiegetic sound  
   D. The argument must be shown in the future  
   **Answer:** A. An earlier event presented after a later one is a flashback.

3. **Which distinction best separates plot from story?**  
   A. Plot concerns costumes, while story concerns camera lenses  
   B. Plot and story are always identical in a film  
   C. Plot is the presented order of selected events, while story includes the chronology and relations viewers infer  
   D. Story is only dialogue, while plot is only music  
   **Answer:** C. Plot is the presentation of events, while story includes a broader inferred chronology and relation.

4. **A film stays with one student and gives viewers no information that the student cannot see, hear, or discover. Which term is the most precise starting point?**  
   A. Parallel editing  
   B. A leitmotif  
   C. Broader narration  
   D. Restricted narration  
   **Answer:** D. Information is limited to one character's access.

5. **A character tries to return a borrowed laptop before a deadline, but a cancelled bus and missing charger delay the trip. What is the most careful narrative observation?**  
   A. The character has a goal, and the transit and missing charger function as obstacles  
   B. The cancelled bus proves the work is a comedy  
   C. Every deadline creates the same audience emotion  
   D. The sequence cannot contain cinematography or sound  
   **Answer:** A. The laptop return gives a goal while the transit and charger problems complicate progress.
