# Animation

## In 30 Seconds

Animation builds movement from a rapid succession of still images, each one constructed rather than recorded. Artists may draw frames by hand, photograph physical models frame by frame, or generate images with software. Because nothing on screen was captured as live action, every motion, cut, and sound cue is planned in advance. Animation is a medium, not a genre: it has carried comedy, drama, and serious historical subjects for over a century.

## Why This Matters

Understanding animation as a constructed medium changes how you watch it. Instead of asking what was recorded, you ask what was drawn, modeled, or rendered, and why. That question connects animation to the rest of film language: editing, sound, and cinematography all operate here, but they are planned before a single frame exists. The medium-versus-genre distinction matters for analysis as well. Calling animation a genre or a children's format misdescribes the wide range of work made with it, from short experiments to feature dramas. Recognizing the technique behind the image is the first step toward a precise claim about any animated work.

## Learning Objectives

- Define animation as a filmmaking medium that creates movement from a rapid succession of constructed still images.
- Distinguish drawn/cel animation, stop-motion, computer-generated animation, and rotoscoping by how each produces its frames.
- Explain how 2D and 3D approaches differ in how images are made, moved, and lit.
- Explain why animation is a medium rather than a genre, and why it is not a children's format by definition.
- Analyze how editing, sound, and cinematography operate in animated works built frame by frame.
- Describe key pre-cinema and early-cinema developments in animation history using verifiable dates and events.

## The College Version

### The animated image is constructed, not recorded

Animation is the art of making inanimate objects appear to move. Where live-action cinema records motion that already exists in front of a camera, animation builds motion from a rapid succession of still images, each of which is made by an artist, a model, or software. Encyclopaedia Britannica notes that the theory of the animated cartoon preceded cinema by about half a century: experimenters found that drawings of the stages of an action, shown in fast succession, are perceived as continuous movement. The Open Oregon textbook makes the same point through Eadweard Muybridge, whose sequence of photographs, shown in rapid succession, created the look of motion.

The consequence is total control. Because no frame records real continuous action, an animated character can float, stretch, or change shape in ways a live performer cannot, and the timing of every motion is decided in advance. This is what animation practitioners mean by the illusion of life: constructed images are arranged so carefully that they seem to breathe and move on their own. When a viewer asks how a scene was made, the first answer is always that each frame was built for the screen.

### Four families of technique

Drawn and cel animation creates each frame as a drawing. In classic studio practice, characters were drawn on transparent sheets and photographed over separately painted backgrounds, so artists redrew the moving character while reusing the background. Stop-motion photographs a physical object, moves it slightly, and photographs it again, frame by frame; the Library of Congress collection of early American animation (1900–1921) includes clay, puppet, and cut-out examples of this physical, frame-by-frame approach.

Computer-generated animation produces images with software. Two-dimensional computer animation draws on a flat picture surface, in effect a digital version of drawn animation. Three-dimensional animation models volumetric objects in a virtual space, adds lights and a virtual camera, and renders each frame; Encyclopaedia Britannica describes Toy Story (1995) as the first entirely computer-animated feature-length film. Rotoscoping is a hybrid: live-action footage is traced frame by frame so the drawing follows the recorded motion, a process the Fleischer brothers developed in the 1910s and used in their Out of the Inkwell series (1919–29). The 2D-versus-3D label describes how frames are made, not which approach is better or which audience a work suits.

### The same film language, planned in advance

Animation uses the full film language, but every choice is planned before the images exist. Editing still selects, orders, and times shots, yet there is no footage to reorder later: storyboards and timing sheets function as a form of pre-editing, deciding each cut and its duration before a frame is finished. Sound follows the same logic. Synchronized-sound cartoons such as Steamboat Willie (1928) showed that music and effects could be timed to individual drawings, and sound design remains a frame-accurate craft in which cues are locked to images that may not yet exist.

Cinematography also carries over. Framing, lighting, lens-like choices, and camera movement still shape the image; in drawn animation, the multiplane camera of the 1930s separated painted layers to create the illusion of depth, while in 3D computer animation the camera is virtual but composes, moves, and focuses like a real one. MIT's viewing guide treats image, editing, sound, and narrative as connected elements to notice, and that checklist works for animation with one adjustment: everything the viewer sees was built, so every element is analyzable as a deliberate choice.

### A medium, not a genre, and not a children's format by definition

Animation is not a genre in the way horror or the Western are genres. Genre labels describe kinds of story and convention; animation describes how images are made, and genres cut across it: animated comedy, animated drama, animated adventure. Because much animated content has been marketed to children, animation is often assumed to be a children's format, but the medium itself carries no such limit. Early American animation was tied to newspaper comic strips and reflected the social attitudes of its time, and Winsor McCay's 1918 re-creation of the sinking of the Lusitania treated a wartime disaster with seriousness, both within the first two decades of film animation. Whether a particular animated work suits a particular audience is a question about that work's content, not about the medium that made it.

For analysis, the practical rule is to describe the technique first: what was drawn, what was moved, what was rendered, and how the frames were timed. Then connect that description to the film language of editing, sound, and cinematography, and only then make a claim about meaning or effect. Genre classification, representation, and nonfiction questions belong to their own topics; here the question is how the medium builds its images.

## Key Vocabulary

- **animation:** A filmmaking medium that creates the appearance of movement from a rapid succession of still images, each constructed rather than recorded.
- **frame:** One still image in a moving-image sequence; in animation, the basic unit that an artist, model, or computer builds for the screen.
- **cel animation:** A drawn-animation practice in which characters are drawn on transparent sheets and photographed over separately painted backgrounds.
- **stop-motion:** An animation technique in which a physical object is photographed, repositioned slightly, and photographed again, one frame at a time.
- **computer-generated animation:** Animation produced with software, either by drawing on a flat digital surface (2D) or by modeling, lighting, and rendering a virtual scene (3D).
- **rotoscoping:** A technique that traces live-action footage frame by frame so that drawn animation follows recorded motion.
- **2D animation:** Animation made on a flat picture surface, such as drawings, in which movement is created within that plane.
- **3D animation:** Animation built from volumetric models in a virtual space, with virtual cameras and lights, rendered frame by frame.

## Eli-10

Animation makes things move on screen by showing many still pictures one after another, very quickly. Each picture is made on purpose: drawn, built, or created with a computer, not filmed as real action. When the pictures change a little each time, your eyes and brain put them together as movement. Some animation is made of drawings, some of small models moved and photographed step by step, and some entirely with software. Because every picture is planned, animators control exactly what moves, how fast, and what it looks like. Watch an animated scene and ask: what was drawn, what was moved, what was built, and what was planned before the first picture existed?

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a flipbook: a pad of paper with a slightly different drawing on each page. Flick the pages and the character seems to walk. The walking is not recorded anywhere; your eyes connect the drawings. Animation is a flipbook scaled up to a movie, with thousands of frames instead of a few dozen.

The analogy has limits. A flipbook shows one line of action on flat pages. An animated film combines thousands of frames with sound, editing, camera movement, and color, and it can be built from models or 3D software as well as drawings. Flicking pages is only the starting idea.

## Worked Example

Imagine a short stop-motion scene: a small ceramic fox sits on a model kitchen counter at dawn. The animator photographs the fox, moves its leg a fraction of a millimeter, and photographs again, repeating this dozens of times for every second of screen time. The lamp in the corner is a real tiny bulb, dimmed by hand between shots. When the frames play, the fox hops toward a cereal box, and the light seems to warm. The motion was never recorded; it was assembled from repositioned moments. A supportable claim is that the scene's warmth comes from constructed choices: the lamp's slow brightening and the hop's rhythm, each planned frame by frame.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling animation a genre, like horror or comedy.** Genre describes kinds of story and convention; animation describes how images are made, and it can carry any genre.
- **Assuming animation is for children by definition.** Much animation is aimed at young audiences, but the medium has carried adult-oriented comedy, drama, and serious historical subjects for over a century. Evaluate each work's content.
- **Treating animation as recorded performance and asking what the camera caught.** In animation nothing is captured as live action; every frame is constructed, so ask what was drawn, moved, or rendered, and how the frames are timed.
- **Confusing 2D and 3D with quality or audience.** 2D and 3D describe how frames are made: flat drawings versus modeled virtual space. Neither label tells you whether a work is good or who it is for.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2D animation | 3D animation | 2D animation builds movement on a flat picture surface, while 3D animation models volumetric objects in a virtual space with virtual cameras and lights. |
| Drawn/cel animation | Stop-motion | Drawn animation creates each frame as a new drawing, while stop-motion photographs a physical object, moves it, and photographs it again. |
| Animation | Live-action recording | Live-action captures real motion as it happens; animation constructs each frame, so motion is assembled rather than recorded. |

## Key Takeaway

Animation is a constructed medium: every frame is made, not recorded. Watch for the technique behind the image, and remember that the medium carries no single genre and no inherent age group.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best defines animation as a filmmaking medium?**  
   A. Recording real motion with a camera in continuous time  
   B. Creating the appearance of movement from a rapid succession of constructed still images  
   C. A genre defined by comedy and talking animals  
   D. A technique used only for children's films  
   **Answer:** B. Animation builds movement from successive constructed frames. It is a medium, not a genre, and it is not limited to comedy or to children's material.

2. **A crew photographs a clay figure, moves its arm a few millimeters, photographs again, and repeats this for every frame of a scene. Which technique are they using?**  
   A. Rotoscoping  
   B. 3D computer animation  
   C. Cel animation  
   D. Stop-motion  
   **Answer:** D. Photographing an object, repositioning it, and photographing it again frame by frame is the stop-motion process. The other techniques trace footage, render virtual models, or draw frames.

3. **Which item is a pre-cinema device that showed that rapid successive drawings are perceived as continuous movement?**  
   A. The zoetrope, a rotating drum lined with a band of pictures (1834)  
   B. The first synchronized-sound cartoon (1928)  
   C. The multiplane camera (1937)  
   D. The first entirely computer-animated feature film (1995)  
   **Answer:** A. The zoetrope is a pre-cinema device. The other options are later film-history events, not devices from before the cinema.

4. **A critic says: "This animated feature cannot be a serious film because it is animation." Which response best fits the evidence in this lesson?**  
   A. The critic is right, because animation cannot present drama  
   B. The critic is right, because every animated film is a comedy  
   C. The critic treats the medium as a genre or an age limit, but animation has carried serious adult subjects, such as McCay's 1918 re-creation of the sinking of the Lusitania  
   D. The critic is right, because animation never uses editing or sound  
   **Answer:** C. Animation is a medium built from constructed frames; serious adult subjects and the full film language appear in it, so judging a work by the medium alone misdescribes it.

5. **A studio needs a sequence in which a character moves while the painted background stays perfectly still across many frames. Which approach makes that reuse possible?**  
   A. Rotoscoping live-action footage of the character  
   B. Drawing the character on transparent layers and photographing them over the separately painted background  
   C. Photographing the background once and treating that single photo as the entire film  
   D. Rendering every frame without planning any frame in advance  
   **Answer:** B. Cel-style layering keeps the background fixed while the character layers change frame by frame. The other options trace footage, mistake one photo for a film, or deny frame-by-frame planning.
