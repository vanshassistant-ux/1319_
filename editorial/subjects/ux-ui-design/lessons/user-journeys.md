# User Journeys

## In 30 Seconds

A user journey is the step-by-step path a person takes to reach a goal with a product or service, often stretching across several channels and days. A journey map visualizes that path in stages, actions, thoughts, and feelings, and it marks the touchpoints where the person meets the brand. Because journeys reveal pain points no single screen shows, teams can improve the whole experience instead of one page.

## Why This Matters

People experience products as a sequence, not as one screen: they hear about a service, try it, use it, ask for help, and decide whether to return. A team that reviews only single screens misses the moments in between, where people get stuck, frustrated, or quietly give up. A journey map gathers the whole arc into one picture the entire team can read, turning vague complaints into specific fixable moments and showing what to improve first. It is a way of seeing the experience the way the person actually lives it.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a user journey using the working definition from Nielsen Norman Group.
- Name the parts of a journey map: actor, scenario, stages, actions, thoughts, feelings, and touchpoints.
- Explain why journey maps reveal pain points that a screen-by-screen review misses.
- Distinguish a user journey from a user flow.
- Explain why a journey map is a research-based model, not an exact record.

## The College Version

### The journey: a path to a goal

A user journey is the step-by-step path a person takes to reach a goal with a product or service. The working definition used in this lesson comes from Nielsen Norman Group: a scenario-based sequence of the steps a person takes to accomplish a high-level goal with a company or product, usually across channels and over time. Two details in that definition matter. First, a journey is a sequence: it has a beginning, a middle, and an end, and it unfolds over time, sometimes across days or weeks. Second, it usually crosses channels: the same person may read a website, use an app, and call support during one journey. A journey is not the same as a moment of use. Opening an app once is a moment; deciding to try a service, learning it, using it, hitting a problem, and choosing to stay or leave is the journey. Each person travels many journeys in a year, and each one is a candidate for study.

### The map and its parts

A journey map is the visualization of that path. Nielsen Norman Group's Journey Mapping 101 describes the elements most maps share: an actor, a scenario, journey phases, and the actions, mindsets, and emotions within each phase. The actor is the person whose journey the map describes, and each map keeps one point of view so the story stays clear. The scenario names the situation, such as a first-time renter searching for an apartment. Phases are the high-level stages that organize the map; for a shopping journey, the stages might be discover, try, buy, use, and seek support. Under each stage, the map records what the person does, what they are thinking, and how they feel. Touchpoints mark the moments when the person actually meets the company, such as opening the app, reading a confirmation email, or calling support. Near the bottom, teams usually note opportunities: the places where a change could improve the experience. The finished map is one story told from one person's point of view.

### Why the whole arc matters

A journey map earns its keep by showing what a screen-by-screen review cannot: the experience between and around the screens. Consider a grocery delivery service. Every screen works: the catalog is clear, checkout is fast, and the payment form never errors. Yet customers quietly stop ordering. A journey map that covers the stages from placing an order to receiving it reveals why: after checkout the app goes silent, and people wait at home unsure whether their order is coming. No single screen contains that problem; it lives in the gap between screens. Nielsen Norman Group notes that organizations often suffer from fragmented understanding because nobody pieces together the entire experience, and that friction often exists simply because no internal team owns that part of the journey. Making the missing piece visible is the first step toward fixing it.

### Big picture versus task path

A user journey is not the same as a user flow. Nielsen Norman Group describes the journey as the macro view: a holistic, high-level experience across channels and over time, such as a family planning and taking a week-long camping trip with a booking service. A user flow is the micro view: the specific set of interactions for one common task inside one product, such as reserving a single campsite on the service's website. User flows are covered in their own lesson in this course; the point here is the difference in scope. Journey maps also record emotions, what people feel at each stage. A patient booking a dentist appointment may feel relief when a free slot appears, frustration when the confirmation form rejects a birth date format, and satisfaction when the text message confirms the booking: three feelings inside one small journey. Finally, a map is a model, not the truth. It condenses and polishes many individual experiences into one representative narrative, built from research such as interviews and observations. One map shows one actor in one scenario, simplified on purpose so a team can read it, discuss it, and act on it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **user journey:** The step-by-step path a person takes to accomplish a goal with a product or service, usually across channels and over time.
- **journey map:** A diagram that lays out the stages, actions, thoughts, and feelings of a user journey, along with its touchpoints and opportunities.
- **stage:** One of the high-level phases into which a journey is divided, such as discover, try, buy, use, or seek support.
- **touchpoint:** A moment of contact between a person and a company, such as opening an app, reading an email, or calling support.
- **channel:** A method of communication or service delivery, such as a website, an app, or a physical store.
- **pain point:** A moment in an experience where a person is blocked, confused, or frustrated, often causing them to give up.
- **user flow:** The set of steps for completing one specific task inside a product, narrower than a whole journey.
- **actor:** The person whose journey a map describes, with one point of view kept per map.

## Eli-10

A user journey is the whole path someone walks to get something done: noticing a need, finding a product, using it, and getting help if it goes wrong. A journey map is a picture of that path. The picture is divided into stages, and under each stage the team writes what the person does, what they are thinking, and how they feel. The map also marks the touchpoints, the places where the person actually meets the product or brand. Teams use the picture to find the rough spots, like a step where people get confused or give up. It shows the whole walk, not just one doorway.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a journey map as a trail guide for a hiking route. It shows the trailheads, the steep sections, the viewpoints, and the spots where hikers usually turn back, so the trail crew knows where to add handrails or signs.

The analogy has limits: a trail guide describes a fixed route through real terrain, while a journey map is built from interviews and observations of people, and it changes as the product and its users change. The map is a model for discussion, not a surveyor's exact record.

## Worked Example

A city's transit app team wants to know why riders stop planning trips on the app. They choose one actor, a commuter named Rosa who rides the bus on weekdays, and one scenario: Rosa's first week with the app. The team maps five stages: hear about the app, download and set up, plan a trip, ride the bus, and check the return trip. Under each stage they record Rosa's actions, thoughts, and feelings. The map shows that setup feels easy and trip planning works, but riders grow anxious at the ride stage because the app never shows where the bus actually is. That is a pain point no single-screen review would catch, and the team now knows which feature to design and test next.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reviewing one screen at a time and calling it a journey.** The pain points often live between screens, so look at the whole path.
- **Drawing a map from the team's guesses and presenting it as user experience.** Build the map from interviews, observations, or other research.
- **Assuming every customer takes the same journey.** One map covers one actor and one scenario; build separate maps when people differ.
- **Trying to capture every possible path in a single map.** Maps simplify on purpose, and a map with every branch stops being readable.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
|---|---|---|
| User journey | User flow | A journey is the broad, high-level path across channels and time; a flow is the specific set of steps for one task in one product. |
| Touchpoint | Channel | A touchpoint is one moment of contact; a channel is the method of delivery, such as the website or the store. |
| Journey map | The journey itself | The journey is what people actually do; the map is a simplified visualization of it, built from research. |

## Key Takeaway

A user journey is the full path a person takes to reach a goal, and a journey map makes that path visible: stages, actions, thoughts, feelings, and touchpoints. The map is a research-based model that exposes pain points across the whole experience, so a team can improve the arc rather than one screen.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best matches the working definition of a user journey used in this lesson?**
   - A. The visual styling of a single screen, such as colors, spacing, and fonts
   - B. A ranked list of every feature a product offers
   - C. The step-by-step path a person takes to reach a goal with a product or service, usually across channels and over time
   - D. A summary of the audience a marketing campaign is trying to reach
   **Answer:** C. The step-by-step path a person takes to reach a goal with a product or service, usually across channels and over time

2. **Which set names the parts of a journey map that this lesson teaches?**
   - A. Stages, actions, thoughts, and feelings
   - B. Server logs, database tables, and error codes
   - C. Brand colors, logo sizes, and typefaces
   - D. Invoice numbers, warehouse counts, and shipping routes
   **Answer:** A. Stages, actions, thoughts, and feelings

3. **A grocery delivery service notices that many customers order successfully but stop using the service after their first delivery. Which approach is most likely to reveal why?**
   - A. Review the checkout screen's layout on a phone
   - B. Test only the password reset form
   - C. Audit the brand colors used in the app
   - D. Map the journey from placing the order through delivery, recording what customers do, think, and feel at each stage
   **Answer:** D. Map the journey from placing the order through delivery, recording what customers do, think, and feel at each stage

4. **A rider opens a ride-hailing app, sends a message to support about a missing driver, and later receives a reply by email. Which of these is best described as a touchpoint?**
   - A. The company's headquarters building
   - B. The moment the rider sends the support message from the app
   - C. The internal team that reviews support messages
   - D. The payment method stored on the rider's account
   **Answer:** B. The moment the rider sends the support message from the app

5. **A team builds a journey map of a new customer's first month with a budgeting app. Why is it accurate to call the map a model rather than the truth?**
   - A. Because the map was drawn from guesswork instead of research
   - B. Because the map records every person's exact experience in full detail
   - C. Because the map condenses many individual experiences into one representative path and simplifies on purpose
   - D. Because a model can never be based on data
   **Answer:** C. Because the map condenses many individual experiences into one representative path and simplifies on purpose
