# Surveys

## In 30 Seconds

A survey is a structured set of questions given to many people at once. It buys breadth: dozens or hundreds of answers, all to the same questions. Good survey questions are clear, neutral, and ask one idea at a time. Common types include open-ended questions, multiple choice, and rating scales. Watch for leading questions, double-barreled questions, response bias, and low response rates. Remember that surveys measure what people say about themselves, not what they actually do.

## Why This Matters

Surveys are one of the fastest ways to hear from many people, which is why teams reach for them early in user research. A well-run survey can reveal patterns across a whole audience: which features people actually use, how satisfied they are, what frustrates them. But the same convenience hides traps. A leading or double-barreled question produces confident-looking numbers that are simply wrong, and a low response rate means the people who answered may not represent the people you care about. Learning to write good questions and read results critically helps you build on solid feedback, and it makes you a sharper reader of every poll and feedback form you meet.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a survey as a structured set of questions given to many people and contrast it with interviews in terms of breadth versus depth.
- Describe the qualities of a good survey question: clear, neutral, and one idea at a time.
- Distinguish open-ended, multiple-choice, and rating-scale questions and give an example of each.
- Recognize leading questions, double-barreled questions, response bias, and low response rates in a survey.
- Explain why the people who receive a survey matter as much as the questions it asks.
- Interpret survey results as self-reported statements that should be combined with other research methods.

## The College Version

### A survey: many people, one set of questions

In user research, a survey is a structured set of questions given to many people. The Interaction Design Foundation describes UX surveys as tools that gather information about users' feelings, thoughts, and behaviors related to a product or service, typically through a set of questions. 'Structured' is the key word: every respondent sees the same questions, in the same order, with the same answer options. That sameness is what makes the answers comparable. Ask fifty people and you can count how many chose each option; ask five thousand and you can still count. This breadth is the reason surveys exist. The trade-off is depth. A survey answer is short and fixed, so a person's reasons, hesitations, and context stay hidden behind the option they picked. That missing depth is exactly what interviews are built to capture, which is why the two methods are siblings: surveys for breadth, interviews for depth.

### Writing questions people can answer honestly

The craft of a survey lives in its wording. Pew Research Center, which runs national surveys, treats question writing as the most important part of the survey process, because questions must accurately measure opinions, experiences, and behaviors. Three rules cover most of the craft. First, be clear and specific. Before: 'Do you find our app's settings menu layout arrangement user-friendly?' After: 'When you last changed a password in the app, how easy was it to find the Settings menu?' Second, be neutral. A neutral question does not imply a particular answer or give away what the team expects, as Nielsen Norman Group puts it. Before: 'How much did you enjoy our redesigned home screen?' After: 'How would you rate the redesigned home screen?' Third, ask one idea at a time. Before: 'How satisfied are you with the price and the delivery time of your order?' After: two separate questions, one about price and one about delivery time. Pew calls a question that asks for two evaluations at once a double-barreled question: respondents find it hard to answer, and the results are hard to interpret.

### Three question types to know

Most surveys mix three question types. An open-ended question lets respondents answer in their own words, for example 'What, if anything, stopped you from completing your purchase today?' These answers are rich but slow to analyze. A multiple-choice question offers a fixed set of options, for example 'How often do you use the search bar in the app?' with answers like 'Never,' 'A few times a month,' 'A few times a week,' and 'Daily.' A rating scale asks for a position on a numbered or labeled range, for example 'How likely are you to recommend this service to a friend?' on a zero-to-ten scale. Nielsen Norman Group notes that closed questions used in surveys usually take the form of multiple-choice or rating-scale items, and the Interaction Design Foundation recommends mixing types so that numbers and stories both come out of the same survey.

### Four traps that quietly poison results

Four failure modes deserve names. A leading question nudges respondents toward a particular answer; 'How much did you enjoy our award-winning checkout?' is really an opinion wearing a question mark. A double-barreled question packs two ideas into one, so a 'no' answer to 'Is the app reliable and fast?' is ambiguous, because nobody can tell which half failed. Response bias covers the ways answers drift from the truth: respondents who are tired of long surveys, respondents who do not understand the question, and respondents influenced by the order of the questions. Pew's example is striking: in a 2008 poll, when 'the economy' was offered as a listed answer option, 58 percent chose it, while only 35 percent of those answering in their own words volunteered it. Finally, low response rates: Nielsen Norman Group warns that every extra question reduces the response rate and makes all results suspect, so keep surveys short and test them with a few people from the target audience before sending them out.

### Who you send it to matters as much as the questions

A survey's results describe only the people who received it and answered it. If a team sends a checkout survey to people who finished buying, they hear from satisfied buyers and miss everyone who left. If they post the survey link on their own social feed, they reach existing fans rather than confused newcomers. Pew's point is the mirror image: even accurate random sampling is wasted if the questions themselves are biased. Choosing who receives a survey, the sample, is its own topic taught in data-science-and-ai-literacy; here the practical rule is simpler. Before reading any results, ask who was asked and whether that group matches the people the team needs to understand.

### Surveys measure what people say

Every survey answer is a statement about the person who gave it, not a recording of what they did. Nielsen Norman Group makes the point bluntly: quantitative surveys count how many people say they do this or that. Someone may report exercising daily, being very satisfied, or never using a feature, and all of it may be true, exaggerated, or simply what they wished were true. That is why survey results work best as one layer of evidence, combined with other methods: interviews add depth to the numbers, and usability testing shows what people actually do with a product. This honest framing is not a criticism of surveys; it is what makes them useful. Know what a survey can and cannot tell you, and you will know how much weight to give it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **survey** — A structured set of questions given to many people so their answers can be compared.
- **respondent** — A person who receives a survey and answers its questions.
- **open-ended question** — A question that lets the respondent answer freely in their own words.
- **closed question** — A question that restricts the respondent to a fixed set of answer options.
- **rating scale** — A closed question format that asks respondents to place their answer on a numbered or labeled range.
- **leading question** — A question worded so that it nudges respondents toward a particular answer.
- **double-barreled question** — A question that asks about two different ideas at once, making the answers hard to interpret.
- **response bias** — Any tendency in a survey that makes answers drift from the truth, such as fatigue or question order.
- **response rate** — The share of the people who received a survey who actually answered it.
- **self-report** — Information people provide about themselves, which may differ from what they actually do.

## Eli-10

**The simple version.** A survey is like handing the same question card to lots of people at once. Everyone gets the same card, so you can count answers and see patterns: eight out of ten people circled 'easy,' two circled 'hard.' The trick is the card itself. If the card says 'How much do you love our easy checkout?' people will mostly circle 'a lot,' because the question already told them the answer it wanted. If the card asks two questions at once, like 'Was checkout fast and clear?', you cannot tell whether a 'no' means slow or confusing. If only a few people hand the card back, the answers you have may not represent everyone else. And the answers are what people say about themselves: someone can circle 'very healthy' while skipping every workout.

**The analogy.** Think of a survey as a crowd vote at a town meeting. Everyone holds up a colored card for the same question, and you count the colors. It is fast, and it tells you what the crowd says. But the crowd is only the people who showed up, the question must be one you can answer with a single card, and a card raised in the air is a claim, not proof: someone can hold up the green 'I recycle every week' card and still toss cans in the trash.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** Real surveys are not votes: people answer privately, answers are often graded scales rather than colors, and the counting requires care. The analogy also hides that question wording, not just the crowd, shapes what people say; a slightly different card can produce a different count.

## Practice Question Bank

### Question 1

**In user research, what is the working definition of a survey?**

A. A structured set of questions given to many people.  *(correct)*
B. A guided conversation with one participant that follows their lead.
C. A recording of what users actually do while using a product.
D. A checklist an expert uses to score a design against best practices.

*A survey is a structured set of questions given to many people so the answers can be compared. A guided one-on-one conversation describes an interview, recording real behavior describes observation-based methods, and an expert checklist describes evaluation rather than a survey.*

### Question 2

**A team drafts the question 'How satisfied are you with the price and the delivery time of your order?' What is the main problem with this question?**

A. It is open-ended, so the answers will be hard to count.
B. It uses jargon that most respondents will not understand.
C. It asks about two different things at once, making it a double-barreled question.  *(correct)*
D. Its answer options are not balanced between positive and negative.

*The question packs two ideas, price and delivery time, into one sentence. Pew Research Center calls such questions double-barreled: respondents struggle to answer them and the results are hard to interpret. The fix is to split it into two separate questions.*

### Question 3

**A survey asks, 'How much did you enjoy our newly redesigned, award-winning home screen?' The most serious flaw in this question is that it:**

A. is closed-ended, so it cannot capture any detail.
B. is leading, because the wording signals the answer the team expects.  *(correct)*
C. is too long for most respondents to finish.
D. asks about feelings instead of about behavior.

*Words like 'enjoy' and 'award-winning' point respondents toward a positive answer, which is the definition of a leading question. Nielsen Norman Group advises writing neutral questions that do not imply particular answers or give away expectations.*

### Question 4

**Why does a low response rate make survey results unreliable?**

A. Because a low response rate guarantees that the questions were biased.
B. Because response rates matter only for surveys that use open-ended questions.
C. Because a high response rate proves that the sample is representative.
D. Because the people who answer may differ from the people who do not, so the results may not hold for the whole audience.  *(correct)*

*A low response rate means the answers come from whoever happened to respond, and that group may differ from everyone else. Nielsen Norman Group notes that every extra question reduces the response rate and makes results suspect, which is why short surveys perform better.*

### Question 5

**Which question type lets respondents answer in their own words?**

A. Open-ended question  *(correct)*
B. Multiple-choice question
C. Rating scale
D. Yes-or-no question

*An open-ended question allows a free-form answer in the respondent's own words, while multiple-choice questions, rating scales, and yes-or-no questions all restrict answers to a fixed set of options.*
