# Archaeological Evidence

## In 30 Seconds

Archaeological evidence is the material traces of past human activity — the objects people made, used, and left behind. It comes in five forms: artifacts, features, structures, ecofacts, and human remains. Preservation is selective: most material decays, so what survives is a partial sample. Archaeologists read these remains through context — where an object lay and what surrounded it — and date them with relative methods like stratigraphy and absolute methods like radiocarbon dating and dendrochronology. Every artifact is a witness with a story; the honest catch is that the record is fragmentary.

## Why This Matters

For most of human existence, people left no written records; the only evidence of how they lived is what they left behind. That makes archaeological evidence the backbone of our knowledge about the human past — and the skills used to read it transfer far beyond digs. Context teaches you to ask where a piece of information came from before trusting it; relative and absolute dating teach you to separate sequence from clock-time; the fragmentary-record reality check teaches you to say what you do not know. Anyone who evaluates claims about the past — a museum visitor, a journalist, a citizen — reads archaeological evidence more carefully with these tools. And the evidence itself keeps growing: every excavation and every new dating technique revises part of the story.

## The College Version

### What archaeological evidence is

Archaeological evidence is the material traces of past human activity — the objects people made, used, modified, and left behind. OpenStax's Introduction to Anthropology frames it as the means by which we begin to understand the thoughts and worldviews of earlier peoples: the human-made objects, buildings, and burial remains left by those who lived before. Written records cover only a thin, recent slice of the human story; for everything before writing — and for most people even after it — the material record is the evidence. Archaeology as a discipline (a sibling lesson of its own) is the field that recovers and reads this evidence; this lesson is about the evidence itself: its types, its survival, its dating, and its context.

### The types of evidence

Archaeological evidence is usually sorted into five categories. Consider each one as it might appear at a fictional riverside settlement called Tarmo. **Artifacts** are portable objects made or modified by people — at Tarmo, a hand-sized clay bowl with a fingertip-pressed rim, found in a collapsed floor. **Features** are non-portable traces of past activity that stay in the ground, such as hearths, storage pits, postholes, or road cuts — at Tarmo, a ring of fire-cracked stones with ash inside. **Structures** are built constructions: houses, walls, granaries — at Tarmo, the post-and-beam frame of a raised granary. **Ecofacts** are natural objects that people used or that entered the site through human activity, such as seeds, shells, or animal bones — at Tarmo, charred wheat grains and fish bones in the hearth ash. **Human remains** are burials and skeletons, which carry both biological and cultural information — at Tarmo, a flexed burial wearing a shell necklace. The categories shade into one another, but this map is the standard one.

### Preservation: what survives and why

The honest baseline: most material decays. Wood rots, cloth unravels, food disappears — unless conditions slow the decay. Survival favors the durable — stone, fired clay, bone — and the lucky: waterlogged bogs, dry caves, or rapid burial by ash and flood silt can preserve even organic material. Original example: at Tarmo, a wooden spoon rotted away entirely in the damp loam, while a linen sack in the same midden vanished and the five clay beads it held fell exactly where they had stood. The Smithsonian's Human Origins Program states the point plainly: preservation can occur only under very specific conditions, and the record is incomplete. So absence is not evidence of absence — a site that yields no wooden tools is not a site without woodworkers; it is a site where wood did not survive.

### Dating: relative and absolute

Two families of dating answer different questions. **Relative dating** orders things — older than, younger than — without assigning calendar years. Its anchor is stratigraphy, built on the law of superposition, proposed by Nicolaus Steno in 1669: in undisturbed layers, lower strata were deposited earlier, so what lies beneath is older. Original example: at Tarmo, an ash layer holding a broken bowl sits directly beneath a flood-deposit layer, so the bowl is older than the flood. Typological sequences and seriation order objects by style change over time, a relative approach Flinders Petrie applied to Egyptian pottery in the nineteenth century. **Absolute dating** — also called chronometric dating — uses physical and chemical analysis to estimate a calendar-age range. Radiocarbon dating tracks the decay of carbon-14 in organic material, usable on samples roughly 300 to 50,000 years old — a charred grain, a bone, a scrap of cloth. Dendrochronology counts and matches tree rings, one per year, to date wooden timbers precisely. Original example: a Tarmo granary beam with 212 rings was matched against a regional tree-ring sequence, yielding a felling year. The strongest practice is triangulation: applying several methods to the same site and letting them agree.

### Context: where an object lies matters

An object's meaning lives in its surroundings: its position in the ground, the layer it came from, and what it lay beside. OpenStax calls context the key foundation of archaeological principles and practice. An object found and documented in place is in **primary context**; one that has been moved — by a flood, a plow, or a looter — is in **secondary context**, and its original associations are lost. Original example: a flint arrowhead picked off the surface of a plowed field is a handsome object, but little more. The same arrowhead sealed beneath a collapsed wall, with hearth ash and charred seeds beside it, is evidence: it says when people were there, what they ate, and how the house came down. The object is identical; the information is not. Recording position is therefore not paperwork — it is the difference between a curiosity and a fact.

### The honest notes

Two reality checks close the lesson. First, the record is **fragmentary**. The remains that survive are a small, biased sample of everything past people made, and what decays is not random — it skews toward the durable and the lucky. Archaeological knowledge is built from partial evidence and is revised as finds and techniques improve; certainty is earned slowly, one documented layer at a time. Second, the honest framing: **every artifact is a witness with a story**. Objects survive because people made them, used them, treasured them, or threw them away — and the archaeologist's job is to let that story be heard carefully, without inventing what the evidence cannot support. The witness speaks; the archaeologist listens, records, and interprets — and stays honest about what the witness cannot tell us.

## Key Vocabulary

- **artifact** — A portable object made or modified by people, such as a clay bowl or a stone scraper, found at an archaeological site.
- **feature** — A non-portable trace of past activity, such as a hearth, a storage pit, or a posthole, that stays in the ground as part of the site.
- **ecofact** — A natural object that people used or that entered a site through human activity, such as charred seeds or animal bones.
- **context** — The position of an object in the ground and its associations with the layers and objects around it; where an object lies and what surrounds it.
- **stratigraphy** — The study of layered deposits, called strata, based on the principle that in undisturbed sequences lower layers were deposited before higher ones.
- **law of superposition** — The principle that in undisturbed layered deposits, a lower stratum and the objects within it are older than the strata above them.
- **relative dating** — Ordering objects or layers as older or younger than one another without assigning calendar years.
- **absolute dating** — Dating methods, also called chronometric dating, that use physical or chemical analysis to estimate a calendar-age range for an object.
- **radiocarbon dating** — An absolute dating method that measures the decay of carbon-14 in organic material, usable on samples roughly 300 to 50,000 years old.
- **dendrochronology** — Tree-ring dating, in which the annual rings of wood are counted and matched against regional sequences to date timber.

## Eli-10

**Simplified explanation.** The past left us a partly burned book. Nearly everything people once made has decayed: wooden bowls, cloth, food, leather — gone. What remains are the tough pages — stone, fired clay, bone — and the lucky ones that landed where decay slowed down, like a bog or a dry cave. That is why archaeologists say the record is fragmentary: it is a sample of the past, not the past itself. The reading methods come in two kinds. Relative dating puts pages in order — the page under another page came first — using the layers of earth, which is stratigraphy. Absolute dating measures the page itself, like counting the radioactive decay in a bone (radiocarbon) or the rings in a wooden beam (dendrochronology), to get real years. And where a page sat in the book matters: an object torn out and dropped on a modern field has lost its place in the story. Context is the bookmark; without it, the witness goes mute.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a library after a fire. Most books burned. The survivors are the thickest ones with the toughest bindings, plus whatever happened to be stored in the fireproof basement. You cannot reconstruct the library — but you can read every surviving page, and you can tell which shelf a charred page came from by the neighbors that remain on that shelf. The shelf position is context; the age of the paper is dating; the burned library is the archaeological record.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** Books have authors, titles, and dates printed on them; artifacts announce none of these, so their meaning and age must be inferred and argued over. A fire destroys fairly, but decay is choosy — it favors some materials and places over others, quietly biasing the record. And unlike a page, an object can be carried away after it is dropped, losing its shelf — which is why context must be documented, never assumed.

## Worked Example

A field team excavates a riverside settlement called Tarmo — a fictional site built like a real one. In a test pit they find, from top to bottom: modern topsoil; a flood deposit of fine gray silt; a thick layer of ash and dark soil; and undisturbed clay. In the ash layer they uncover a ring of fire-cracked stones (a hearth feature), a broken clay bowl with a fingertip-pressed rim (an artifact), charred wheat grains and fish bones (ecofacts), and, sealed beneath a collapsed mud wall, a human burial wearing a shell necklace (human remains). Dating runs on two tracks. Relative: the bowl lies in the ash layer beneath the flood silt, so it is older than the flood and younger than the clay below — order, not years. Absolute: a charred wheat grain is radiocarbon dated to roughly 1,200 years ago, and a wooden roof beam from the collapsed wall is matched against a regional tree-ring sequence, giving a felling year that agrees. Context does the rest: because the burial was sealed by the wall collapse, the team knows the house fell after the burial was made. Four types of evidence, two dating families, one documented context — and a story about a household that stored grain, cooked over a hearth, and buried its dead at home.

## Common Mistakes

- **"Archaeologists mainly dig up dinosaur bones."** Dinosaur bones belong to paleontology. Archaeology studies the material traces of past human activity — what people made, used, and left behind — so an archaeologist's evidence is human evidence, not prehistoric reptile remains.
- **"Finding an old object is what matters; its exact position is a detail."** Position is everything. An object torn from its context — moved by a plow, a flood, or a looter — loses the associations that let archaeologists date and interpret it. Context, not age alone, turns a curiosity into evidence.
- **"Radiocarbon dating works on anything old."** Radiocarbon dating works only on organic material — things that were once alive, like wood, bone, seeds, or cloth — and only within a range of roughly 300 to 50,000 years. Stone tools and fired clay are dated by other means or by association with datable material.
- **"An artifact's meaning is built into the object itself."** Meaning comes from context — where the object lay, what surrounded it, what it was associated with. The same arrowhead on a plowed surface and sealed under a collapsed wall carries entirely different information.
- **"The archaeological record gives us the full story of a past society."** The record is fragmentary. Most material has decayed, and what survives is a biased sample of the durable and the lucky. Archaeologists build knowledge from partial evidence and revise it as finds and techniques improve.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Relative dating vs absolute dating** — Relative dating orders things as older or younger than one another (for example, by stratigraphy or style sequences), while absolute dating uses physical and chemical analysis to estimate a calendar-age range, as radiocarbon and dendrochronology do.
- **Artifact vs ecofact** — An artifact is a portable object made or modified by people; an ecofact is a natural object, such as a seed or an animal bone, that people used or that entered the site through human activity.
- **Primary context vs secondary context** — An object in primary context is found and documented in place, keeping its associations; an object in secondary context has been moved — by nature or people — so its original position and associations are lost.

## Key Takeaway

Archaeological evidence is the material trace of past lives: fragmentary, context-bound, and readable through careful dating. Every surviving object is a witness with a story — a story told by the ground it lay in.

## Practice Question Bank

**Q1. Which item found at a dig is an artifact?**
- A. A ring of fire-cracked stones surrounding a bed of ash
- B. A hand-sized clay bowl shaped and fired by people, found beside a collapsed wall
- C. Charred wheat grains scattered in a storage pit
- D. The skeleton of an adult buried with a shell necklace

**Answer: B.** An artifact is a portable object made or modified by people — the clay bowl fits. The hearth ring is a feature, the grains are ecofacts, and the skeleton is human remains. *(recall)*

**Q2. Archaeologists match the ring pattern of a wooden beam from a collapsed granary against a regional tree-ring sequence. Which dating method are they using?**
- A. Stratigraphy
- B. Radiocarbon dating
- C. Dendrochronology
- D. Seriation

**Answer: C.** Tree rings form one per year, and matching ring-width patterns across timbers is dendrochronology. Stratigraphy and seriation are relative methods; radiocarbon measures carbon-14 decay in organic material. *(recall)*

**Q3. At a riverside dig, an ash layer holding a broken clay bowl sits directly beneath a thick flood-deposit layer. Applying the law of superposition, what can the archaeologists conclude?**
- A. The bowl was dropped after the flood deposited its silt
- B. The bowl was in use before the flood buried the ash layer
- C. The bowl and the flood happened in the same year
- D. The bowl is younger than the flood deposit

**Answer: B.** In undisturbed strata, lower layers were deposited first, so the ash layer — and the bowl within it — predates the flood silt above it. Deeper is earlier, not the reverse. *(application)*

**Q4. A linen sack in an ancient midden rots away completely, while the five clay beads it held stay exactly where they fell. What does this show about preservation?**
- A. Preservation is selective: durable materials often outlast organic ones, and most material decays
- B. Organic material usually survives better than fired clay
- C. Clay beads decay faster than linen
- D. Middens preserve everything equally well

**Answer: A.** Fired clay outlasted linen in the same deposit, showing that survival depends on material and conditions; most organic material decays, which is why the record is fragmentary. *(understanding)*

**Q5. Two flint arrowheads of identical shape are found in the same region: one picked up loose from plowed topsoil, one sealed beneath a collapsed wall 1.5 meters down with hearth ash and charred seeds around it. Why do archaeologists treat the buried find as far more informative?**
- A. Because depth alone proves the buried arrowhead is older
- B. Because the surface arrowhead was made by different people
- C. Because buried stone is better preserved than surface stone
- D. Because the buried arrowhead keeps its context — documented position and associations — which the surface find has lost

**Answer: D.** The buried arrowhead retains primary context: its layer, position, and associations are documented, enabling dating and interpretation. The surface find is in secondary context — moved and disconnected — so depth, style, and stone quality cannot rescue the information it lost. *(analysis)*
