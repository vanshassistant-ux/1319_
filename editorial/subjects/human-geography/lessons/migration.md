# Migration

## In 30 Seconds

Migration is the permanent or semi-permanent move from one home to another. Geographers sort it by direction — emigration (leaving) versus immigration (arriving) — and by scale: internal (within a country) versus international (across borders). Net migration is arrivals minus departures. Moves range from voluntary to forced, and often unfold in steps or along family chains. E.G. Ravenstein's 1880s observations still frame how we describe these flows.

## Why This Matters

Migration reshapes both the places people leave and the places they join, driving urban growth, labor markets, cultural change, and public policy. Learning its vocabulary lets you read demographic data without confusing a departure with an arrival, or an internal move with an international one. It is the foundation for later work on refugees and displacement, push-and-pull factors, urbanization, and economic development. In a century when the UN counts hundreds of millions of international migrants, describing movement precisely — and neutrally — is a basic analytic skill across geography, sociology, economics, and planning.

## Learning Objectives

- Define migration and distinguish it from short-term mobility or circulation.
- Distinguish emigration from immigration and internal from international migration.
- Calculate and interpret net migration for an area.
- Distinguish voluntary from forced migration and step from chain migration.
- Explain Ravenstein's laws of migration as an early empirical framework rather than deterministic laws.

## The College Version

### What migration is — and what it is not

Migration is the permanent or semi-permanent relocation of a person from one place of usual residence to another. The key qualifier is a lasting change of residence: a two-week vacation, a daily commute, or a seasonal work trip is mobility or circulation, not migration, because the person keeps a home base to return to. Geographers first describe a move by its direction relative to a place. A person who leaves an origin is an emigrant (emigration is the departure); the same person arriving at a destination is an immigrant (immigration is the arrival). One move therefore has two names at once — a family leaving Portugal for Canada emigrates from Portugal and immigrates to Canada. Keeping direction straight matters because national statistics are kept separately for arrivals and departures, and confusing the two reverses the meaning of the data.

### Scale: internal vs international, and net migration

The second basic distinction is scale, meaning whether a move crosses an international border. Internal migration stays within a single country — moving from a rural village to a large city, or from one region to another. It can be interregional (between regions) or intraregional (within one region). International migration crosses national borders, so the mover is an emigrant to the country of origin and an immigrant to the country of arrival. The two scales are governed by very different rules: internal migration usually requires no legal permission, while international migration is shaped by passports, visas, and immigration law. To summarize the balance of movement for a place, geographers use net migration — the number of immigrants minus the number of emigrants over a period. A positive value means more people arrived than left (net in-migration); a negative value means more left than arrived (net out-migration). Expressed per 1,000 residents, this becomes the net migration rate, one of the components — alongside births and deaths — that changes a population's size.

### Voluntary and forced migration

Migrations also differ in how much choice the mover had. Voluntary migration reflects the mover's own decision, typically the pursuit of work, study, family reunion, or a better environment. Forced migration involves an element of coercion: people flee conflict, persecution, or disaster and cannot safely stay. In practice these are ends of a spectrum, not a clean binary — economic desperation, environmental stress, and violence often overlap, and the same move can carry elements of both. The scale of the forced end is large: UNHCR reported roughly 117.3 million people forcibly displaced worldwide at the end of 2023, including about 43.4 million refugees and 68.3 million people displaced inside their own countries. This lesson gives the overview and typology; the precise legal definitions of refugee, asylum-seeker, and internally displaced person — and the 1951 Refugee Convention that governs them — are treated in the refugees-and-displacement topic. What matters here is that "forced" and "voluntary" describe the degree of coercion behind a move, not its scale or direction.

### How migrations unfold: step and chain migration

Beyond why people move, geographers describe how a stream of migration actually develops over space and time. Step migration is movement that happens in stages rather than one long jump: a person moves from a village to a nearby town, then to a small city, then to a major metropolitan center, climbing the settlement hierarchy in increments. Chain migration is movement built on social networks: once a few pioneers settle at a destination, relatives and neighbors follow because those pioneers can offer housing, job leads, and familiar community. Chain migration tends to cluster people from the same origin in the same neighborhoods, which is one way ethnic enclaves — a "Little Italy" or a "Chinatown" — form and persist. Step and chain migration are not opposites; a single stream can be both, unfolding stepwise across space while pulling later movers along established family chains.

### Ravenstein's laws of migration

The earliest systematic model of these patterns came from E.G. Ravenstein, a German-English geographer who published his "laws of migration" in the 1880s using British census data. Presented today as an early empirical framework rather than literal laws, his observations included that most migrants move only a short distance; that migration proceeds in steps; that long-distance migrants are drawn disproportionately to major centers of economic activity; that each migration current produces a counter-current, or return flow; that rural residents are more migratory than town dwellers; and that economic motives are the leading reason people move. Much of this still holds — short-distance and stepwise moves toward economic centers, with counterflows, remain common. But treating these as fixed "laws" overstates them: they are generalizations from one 19th-century national dataset, and modern migration includes long-distance labor and refugee movements that Ravenstein's frame captures only loosely. The right posture is to use his framework as a set of tendencies to test against data, not as rules that any given flow must obey.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Migration** — A permanent or semi-permanent change of a person's usual place of residence, as opposed to short-term travel or commuting.
- **Emigration** — The act of leaving one's country or region of origin to settle elsewhere; the person is an emigrant to the place left behind.
- **Immigration** — The act of arriving in a new country or region to settle; the same person is an immigrant to the place of arrival.
- **Internal migration** — A move that stays within the borders of a single country, such as rural-to-urban migration.
- **International migration** — A move that crosses a national border, making the mover an emigrant from one country and an immigrant to another.
- **Net migration** — The number of immigrants minus the number of emigrants for an area over a period; positive means net in-migration, negative means net out-migration.
- **Voluntary migration** — A move made largely by the mover's own choice, such as relocating for work, study, or family.
- **Forced migration** — A move driven by coercion — conflict, persecution, or disaster — in which staying is not a safe option.
- **Step migration** — Migration that occurs in stages, with movers relocating through progressively larger settlements toward major centers.
- **Chain migration** — Migration in which later movers follow relatives or community members already settled at a destination, often forming ethnic enclaves.

## Eli-10

Migration is when someone moves their home for good, not just for a trip. If you leave a place, you are an emigrant; when you show up in the new place, you are an immigrant — same move, two labels. Moving to a new town in the same country is internal; moving to a new country is international. If you count everyone who arrived and subtract everyone who left, you get net migration: a plus means the place grew from moving, a minus means it shrank. Some people choose to move; others are forced out by danger. And people often move in steps, a bit farther each time, or follow family who already went ahead.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a town as a bucket with water flowing in from one hose and out through a drain. The hose is immigration, the drain is emigration, and the change in the water level is net migration — you care about the difference, not just how fast either one runs.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The bucket hides what water cannot show: people move for reasons — a job, a war, a relative already settled — and they can flow back the way they came. Ravenstein's point about counterflows is exactly the part a one-way hose-and-drain picture leaves out.

## Worked Example

Riverton records 12,000 people arriving to live there during one year and 8,000 residents leaving to live elsewhere. Net migration is immigrants minus emigrants: 12,000 − 8,000 = +4,000. The positive sign means net in-migration — the town gained 4,000 people through movement alone, before counting any births or deaths. If Riverton's population is 200,000, its net migration rate is 4,000 ÷ 200,000 × 1,000 = +20 per 1,000 residents. Note what the single number hides: the 8,000 who left are a real counterflow, the kind Ravenstein observed accompanying every migration stream, and many of the 12,000 arrivals may have come in steps or by following relatives already in Riverton.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using "emigrant" and "immigrant" as if they were different people.** They are the same person seen from two sides of one move: an emigrant leaving the origin is the immigrant arriving at the destination.
- **Assuming any move counts as migration.** Migration requires a lasting change of residence; commuting, vacations, and seasonal trips are mobility or circulation, not migration.
- **Reading a positive net migration as the total number of migrants.** Net migration is arrivals minus departures. A net of +4,000 can hide tens of thousands of moves in both directions.
- **Treating Ravenstein's "laws" as strict rules that every migration must follow.** They are early generalizations from one 19th-century dataset — useful tendencies to test against evidence, not deterministic laws.
- **Confusing forced and voluntary with international and internal.** Coercion (forced vs voluntary) and scale (internal vs international) are independent: a forced move can stay inside a country, and a voluntary move can cross a border.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Emigration vs Immigration** — Both name the same move from opposite ends: emigration is the departure from the origin, immigration is the arrival at the destination.
- **Internal migration vs International migration** — Internal migration stays within one country's borders; international migration crosses a national border and is shaped by passports, visas, and immigration law.
- **Step migration vs Chain migration** — Step migration describes moving in stages up the settlement hierarchy; chain migration describes later movers following relatives or neighbors already settled at a destination.
- **Voluntary migration vs Forced migration** — Voluntary migration reflects the mover's own choice; forced migration involves coercion such as conflict or disaster. They mark ends of a spectrum, not a clean binary.

## Key Takeaway

Describe any move along two axes — direction (emigration vs immigration) and scale (internal vs international) — then note how much choice was involved (voluntary vs forced) and how the stream unfolds (step, chain). Net migration summarizes the balance, and Ravenstein's 1880s framework offers tendencies to test, not laws to obey.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In one year a country records 500,000 immigrants and 200,000 emigrants. What is its net migration for that year, and what does the sign mean?**
   - A. +300,000; more people entered than left, so the country had net in-migration.
   - B. −300,000; more people left than entered, so the country had net out-migration.
   - C. 700,000; the combined total volume of movement in both directions.
   - D. +300,000, but net migration cannot be positive because emigration always exceeds immigration.

   **Answer: A.** Net migration is immigrants minus emigrants: 500,000 − 200,000 = +300,000, and the positive sign means net in-migration. B has the sign backwards; C adds the flows instead of subtracting; D states a false rule.

2. **A family leaves Portugal to settle permanently in Canada. From Portugal's perspective and then from Canada's perspective, the family are:**
   - A. immigrants to Portugal; emigrants from Canada.
   - B. internal migrants in Portugal; international migrants in Canada.
   - C. refugees leaving Portugal; asylum-seekers in Canada.
   - D. emigrants from Portugal; immigrants to Canada.

   **Answer: D.** Leaving the origin is emigration and arriving at the destination is immigration, so they are emigrants from Portugal and immigrants to Canada. A reverses the terms; B misuses the internal/international distinction; C assumes a forced move and legal status the scenario does not support.

3. **Which of the following is an example of INTERNAL migration?**
   - A. A software engineer relocating permanently from India to Germany for a job.
   - B. A worker moving from a rural village to a large city within the same country.
   - C. A person crossing an international border to escape armed conflict.
   - D. A student emigrating abroad to attend a foreign university.

   **Answer: B.** Internal migration stays within one country's borders, which the rural-to-urban move does. A and D cross national borders; C also crosses a border and is forced migration.

4. **How is E.G. Ravenstein's 19th-century work on migration best characterized?**
   - A. A set of strict natural laws that predict exactly where any individual will move.
   - B. A binding legal framework that defines who qualifies as a refugee.
   - C. An early empirical framework noting that most migration is short-distance and stepwise, that long-distance movers head to economic centers, and that flows generate counterflows.
   - D. A theory holding that migration is mostly long-distance and essentially random.

   **Answer: C.** Ravenstein's 1880s "laws" are early empirical generalizations: short-distance and stepwise movement, long-distance flows toward economic centers, and counterflows. A overstates them as deterministic; B confuses them with refugee law; D contradicts his central finding.

5. **Over several years, migrants from one town increasingly settle in the same neighborhood of a distant city because relatives already living there provide housing and job leads. This pattern is best described as:**
   - A. step migration, because the movers climb the settlement hierarchy in stages.
   - B. forced migration, because the movers had no choice about leaving.
   - C. counter-migration, because the flow reverses an earlier stream.
   - D. chain migration, because later movers follow social networks of family and community already established at the destination.

   **Answer: D.** Following relatives who supply housing and job leads is the defining mechanism of chain migration, which clusters origin communities into enclaves. A describes movement in stages; B assumes coercion the scenario does not show; C describes a return flow.
