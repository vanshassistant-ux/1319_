# Refugees and Displacement

## In 30 Seconds

Forced displacement means people compelled to flee their homes by persecution, conflict, violence, or disaster. International law draws sharp lines. A refugee has crossed a border with a well-founded fear of persecution and qualifies for protection under the 1951 Refugee Convention. An asylum-seeker is awaiting a decision on that claim. An internally displaced person has fled but stayed inside their own country. Each differs from a migrant, who moves, often by choice, for reasons like work or study.

## Why This Matters

These categories decide who receives protection. A recognized refugee cannot lawfully be sent back to danger, the principle of non-refoulement, while the label migrant carries no such guarantee, so the words governments use have real consequences for real people. Displacement is also one of the defining human-geography patterns of our time: UNHCR counted about 123 million forcibly displaced people at the end of 2024, most of them displaced inside their own countries. Understanding the legal definitions, the causes, and the scale lets you read the news accurately and reason about migration policy, humanitarian response, and the growing role of disaster- and climate-driven displacement.

## Learning Objectives

- Define refugee, asylum-seeker, internally displaced person, and migrant, and distinguish them precisely.
- Explain the 1951 Refugee Convention and the principle of non-refoulement.
- Identify the major causes of forced displacement, including conflict, persecution, disaster, and climate change.
- Interpret UNHCR displacement figures with attention to their date and attribution.
- Apply the legal definitions to classify realistic scenarios of people on the move.

## The College Version

### Four categories, four legal realities

Forced displacement describes people who did not choose to leave but were compelled to flee. International law and humanitarian practice sort them into categories that carry very different rights, and precision here matters because the label attached to a person shapes what protection they can claim.

A **refugee**, under the 1951 Refugee Convention, is someone who is outside their country of nationality owing to a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and is unable or unwilling to return. Two features are essential: the person has crossed an international border, and the reason is persecution on one of those five grounds.

An **asylum-seeker** is a person who has applied for refugee status and is waiting for a decision. UNHCR stresses that refugee status is *declaratory*: a person does not become a refugee because a government recognizes them; they are a refugee the moment they meet the definition, and recognition simply confirms it. For that reason, asylum-seekers should be treated as refugees until it is determined that they are not.

An **internally displaced person (IDP)** has been forced to flee, often for the very same reasons as a refugee, but has not crossed an international border. IDPs remain inside their own country and, in law, under their own government's jurisdiction. They are the largest displaced group worldwide.

A **migrant**, by contrast, is an umbrella term that the International Organization for Migration notes has no agreed definition in international law. It covers people who move, within a country or across a border, for many reasons, frequently including work, study, or family. The key distinction is that being a migrant, on its own, does not entitle a person to international protection, whereas refugee status does. Using *migrant* loosely to describe people fleeing persecution can obscure that they are legally entitled to protection.

### The 1951 Refugee Convention and non-refoulement

The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees was adopted in 1951, in the aftermath of the Second World War, and originally applied mainly to people displaced by events in Europe before that date. The 1967 Protocol removed those temporal and geographic limits, turning the definition into a universal one that applies regardless of where or when displacement occurs. Together the 1951 Convention and its 1967 Protocol form the core of international refugee law.

The cornerstone of the Convention is the principle of **non-refoulement**, set out in Article 33. It provides that no state shall expel or return (*refouler* in French) a refugee in any manner whatsoever to the frontiers of territories where their life or freedom would be threatened on account of the same five grounds in the refugee definition. In practice this means a person with a valid claim cannot simply be pushed back into danger while, or before, that claim is assessed. The Convention allows narrow exceptions, for example where a refugee is a serious danger to the security of the host country, but the protection is broad and widely regarded as binding even on states that have not signed the Convention.

Some regions extend the definition. The 1969 OAU Convention in Africa and the 1984 Cartagena Declaration in Latin America recognize people fleeing generalized violence, external aggression, or events seriously disturbing public order, not only individualized persecution. These broader instruments coexist with the 1951 framework rather than replacing it.

### Why people are forced to flee

The drivers of forced displacement cluster into a few recurring causes. Armed conflict and war are the largest: at the end of 2024 the biggest displacement situations were Sudan, Syria, Afghanistan, and Ukraine, according to UNHCR. Persecution and serious human rights violations, targeting people for their identity, beliefs, or political activity, are the second, and they sit at the heart of the legal refugee definition. Generalized violence and the collapse of public order push others across borders even when no single group is singled out.

Disasters and, increasingly, the effects of climate change are a growing driver. Floods, droughts, and storms displace large numbers of people every year, and slow-onset changes can make places gradually unlivable. An important legal subtlety follows: people who flee a disaster or a changing climate usually do not fit the 1951 Convention definition, because they are not being persecuted on one of the five grounds. Many become IDPs, and those who cross a border are often classified as migrants rather than refugees. This gap between the causes of modern displacement and the categories of mid-twentieth-century law is an active area of policy debate, and this lesson describes it rather than resolving it.

### The scale, and how to read the numbers

Displacement figures move constantly, so they should always be quoted with a date and an attribution. UNHCR, the UN Refugee Agency, is the standard source. In its Global Trends report covering 2024, released in June 2025, UNHCR estimated that about 123.2 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide at the end of 2024, roughly one in every 67 people on the planet.

The composition of that total corrects a common misconception. The largest group is not refugees who have reached wealthy countries but internally displaced people, about 73.5 million, close to 60 percent of the total, who remain inside their own borders. Refugees and others in need of international protection numbered around 42.7 million, and several million more were asylum-seekers awaiting decisions. UNHCR also reports that most refugees are hosted by low- and middle-income countries near the crises they flee, not by the wealthiest nations. When you encounter a displacement statistic, check three things: the year it refers to, who is included in it, and who published it. A precise number without a date is a warning sign.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Forced displacement** — The situation of people compelled to leave their homes by persecution, conflict, violence, human rights violations, or disaster, as opposed to moving by choice.
- **Refugee** — A person outside their country of nationality who has a well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group, or political opinion, and cannot safely return; defined by the 1951 Refugee Convention.
- **Asylum-seeker** — A person who has applied for refugee status and is awaiting a decision on that claim; not yet formally recognized, but entitled to have the claim assessed.
- **Internally displaced person (IDP)** — Someone forced to flee their home but who has not crossed an international border and remains within their own country.
- **Migrant** — An umbrella term, not defined in international law, for a person who moves from their usual place of residence, within a country or across a border, for a wide range of reasons; carries no automatic entitlement to international protection.
- **1951 Refugee Convention** — The Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees, adopted in 1951 and extended worldwide by its 1967 Protocol, the central treaty defining who is a refugee and the protections they are owed.
- **Non-refoulement** — The core rule, in Article 33 of the 1951 Convention, that a refugee must not be expelled or returned to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened on the protected grounds.
- **Well-founded fear of persecution** — The legal test at the heart of the refugee definition: a fear that is both genuinely held and objectively reasonable, tied to one of the five Convention grounds.
- **UNHCR** — The Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees, the UN agency responsible for protecting refugees and the standard authority for global displacement statistics.

## Eli-10

When people are forced to leave home because staying is too dangerous, the world sorts them into groups, and the group name decides what help they can get. A refugee has crossed into another country because someone would harm them for who they are or what they believe; the law says that country generally cannot send them back into danger. An asylum-seeker is a person still waiting to hear whether they count as a refugee. An internally displaced person ran from the same dangers but stayed inside their own country. A migrant is anyone who moves, and often it is a choice, like moving for a job. Most displaced people in the world are actually the ones who never left their own country.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a line painted across the ground marking a border. While you are on your own side, your own country's government is responsible for you. If you step across that line into another country because you fear you will be harmed, a different rulebook, international refugee law, can apply, and that country generally is not allowed to push you back into the danger. People who fled the fire but stayed on their own side of the line are the internally displaced.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The painted-line picture is too simple. Whether someone counts as a refugee depends on why they left, not just which side of a line they stand on, so a person can cross a border and still be treated as a migrant if the reason was not persecution. It also skips the hard part in the middle: an asylum claim has to be judged well-founded, decisions take time, and non-refoulement has narrow exceptions that a single painted line cannot show.

## Worked Example

Picture three people leaving the same country during a period of unrest. The first is a journalist threatened with arrest for her reporting; she crosses into a neighboring country and applies for protection. She is an asylum-seeker while her case is pending and, if her fear of persecution is judged well-founded on a Convention ground such as political opinion, a refugee, whom that country cannot lawfully return home. The second flees fighting in his village and moves to a safer city in the same country; he has not crossed a border, so he is an internally displaced person, still under his own government's responsibility. The third is unaffected by the violence but takes a better-paying job abroad; he is a migrant, moving by choice, with no automatic claim to international protection. Same origin, three legal categories, three very different sets of rights, which is exactly why the definitions are worth getting right.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using refugee and migrant as interchangeable words.** A refugee has a specific legal status and protection under the 1951 Convention; migrant is a broad term with no international legal definition and no automatic right to protection. The reason for moving, persecution versus choice, is what separates them.
- **Assuming a person is only a refugee once a government has approved their case.** Refugee status is declaratory: a person is a refugee as soon as they meet the definition. Someone awaiting a decision is an asylum-seeker and should be treated as a refugee until determined otherwise.
- **Thinking all displaced people have crossed a border.** The largest displaced group is internally displaced persons, who remain inside their own country. At the end of 2024 they were roughly 60 percent of the global total, per UNHCR.
- **Calling everyone who flees a disaster or climate change a refugee.** The 1951 Convention ties refugee status to persecution on five specific grounds. People displaced by disasters usually fall outside that definition, becoming IDPs or migrants, a recognized gap in the law.
- **Quoting a displacement figure with no year attached.** These numbers change every year. Always state the date and the source, for example about 123 million forcibly displaced at the end of 2024 per UNHCR, rather than a bare number.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Refugee vs. Internally displaced person (IDP):** Both are forced from home, often for the same reasons, but a refugee has crossed an international border while an IDP remains within their own country and under its jurisdiction.
- **Refugee vs. Asylum-seeker:** A refugee meets the Convention definition and is recognized as such; an asylum-seeker has lodged a claim that has not yet been decided. Because status is declaratory, the difference is one of recognition, not of the underlying situation.
- **Refugee vs. Migrant:** A refugee flees persecution and is entitled to international protection, including non-refoulement; a migrant is anyone who moves, often voluntarily, and has no automatic claim to that protection.

## Key Takeaway

Forced displacement splits into precise legal categories, refugee, asylum-seeker, and internally displaced person, that differ from a voluntary migrant, and those categories, anchored in the 1951 Refugee Convention and its non-refoulement rule, determine who is protected from being returned to danger.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Under the 1951 Refugee Convention, which situation best fits the definition of a refugee?**
   - A. A person who moves to another country to take a higher-paying job
   - B. A person outside their country with a well-founded fear of persecution based on their political opinion, unable to safely return
   - C. A person who flees fighting but relocates to a safer region within their own country
   - D. A person who applies for a student visa to study abroad

   **Answer: B.** The Convention defines a refugee as someone outside their country of nationality with a well-founded fear of persecution on one of five grounds, including political opinion, who cannot safely return. Moving for a job or study is voluntary migration, and fleeing within one's own country makes a person an IDP.

2. **What single feature most clearly distinguishes an internally displaced person (IDP) from a refugee?**
   - A. An IDP fled voluntarily while a refugee was forced to flee
   - B. An IDP faces persecution while a refugee flees only economic hardship
   - C. An IDP has already received legal status while a refugee has not
   - D. An IDP has not crossed an international border, whereas a refugee has

   **Answer: D.** The defining line is the border. IDPs are forced from their homes but remain within their own country, while refugees have crossed into another state. Both are forced to flee, IDPs can face the same persecution as refugees, and IDPs are not defined by any special legal status.

3. **A person has crossed a border, applied for protection, and is waiting for the government to decide their case. What are they, and how should they be treated in the meantime?**
   - A. An asylum-seeker, who should be treated as a refugee until it is determined they are not
   - B. A migrant, who has no claim to any protection while waiting
   - C. An internally displaced person, because their case is not yet decided
   - D. Already a full refugee, because the government has approved the claim

   **Answer: A.** A person awaiting a decision on a protection claim is an asylum-seeker, and because refugee status is declaratory, UNHCR guidance holds they should be treated as refugees until determined otherwise. They have crossed a border, so they cannot be IDPs, and a pending claim means status has not been formally confirmed.

4. **The principle of non-refoulement, at the core of the 1951 Convention, primarily requires that:**
   - A. Every country must grant citizenship to any refugee who arrives
   - B. Refugees must return home as soon as a conflict formally ends
   - C. A refugee must not be expelled or returned to a territory where their life or freedom would be threatened
   - D. Displaced people must be resettled only in high-income countries

   **Answer: C.** Article 33 prohibits expelling or returning a refugee to a place where their life or freedom would be threatened on the protected grounds. It does not require citizenship, does not force return when conflict ends, and does not limit resettlement to wealthy states; in fact most refugees are hosted by low- and middle-income countries.

5. **A family leaves their home after repeated flooding destroys their farmland and moves to a city in a neighboring country to find work. Based on the standard legal categories, how are they most accurately described, and why?**
   - A. Refugees, because any cross-border move caused by hardship qualifies under the 1951 Convention
   - B. Migrants, because displacement by disaster generally does not meet the Convention's persecution-based definition of a refugee
   - C. Internally displaced persons, because farmland loss is an internal matter
   - D. Asylum-seekers, because leaving after a flood automatically starts a protection claim

   **Answer: B.** The Convention ties refugee status to a well-founded fear of persecution on five grounds, and a disaster such as flooding does not fit that test, so people who cross a border after a disaster are generally classified as migrants, a widely noted gap in the law. They are not IDPs because they crossed a border, and not asylum-seekers unless they actually lodge a protection claim.
