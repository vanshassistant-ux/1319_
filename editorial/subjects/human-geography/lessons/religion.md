# The Geography of Religion

## In 30 Seconds

Geographers study religion the way they study language or ethnicity: as a human pattern spread across space, not as a set of truth claims to judge. They ask where religions began, how they moved from those hearths, and how faith marks the land in places of worship, sacred sites, burial grounds, and calendars. A key distinction is between universalizing religions, which seek converts among all peoples, and ethnic religions, tied to a particular people or place.

## Why This Matters

Religion shapes where people build, gather, bury their dead, and mark time, so reading a cultural landscape means reading its religious imprint. Courses in human geography, sociology, and international studies use the universalizing-versus-ethnic distinction and the idea of diffusion from a hearth to explain patterns that show up on any world map of belief. Doing this well requires describing every tradition even-handedly and grounding population claims in dated, attributed survey data rather than impressions. The same descriptive habits transfer to analyzing migration, urban form, conflict, and the growing category of people who report no religious affiliation.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain how a geographer studies religion descriptively, distinct from making or judging theological claims.
- Distinguish universalizing religions from ethnic religions and give examples of each.
- Describe how religions diffuse from hearths through expansion and relocation diffusion.
- Identify how religion imprints the cultural landscape through sacred space, worship sites, burial, and calendars.
- Interpret the religiously unaffiliated as a descriptive demographic category using dated, attributed figures.

## The College Version

### What it means to study religion geographically

A geographer approaches religion as a spatial and cultural phenomenon: a system of beliefs and practices that is distributed unevenly across the earth's surface and that leaves visible marks on places. The geographer's questions are descriptive, not evaluative. Where did a tradition originate? By what routes did it spread? How does it shape settlement, architecture, land use, and the rhythm of the year? Crucially, this work never assesses whether any tradition's claims are true and never ranks traditions against one another. That neutrality is a methodological requirement, the same stance a geographer takes toward language or ethnicity. Religion sits under the broader umbrella of culture, alongside language and ethnicity, and this lesson owns specifically the geography of belief: its distribution, diffusion, and landscape imprint. Some useful vocabulary describes the internal structure of belief without endorsing it: monotheism is belief in one god, polytheism belief in many, and a syncretic religion is one formed by blending elements of others. These are analytic labels, applied to describe, not to rank.

### Universalizing and ethnic religions

Geographers commonly sort religions into two spatial categories. A universalizing religion seeks converts and is open, in principle, to all peoples regardless of place or ancestry; Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism are the standard examples. An ethnic religion is closely associated with a particular people or place and generally does not actively seek outside converts; Hinduism and Judaism are the usual examples. The distinction is geographic and descriptive, capturing how a tradition relates to territory and membership, not a judgment of any tradition's value or a full account of how adherents understand themselves. It also explains distribution: universalizing religions tend to be spread widely across many regions, while ethnic religions tend to remain concentrated among a particular population or in a particular homeland, moving mainly when their adherents move. As with any model, the two categories are useful generalizations with blurred edges; individual traditions and communities do not always fit neatly.

### Hearths and diffusion

Every religion has a hearth, the area where it originated before spreading outward. The hearths of the largest universalizing religions lie in Asia: Christianity and Islam arose in Southwest Asia around the eastern Mediterranean and the Arabian Peninsula, and Buddhism arose in South Asia near the present India-Nepal border. From a hearth a religion diffuses, and geographers name the mechanisms. In expansion diffusion the faith grows outward from person to person or place to place while remaining strong at its source; this includes contagious diffusion through direct proselytizing and hierarchical diffusion, in which the conversion of a ruler or elite leads a wider population to follow. In relocation diffusion the faith travels because believers themselves migrate, carrying their practices to a new region and establishing communities there. Universalizing religions, because they seek converts, have spread far beyond their hearths through missionaries, traders, migrants, and sometimes political power. Ethnic religions have spread mainly through relocation as their adherents migrated, which is why they often remain tied to a homeland and its diaspora.

### Sacred space and the cultural landscape

Religion is one of the most legible forces in the cultural landscape, the human imprint on the visible environment. Sacred spaces are places associated with a sense of the divine, and they range from purpose-built structures to natural features and whole cities that a tradition regards as holy. Places of worship such as churches, mosques, temples, synagogues, and gurdwaras differ in form, orientation, and prominence, and their density and design tell a geographer which traditions are present and how central they are to a community. Religion also organizes death and time: burial customs and the placement of cemeteries mark the land, and religious calendars set the timing of festivals, rest days, and pilgrimage, which in turn shape travel, commerce, and daily rhythm. Pilgrimage sites concentrate movement toward particular locations at particular seasons. Reading these features lets a geographer describe the distribution and influence of religions without ever endorsing or evaluating their beliefs.

### Measuring belief and the unaffiliated

Because religious affiliation is a demographic variable, geographers rely on survey and census data and must date and attribute every figure, since these numbers change and are estimates. Drawing on more than 2,700 censuses and surveys, the Pew Research Center estimated the world's religious composition for 2020 as follows: Christians were the largest group at about 2.3 billion people, roughly 28.8 percent of the world's population; Muslims were about 25.6 percent and were the fastest-growing group over the 2010 to 2020 decade; Hindus were about 14.9 percent; Buddhists about 4.1 percent; Jews about 0.2 percent; and other religions, a category Pew uses for many smaller and folk or traditional traditions, about 2.2 percent. A separate and growing category is the religiously unaffiliated, sometimes called the nones, meaning people who do not identify with any religion; Pew estimated them at about 1.9 billion, roughly 24.2 percent of the world's population in 2020, making them the third-largest category. Secularization refers to the loosening of religion's hold on public life, and secular describes a separation between the state and religion. Geographers treat the unaffiliated as one descriptive category among others, mapping where the group is concentrated rather than reading the trend as a verdict on any tradition.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Universalizing religion**: A religion that seeks converts and is open in principle to people of any background or place; Christianity, Islam, and Buddhism are the usual examples.
- **Ethnic religion**: A religion closely tied to a particular people or place that generally does not actively seek outside converts; Hinduism and Judaism are the usual examples.
- **Hearth**: The area where a religion or other cultural trait originated before spreading outward.
- **Expansion diffusion**: Spread in which a trait grows outward from person to person or place to place while remaining established at its source, including contagious and hierarchical forms.
- **Relocation diffusion**: Spread that occurs because people who carry a trait migrate and re-establish it in a new location.
- **Sacred space**: A place associated with a sense of the divine, ranging from a built house of worship to a natural feature or a whole city held to be holy.
- **Cultural landscape**: The visible human imprint on the environment, including the marks that religion leaves through worship sites, burial grounds, and land use.
- **Religiously unaffiliated**: People who do not identify with any religion, a demographic category sometimes called the nones; distinct from any single belief system.
- **Secularization**: A process in which religion's influence over public institutions and daily life weakens; the related term secular denotes separation between the state and religion.
- **Syncretic religion**: A religion formed by blending elements drawn from two or more existing traditions.

## Eli-10

A geographer studying religion is not asking who is right. They are asking questions you could answer with a map and a notebook: Where did this belief start? How did it travel to new places? What does it build, and where do people gather, celebrate, and bury their dead? Some religions try to welcome everyone anywhere, so they end up spread all over the world. Others stay closely tied to one people or one homeland and mostly move when those people move. Geographers also count how many people follow each religion, and they always write down the year and who did the counting, because the numbers keep changing and they are careful estimates, not exact totals.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of religions like plants spreading across a garden. A universalizing religion is like a dandelion: its seeds blow everywhere and sprout in many gardens far from where they started. An ethnic religion is more like a plant that mostly stays in its original bed and only appears elsewhere when a gardener carries a cutting to a new home.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy captures how far and by what means traditions spread, but it breaks down quickly. Seeds move by accident, while religions spread through human choices, teaching, migration, and history, and a plant does not decide anything. It also should not suggest one kind of plant is better than another; the geographer describes the spreading, never ranks the gardens.

## Worked Example

Suppose a geographer is handed an unlabeled neighborhood map dotted with houses of worship, a large cemetery, and notes on local festival dates, and asked to describe the religious landscape without judging any faith. They start with distribution: which traditions have buildings here, how many, and how prominent. They read the cemetery's layout and burial customs as another landscape clue. They check the festival calendar to see how belief structures the local year and where pilgrimage or travel spikes. To place the neighborhood in a wider context, they turn to dated survey data, noting for example that Pew estimated Christians at about 28.8 percent and the religiously unaffiliated at about 24.2 percent of the world's population in 2020. At no point do they assess whether any tradition is true; every statement is descriptive and, for population claims, dated and attributed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the geography of religion as a place to evaluate or rank beliefs.** The geographer describes distribution, diffusion, and landscape imprint only, and never assesses the truth or worth of any tradition.
- **Assuming ethnic religions are smaller or less important than universalizing ones.** The universalizing-versus-ethnic distinction is about seeking converts and ties to place, not about size or value; Hinduism, an ethnic religion, had roughly 14.9 percent of the world's population in Pew's 2020 estimate.
- **Reading the religiously unaffiliated as proof that religion is vanishing everywhere.** The unaffiliated are one descriptive category among others; geographers map where the group is concentrated rather than treating a global percentage as a verdict on belief.
- **Citing adherent figures as fixed, exact, and timeless.** These are survey-based estimates that change; always attach the year and the source, such as Pew Research Center estimates for 2020.
- **Confusing relocation diffusion with expansion diffusion.** Relocation diffusion moves a faith because believers migrate; expansion diffusion spreads it outward through conversion while it stays established at its source.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Universalizing religion vs. Ethnic religion**: A universalizing religion seeks converts among all peoples and tends to be spread widely; an ethnic religion is tied to a particular people or place and tends to stay concentrated, moving mainly through the migration of its adherents.
- **Expansion diffusion vs. Relocation diffusion**: Expansion diffusion spreads a faith outward through conversion while it remains strong at its hearth; relocation diffusion spreads it because believers themselves move to new regions and re-establish it.
- **An ethnic religion vs. The religiously unaffiliated**: An ethnic religion is a specific belief system tied to a people or place; the religiously unaffiliated is a residual demographic category for people who report no religious identity, not a religion.

## Key Takeaway

Geographers describe religion spatially, mapping where traditions began, how they diffused from their hearths, and how they mark the land, using dated and attributed figures and never judging any belief. The universalizing-versus-ethnic distinction and the growing unaffiliated category are core descriptive tools.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In geographic terms, what distinguishes a universalizing religion from an ethnic religion?**
   - A. A universalizing religion is always older than an ethnic religion
   - B. A universalizing religion seeks converts among all peoples, while an ethnic religion is tied to a particular people or place
   - C. An ethnic religion always has more adherents worldwide than a universalizing religion
   - D. An ethnic religion is defined by belief in a single god, a universalizing religion by belief in many

   **Answer: B.** The geographic distinction is about seeking converts and ties to place. Age, size, and number of gods are not the basis of the categories.

2. **According to Pew Research Center estimates for 2020, which was the world's largest religious group?**
   - A. Christians, at about 28.8 percent of the world's population
   - B. Muslims, at about 25.6 percent of the world's population
   - C. The religiously unaffiliated, at about 24.2 percent of the world's population
   - D. Hindus, at about 14.9 percent of the world's population

   **Answer: A.** Pew estimated Christians at about 2.3 billion, the largest group. Muslims were the fastest-growing but second in size; the unaffiliated were third-largest.

3. **A community of believers migrates to a new country and establishes places of worship there, but does not try to convert the surrounding population. Which process does this best illustrate?**
   - A. Expansion diffusion
   - B. Hierarchical diffusion
   - C. Relocation diffusion
   - D. Secularization

   **Answer: C.** Relocation diffusion occurs when believers themselves migrate and re-establish their faith. Expansion and hierarchical diffusion spread by conversion; secularization is not a mode of spread.

4. **A geographer maps a region's cemeteries, the orientation and density of its houses of worship, and its calendar of religious festivals. This work is best described as studying religion's imprint on the**
   - A. hearth
   - B. demographic transition
   - C. push-pull framework
   - D. cultural landscape

   **Answer: D.** These visible marks on the environment form the cultural landscape. A hearth is a place of origin; the other two are migration and population models.

5. **Pew estimated that the religiously unaffiliated made up about 24.2 percent of the world's population in 2020, the third-largest category. How should a geographer treat this group?**
   - A. As proof that religion is disappearing across every region
   - B. As a universalizing religion diffusing from a single hearth
   - C. As a descriptive demographic category of people who do not identify with any religion, mapped like other categories
   - D. As an ethnic religion tied to one particular nation

   **Answer: C.** The unaffiliated is a residual demographic category mapped descriptively; a global percentage is not proof religion is vanishing, and the category is not a religion at all.
