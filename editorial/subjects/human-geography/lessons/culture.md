# Culture

## In 30 Seconds

Culture is the shared, learned set of beliefs, values, practices, and material objects a group of people passes from one generation to the next. Geographers split it into material culture (tangible things like tools, food, and buildings) and non-material culture (beliefs, norms, values, and language). They track how culture clusters into regions, marks the land as a cultural landscape, and spreads from place to place through diffusion.

## Why This Matters

Culture is the umbrella idea beneath much of human geography: language, religion, and ethnicity are all elements of it. Learning to describe culture precisely, and neutrally, lets you read a landscape, a census map, or a news story without mistaking one group's habits for a universal norm. It explains why a street in one city looks and sounds different from a street in another, and why those differences shift as people and ideas move. Planners, public-health workers, marketers, and diplomats all depend on reading cultural patterns accurately. Because culture is learned rather than fixed, it also keeps changing, which makes it a live tool for understanding a connected, mobile world.

## Learning Objectives

- Define culture as learned, shared behavior and objects passed between people and generations.
- Distinguish material culture from non-material culture with examples of each.
- Explain how cultural traits, culture complexes, and culture regions relate.
- Compare folk (local) culture with popular culture across scale, change, and origin.
- Explain the cultural landscape and attribute the idea to Carl Sauer.
- Apply relocation and expansion diffusion to a scenario of cultural spread.

## The College Version

### What culture is

In everyday speech, "culture" can mean the arts, or refined taste. In human geography it means something broader and more precise: the shared, learned behaviors of a group of people, including their languages, belief systems, social structures, institutions, and material goods. Two features are essential. First, culture is learned, not inherited biologically. Almost every human behavior, from how you shop for dinner to how marriage is arranged, is something a person is taught by the community around them. In modern Paris many people buy cheese, meat, and vegetables daily from specialty stalls, while most people in the United States fill a large cart once a week at a supermarket; neither pattern is natural or normal in an absolute sense, and each can look strange to someone raised in the other. Second, culture is shared and passed on: people transmit it by teaching it to children and to newcomers, and they revise it constantly. Because culture is learned and shared rather than fixed, geographers describe cultural differences without ranking them, treating each group's way of life on its own terms.

### Material and non-material culture

Geographers separate culture into two linked halves. Material culture is the set of tangible objects and belongings a group makes and uses: tools, clothing, food, houses, vehicles, artwork, and the buildings and infrastructure of a settlement. Non-material culture is intangible: the ideas, attitudes, values, beliefs, norms, and language a society holds. The two are tightly connected, and physical objects often symbolize cultural ideas, a wedding ring, a flag, or a place of worship carries meaning far beyond its material. Keeping the halves distinct is useful because they behave differently. Material culture can be photographed, mapped, and counted; non-material culture must usually be inferred from what people say, do, and build. When a lesson later turns to language or religion, it is zooming in on specific elements of non-material culture.

### Traits, complexes, and regions

Geographers build up from small units to large ones. A cultural trait is a single element of culture, one practice, object, or belief, such as eating with chopsticks or celebrating a particular holiday. Related traits that function together form a culture complex; farming a specific crop, for example, may bind together tools, a calendar, foods, and rituals into one interlocking bundle. A culture region is a portion of Earth's surface whose people share common cultural elements. A region can be defined by a single component, such as the area where a language or religion dominates, or by many components at once, as when you characterize a country by its religion, language, architecture, and cuisine together. Like the historical eras used to organize world history, culture regions are drawn by geographers as deliberate simplifications; their boundaries are tools for making cultural variation understandable rather than hard natural lines.

### Folk culture and popular culture

One of the most useful contrasts in cultural geography is between folk and popular culture. Folk culture, sometimes called local culture, is practiced by small, homogeneous groups that see themselves as distinct, often in rural or physically isolated settings, or in communities that deliberately hold popular culture at arm's length. It varies a great deal from place to place but changes slowly, and the time, hearth, and original innovator of a folk trait are usually unknown because the trait grew out of everyday local life. Popular culture, by contrast, is found in large, heterogeneous, urban societies that share cultural elements such as media. It varies little from place to place, because it is commodified and distributed globally, but it changes quickly, and the origin of a given pop-culture innovation is usually well documented in a major city. Neither is superior; they are different modes of cultural life operating at different scales.

### The cultural landscape

Culture leaves visible marks on the land, and the sum of those marks is the cultural landscape: the imprint of human activity on the physical environment. Field patterns, road networks, building styles, religious structures, and place names all record the choices of the people who shaped a place. A helpful image is the palimpsest, a reused manuscript page on which older writing shows faintly beneath the new; a landscape likewise carries traces of past uses that frame what comes next. The idea that culture fashions the natural landscape into a cultural landscape is most influentially associated with the American geographer Carl O. Sauer, whose 1925 paper "The Morphology of Landscape," written at the University of California, Berkeley, helped shift geography away from environmental determinism toward the study of how human cultures shape the land. Sauer did not invent the underlying idea single-handedly, but his formulation became foundational for cultural geography.

### Cultural diffusion

Culture moves, so the geography of culture is always changing. Cultural diffusion is the spread of culture, both material and non-material, and the processes that carry it: migration, trade, communication, and commerce. Traits that originate in a source area, the cultural hearth, spread outward to characterize a larger territory. Geographers distinguish two broad modes. In relocation diffusion, people physically move and take their "cultural baggage" with them, so a trait appears in a new place because its carriers relocated there, as when immigrants establish a cuisine or festival far from its origin. In expansion diffusion, a trait spreads to new places while remaining strong in its original location, passing from person to person or place to place. Expansion diffusion has recognizable sub-types, including contagious diffusion (person-to-person copying), hierarchical diffusion (from large cities down to smaller towns), and stimulus diffusion (the core idea is adopted even when a specific element is rejected). Culture region tells you where a trait is; diffusion explains how and why it got there.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Culture** — The shared, learned beliefs, values, practices, and material objects of a group of people, taught to others and passed between generations rather than biologically inherited.
- **Material culture** — The tangible objects a group makes and uses, such as tools, clothing, food, buildings, and art.
- **Non-material culture** — The intangible parts of culture: ideas, values, beliefs, norms, and language.
- **Cultural trait** — A single element of culture, such as one practice, object, or belief.
- **Culture complex** — A set of interrelated cultural traits that function together as a bundle.
- **Culture region** — A portion of Earth's surface whose people share common cultural elements, defined by one or many components.
- **Folk culture** — Culture practiced by small, often rural or isolated homogeneous groups; varies greatly from place to place but changes slowly.
- **Popular culture** — Culture shared across large, heterogeneous, urban societies; varies little from place to place but changes quickly and is commodified globally.
- **Cultural landscape** — The visible imprint of human culture on the physical environment, such as buildings, field patterns, and place names.
- **Cultural diffusion** — The spread of culture across space through migration, trade, and communication; occurs by relocation or by expansion.

## Eli-10

Culture is everything a group of people learns from each other and passes down: the foods they cook, the words they use, the holidays they keep, the way they build houses, and the beliefs they hold. You are not born knowing any of it; you pick it up from the people around you. Some of culture is stuff you can touch, like clothes and tools, and some of it is invisible, like values and manners. When people move or trade, they carry their culture with them, so ideas and habits spread from place to place and slowly change.

## Eli's Analogy

Culture is like a recipe a family passes down. Each generation cooks the dish, tweaks it a little, and teaches the next cook. Move the family to a new town and the recipe travels too, sometimes swapping an ingredient the new place does not have.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A recipe is one fixed set of steps, but a whole culture is thousands of overlapping "recipes" for living, and no single person holds all of them. A recipe also has a known author, while most cultural habits have no traceable inventor, and unlike a dish you cook on purpose, much of culture is absorbed without anyone deciding to teach it.

## Worked Example

A geography student maps a neighborhood in a mid-sized U.S. city and finds a cluster of Vietnamese restaurants, a Buddhist temple, and shop signs in Vietnamese near an older Catholic church and a diner. Reading it culturally: the restaurants and temple are material culture; the language on the signs and the beliefs behind the temple and church are non-material culture. The Vietnamese cluster is a small culture region within the city. It most likely formed through relocation diffusion, immigrants carrying their language, cuisine, and religion to a new place, while the chain diner reflects popular culture that spread by expansion diffusion. The student describes both groups side by side, without treating either as the normal or default one.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using "culture" to mean only the fine arts, museums, or sophistication.** In geography, culture is the whole learned way of life of a group, from food and tools to values and language, not just the arts or good taste.
- **Treating culture as something people are born with, like a fixed trait of a place.** Culture is learned and shared, so it is transmitted and revised constantly; the same person raised elsewhere would learn a different culture.
- **Assuming folk culture is "backward" and popular culture is "advanced."** They differ in scale, speed of change, and how they spread, not in worth; geographers describe both neutrally without ranking cultures.
- **Confusing relocation diffusion with expansion diffusion.** In relocation diffusion the people themselves move and carry the trait; in expansion diffusion the trait spreads outward while staying strong in its source area.
- **Thinking the cultural landscape means untouched natural scenery.** The cultural landscape is precisely the human imprint on the land, buildings, roads, fields, and place names, which is why Sauer distinguished it from the natural landscape.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Material culture vs. Non-material culture** — Material culture is tangible objects you can touch or photograph; non-material culture is intangible ideas, values, norms, and language.
- **Folk culture vs. Popular culture** — Folk culture is small-scale, local, slow to change, with an unknown origin; popular culture is large-scale, global, quick to change, and commodified.
- **Relocation diffusion vs. Expansion diffusion** — Relocation diffusion spreads a trait because its carriers physically move; expansion diffusion spreads a trait to new places while it stays strong in its source.

## Key Takeaway

Culture is the learned, shared way of life a group passes on, split into material objects and non-material ideas; geographers track how it clusters into regions, marks the land as a cultural landscape, and spreads through relocation and expansion diffusion, describing every culture neutrally.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In human geography, which statement best defines culture?**
   - A. The biological traits a group inherits from its ancestors.
   - B. The shared, learned beliefs, values, practices, and material objects a group passes on to others.
   - C. Only the fine arts, literature, and refined taste of a society.
   - D. The natural climate and terrain that shape where people live.

   **Answer: B.** Culture is the learned behavior of a group, including languages, beliefs, institutions, and material goods, shared and taught rather than inherited. It is not biological (A), not limited to the fine arts (C), and not the physical environment (D).

2. **Which pairing correctly matches an item to its category of culture?**
   - A. A society's religious beliefs are an example of material culture.
   - B. A spoken language is an example of material culture.
   - C. A group's pottery, tools, and buildings are examples of material culture.
   - D. A community's values and norms are examples of material culture.

   **Answer: C.** Material culture is the tangible objects a group makes and uses, so pottery, tools, and buildings qualify. Beliefs (A), language (B), and values and norms (D) are intangible and belong to non-material culture.

3. **Which description fits popular culture rather than folk culture?**
   - A. Practiced by a small, homogeneous group in a physically isolated rural area.
   - B. Varies greatly from place to place but changes very slowly over time.
   - C. Has an innovator and hearth that are usually unknown because it grew from everyday local life.
   - D. Found in large, heterogeneous, urban societies; changes quickly and is commodified globally.

   **Answer: D.** Popular culture is large-scale, urban, fast-changing, and commodified worldwide (D). The other options describe folk culture: small isolated groups (A), great place-to-place variation with slow change (B), and unknown origins (C).

4. **Immigrants from one country settle in a distant city and open restaurants serving their home cuisine, establishing a dish that was previously unknown there while it remains common back home. Which process does this best illustrate?**
   - A. Relocation diffusion
   - B. Expansion diffusion
   - C. A cultural universal
   - D. Environmental determinism

   **Answer: A.** The trait appears in a new place because the people carrying it physically moved there, which is relocation diffusion. Expansion diffusion (B) would spread the dish outward without its carriers relocating. A cultural universal (C) is a trait common to all cultures, and environmental determinism (D) is the discredited idea that environment dictates culture.

5. **A student photographs a valley's terraced fields, farmhouses, irrigation canals, and a hilltop shrine and calls it a cultural landscape. Why is that label appropriate, and who is most associated with the concept?**
   - A. It is appropriate because the valley is untouched wilderness; the concept traces to Ravenstein.
   - B. It is inappropriate because landscapes are purely natural; the concept traces to Thomas Malthus.
   - C. It is appropriate because the scene is the visible imprint of human culture on the land; the concept traces to Carl Sauer.
   - D. It is appropriate only if no people currently live there; the concept traces to Alfred Wegener.

   **Answer: C.** A cultural landscape is the visible human imprint on the environment, exactly what terraced fields, canals, and a shrine represent, and the influential formulation is associated with Carl Sauer's 1925 "Morphology of Landscape." It is not untouched wilderness (A), not purely natural (B), and does not require the site to be empty of people (D); Ravenstein, Malthus, and Wegener are unrelated to the cultural landscape concept.
