# Torts

## In 30 Seconds

A tort is a civil wrong: an act or omission that invades a legal right or causes legally recognized harm and can lead to civil liability. Tort law generally addresses a claimant's request for a remedy, rather than a criminal prosecution by the government. In U.S. law, tort rules are largely shaped by state common law and statutes, so the elements, defenses, and remedies can differ by jurisdiction. This lesson is a high-level framework, not advice about any situation.

## Why This Matters

Tort concepts help students distinguish a claim for injury from a broken promise, a regulatory violation, or a crime. That distinction matters when reading business news, assessing risk in a hypothetical, or understanding why businesses use safety procedures, training, insurance, and documentation. It also encourages precise language: an allegation is not a finding of liability, and a harmful event does not automatically establish a tort. The applicable law, evidence, defenses, damages, and procedural requirements vary by jurisdiction and facts.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a tort as a civil wrong and identify its general function.
- Distinguish tort claims from criminal prosecutions and ordinary contract claims.
- Classify a hypothetical at a high level as intentional, negligence-based, or strict-liability-based.
- Explain the difference between compensatory damages, punitive damages, and an injunction.
- Apply the framework without predicting liability in a real dispute.

## The College Version

### Torts as civil wrongs

A tort is a civil wrong for which a court may impose liability. In broad terms, the law asks whether an act or omission invaded a legally protected interest or caused legally recognized harm, and whether the law supplies a civil remedy. The word civil matters. A tort claim is ordinarily brought by an injured person or organization—often called the plaintiff—against a defendant. The claimant may seek money, an order, or another legally available remedy. This differs from a criminal prosecution, in which the government seeks to prove an offense and obtain an authorized criminal penalty. The same conduct can raise both kinds of questions, but neither kind of case automatically decides the other.

Tort law has several goals. A remedy may compensate a successful claimant for a legally recognized loss, assign responsibility to someone whose conduct meets a claim's requirements, and discourage harmful conduct. Those goals do not mean every injury produces liability. The claimant must use a legally recognized theory, meet the elements that apply in the jurisdiction, and overcome any available defenses or limits. A court's conclusion depends on evidence and governing law; it cannot be inferred merely because an accident, an insult, or a business loss occurred.

For U.S. introductory study, it is especially important not to treat tort law as one nationwide code. Much tort law develops through state court decisions, state statutes, and jurisdiction-specific doctrines. Courts may use Restatements as influential guides, but a Restatement is not itself a universally controlling statute. Therefore phrases such as “always liable” or “everyone has the same remedy” are warning signs in a basic analysis. This lesson names shared categories, while the later negligence and product-liability lessons treat those fields in more detail.

### Three general categories

Introductory materials commonly group torts into intentional torts, negligence-based torts, and strict-liability torts. The categories are useful organizing tools, not a replacement for the particular claim and its elements. Intentional torts concern conduct undertaken with the legally relevant intent. Familiar examples include intentional harmful or offensive contact, conduct causing reasonable apprehension of imminent such contact, intentional confinement without lawful authority, and intentional intrusion onto land. Names and requirements differ across claims and jurisdictions. The key introductory point is not that a person meant for every later consequence to occur; it is that the claim turns on a legally relevant intentional act.

Negligence-based torts concern allegedly unreasonable conduct that causes legally recognized injury. A complete negligence analysis normally asks about duty, breach, causation, harm, and possible defenses. Those elements, standards, and applications are the subject of the separate Negligence lesson. Here, the contrast is enough: negligence does not require the same kind of intent as an intentional tort. Saying that someone was careless does not itself resolve what duty applied, whether the conduct breached it, or whether that conduct legally caused the claimed harm.

Strict liability is another route to liability in limited settings. Rather than centering the inquiry on whether the defendant acted carefully, a strict-liability rule may focus on a designated activity, product, or result and the other requirements of that rule. It does not mean that every harmful product or activity creates automatic liability, and it does not eliminate questions about proof, causation, defenses, or the law of the particular jurisdiction. Product-related strict liability has its own doctrinal details and belongs to the later Product Liability lesson. At this stage, strict liability is best understood as a category in which fault in the ordinary care sense is not the central issue.

### Remedies and careful classification

A tort claimant commonly requests damages: money a court may award after a legally sufficient showing. Compensatory damages are generally directed at making an injured party whole for legally recoverable loss. Depending on the jurisdiction and claim, losses can include direct economic costs and other recognized losses. A court may award punitive damages in addition to compensation in limited circumstances to punish or deter particularly blameworthy conduct. Punitive damages are not a routine add-on for every tort, and their availability is governed by applicable law.

Money is not the only possible civil remedy. An injunction is a court order that directs a party to do or stop specified conduct. It may be sought when money would not adequately address the harm, but the requirements are demanding and jurisdiction-specific. These remedies differ from a criminal fine or incarceration. A criminal fine is a public penalty; civil damages ordinarily address the successful claimant's legal injury. Keeping the terms separate avoids the common error of calling every payment a “fine.”

A sound first pass through a hypothetical is modest. First, identify the alleged conduct and the claimed injury or invaded interest. Next, ask whether the question sounds like an intentional-tort theory, a negligence-based theory, a strict-liability theory, a contract issue, a crime, or more than one category. Then identify what additional facts and governing law would be necessary before reaching a conclusion. For example, if a delivery worker enters fenced private land after being told not to enter, trespass may be a category to investigate; whether a particular claim succeeds depends on local rules, authority to enter, consent, proof, and other facts. The purpose of classification is to ask better questions, not to give a legal conclusion about a reader's case.

## Key Vocabulary

- **tort:** A civil wrong that can give rise to legal liability and a civil remedy.
- **liability:** Legal responsibility imposed when the requirements of a claim are established.
- **intentional tort:** A tort claim based on conduct undertaken with the legally required intent for that claim.
- **negligence:** A tort framework concerning unreasonable conduct that causes legally recognized injury; its elements are analyzed separately.
- **strict liability:** A limited liability framework in which ordinary care is not the central fault inquiry, subject to the governing claim and jurisdiction.
- **compensatory damages:** Money awarded to address legally recoverable loss after a successful civil claim.
- **punitive damages:** Additional damages that may be available under applicable law to punish or deter especially blameworthy conduct.
- **injunction:** A court order directing a party to do or stop specified conduct.

## Eli-10

Tort law is one way the legal system handles a person or business saying, “Someone harmed me or crossed a legal boundary, and I think a court should help fix it.” The person bringing the claim has to show more than that something unpleasant happened. They need a legal kind of claim and evidence that fits its rules.

Some tort claims are about a purposeful act, some are about careless conduct, and some use special rules where ordinary carefulness is not the main question. The category helps organize the investigation, but it is not an answer by itself. Different states can use different rules, and courts need facts and evidence before deciding anything.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a school repair desk sorting reports. A report that someone deliberately smashed a locker, a report that someone left a spill uncleared, and a report about a special piece of equipment might be routed to different checklists. Each checklist asks different questions before the school decides what repair is needed. Tort categories work similarly: they help identify the right legal questions before anyone assumes an answer.

The analogy has limits. A repair desk can make an immediate practical decision, while a court follows legal rules, procedures, and evidence standards. Torts can involve money, rights, and remedies far more complex than repairing a locker. The analogy also cannot tell whether a real person or business is liable.

## Worked Example

For a classroom hypothetical, a café's manager tells a vendor not to enter a fenced storage area. The next day, the vendor knowingly enters that area to retrieve a display stand and damages a locked gate. A student should not announce that the vendor is liable. Instead, the student can classify the issue: intentional entry onto land may point toward a trespass theory, while the gate damage raises questions about another possible civil claim and damages. The student would still need the governing jurisdiction's rules and facts about consent, authority, ownership, proof, and defenses. If the entry resulted from careless navigation rather than a knowing choice, the analysis might instead raise negligence questions, which require a different, fuller framework.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating any injury or financial loss as automatic proof of a tort.  
  **Correction:** A claimant must establish a recognized claim's requirements under the governing law; harm alone does not settle liability.
- **Mistake:** Calling every tort a crime.  
  **Correction:** Torts are civil wrongs, although some conduct can also lead to a separate criminal question.
- **Mistake:** Assuming strict liability means liability without any limits or proof.  
  **Correction:** Strict liability applies only to specified claims and still depends on the rule, evidence, causation, defenses, and jurisdiction.
- **Mistake:** Using negligence as a synonym for any bad outcome.  
  **Correction:** Negligence has legal elements; the separate negligence lesson explains that analysis.
- **Mistake:** Promising that a particular remedy will be available.  
  **Correction:** Remedies depend on the claim, law, evidence, defenses, and court procedure in the relevant jurisdiction.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tort claim | Criminal prosecution | A tort claim ordinarily seeks civil relief for a claimed legal injury; a criminal prosecution is brought by government to prove an offense and seek an authorized penalty. |
| Intentional tort | Negligence-based tort | The first centers on legally relevant intentional conduct; the second centers on alleged unreasonable conduct and requires its own elements analysis. |
| Compensatory damages | Punitive damages | Compensatory damages address legally recoverable loss; punitive damages, when allowed, serve punishment or deterrence and are not routine. |

## Key Takeaway

Torts are civil wrongs that can support civil liability and remedies, but the category alone never decides a case. Use it to identify whether an alleged harm may involve intentional conduct, negligence, or a limited strict-liability rule, then look to the governing jurisdiction and facts.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which description most accurately defines a tort?**  
   A. A civil wrong that can give rise to liability and a civil remedy.  
   B. Any breach of a private promise.  
   C. A criminal charge filed by a government prosecutor.  
   D. Any event that causes financial loss.  
   **Answer: A.** A tort is a civil wrong that can result in civil liability. Not every contract breach, criminal charge, or financial loss is a tort.

2. **Which category is distinguished by legally relevant purposeful conduct rather than ordinary carelessness?**  
   A. Compensatory damages  
   B. An intentional tort  
   C. A criminal prosecution  
   D. An injunction  
   **Answer: B.** Intentional torts concern conduct undertaken with the legally required intent. Damages and injunctions are remedies, while a prosecution is a separate legal process.

3. **A class hypothetical describes an accident after a worker allegedly ignored a safety step. What is the most careful initial conclusion?**  
   A. The worker is automatically liable because an injury occurred.  
   B. The event must be a strict-liability case because a safety step was involved.  
   C. The facts may raise a negligence question, but duty, breach, causation, harm, defenses, and local law still matter.  
   D. The only possible issue is a criminal charge.  
   **Answer: C.** Allegedly unsafe conduct may suggest a negligence framework, but an injury does not establish all elements or exclude defenses. The fuller negligence analysis is separate.

4. **Which statement best describes strict liability at this introductory level?**  
   A. It applies whenever a business makes a mistake.  
   B. It guarantees damages whenever a product causes dissatisfaction.  
   C. It means courts never consider evidence or defenses.  
   D. It is a limited framework in which ordinary care is not the central fault inquiry, subject to the particular claim and jurisdiction.  
   **Answer: D.** Strict liability is limited and claim-specific. It does not make every harmful event actionable or eliminate proof, causation, defenses, or jurisdictional rules.

5. **A court awards money to address a successful claimant's legally recoverable loss. What is that remedy generally called?**  
   A. Compensatory damages  
   B. An indictment  
   C. A criminal sentence  
   D. A licensing regulation  
   **Answer: A.** Compensatory damages are civil monetary relief directed at legally recoverable loss. An indictment and sentence are criminal terms; a regulation is a legal rule, not this remedy.
