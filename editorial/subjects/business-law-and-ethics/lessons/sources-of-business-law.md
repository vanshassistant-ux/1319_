# Sources of Business Law

## In 30 Seconds

Business law is not one book or one agency rule. In a U.S. question, start by identifying the jurisdiction and then the kind of legal material involved: a federal or state constitution, a statute, a regulation issued under delegated authority, or a judicial decision. Agency guidance can explain an agency's position, but it is not automatically the same as a binding rule. A source's authority depends on its level, issuer, and relationship to the specific question.

## Why This Matters

A business can encounter rules about contracts, workplace practices, consumer protection, property, taxes, licensing, and many other subjects. Calling all of them simply “the law” hides the questions that determine whether a source applies. Students who can distinguish a statute from a regulation or guidance are better able to read a case, locate primary authority, and avoid overclaiming. This framework also prevents a common mistake: treating a federal source as the answer to a state or local question. It is general education, not advice for a real dispute or compliance decision.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify constitutions, statutes, regulations, judicial decisions, and agency guidance as distinct legal materials.
- Explain why an agency regulation needs legal authority and why guidance may have a different effect.
- Distinguish common law developed through decisions from legislation enacted by a legislature.
- Apply a jurisdiction-and-authority check to a business-law hypothetical.
- Recognize the limits of a classroom hierarchy when current law, facts, and jurisdiction are unknown.

## The College Version

### Begin with jurisdiction, then identify the source

Business law is a convenient course label, not a separate universe of rules. A question about a business may involve constitutional limits, legislation, agency rules, judicial decisions, or materials that explain how an agency plans to administer a program. The first discipline is to ask which government has authority over the question. In the United States, relevant law can come from federal, state, tribal, territorial, or local institutions. The answer can change with the activity, the place, the parties, and the law being invoked. A federal source may matter greatly without replacing every state or local rule. Likewise, a state statute can be central without applying outside that state.

A constitution is the foundational legal instrument for its government. The U.S. Constitution creates and limits federal governmental power; state constitutions play a comparable foundational role within their states. Article VI of the U.S. Constitution says that the Constitution, federal laws made pursuant to it, and treaties made under U.S. authority are the supreme law of the land, and it binds state judges despite contrary state law. That statement is not a shortcut for saying “federal always wins.” It applies when valid federal law and state law conflict within the federal government's constitutional authority. A careful analysis must still identify the subject, the relevant jurisdiction, and the particular legal text.

For an introductory hierarchy, it is useful to picture a source as having both a level and a job. A constitution supplies the framework and limits. A legislature enacts statutes. An agency may issue regulations when it has delegated authority and follows the required process. Courts decide particular disputes and may establish or apply precedent. Guidance may help the public understand an agency's interpretation or policy, but its label alone does not establish binding legal effect. This lesson maps those source types; the neighboring court-systems lesson addresses institutional structure, and civil-versus-criminal-law addresses categories of cases rather than source authority.

### Statutes and regulations: connected, but not interchangeable

A statute is a law enacted by a legislature. Congress enacts federal statutes; state legislatures enact state statutes. The United States Code organizes the general and permanent federal laws by subject, but a code citation is not a substitute for reading the relevant section, definitions, effective dates, and amendments. State and local legislative materials have their own official publications and citation systems. For a student, the important point is institutional: a statute comes from a legislature exercising lawmaking power, not from an executive agency merely explaining its position.

A regulation is different. Agencies administer programs and may issue rules under authority that a legislature has given them. At the federal level, the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) is the codification of general and permanent rules published in the Federal Register by federal departments and agencies. GovInfo explains that the CFR is organized into fifty subject-matter titles, then chapters, parts, and sections. That structure helps readers locate a rule, but it does not answer whether a particular regulation is authorized, current, applicable, or controlling in a particular dispute. The electronic CFR is updated daily, while GovInfo describes it as an unofficial editorial compilation; for time-sensitive work, a reader must check the official materials and the governing context.

The relationship between a statute and a regulation is often described as delegation: a legislature sets a legal framework and authorizes an agency to make more detailed rules within stated limits. That relationship is why a regulation should be read alongside the statute that authorizes it and any later amendments. An agency cannot convert a preference into a rule simply by posting it online. The federal Administrative Procedure Act distinguishes rules from other agency statements, and procedures and exceptions can matter. In a college analysis, the responsible conclusion is modest: identify the statute and regulation, identify the issuing agency and jurisdiction, and state what still needs verification. Do not leap from a broad statutory purpose to a precise business obligation without the relevant text and facts.

### Judicial decisions, common law, and precedent

Courts also produce legal authority through decisions. A judicial opinion resolves a dispute under the law and explains the court's reasoning. Whether and how a decision controls a later question depends on such factors as the court's authority, jurisdiction, the legal issue actually decided, and later developments. A decision from the highest court in the relevant jurisdiction generally has a different role from a decision of a lower court or a court in another jurisdiction. Calling any case “precedent” without naming the court and jurisdiction is therefore incomplete.

Common law is law developed through judicial decisions rather than enacted statutes. Cornell's Legal Information Institute notes that U.S. courts developed doctrines through precedent and analogy to decided cases, and that state legislatures may later codify, change, or replace judge-made rules. This matters in business-law courses because subjects such as contracts and torts may involve both statutory texts and judicially developed doctrines. The existence of a statute does not always end the inquiry, and the existence of a case does not mean that a court-made rule overrides a valid governing statute. A reader must determine which source governs the specific issue.

A useful research habit is to distinguish primary authority from an explanation of primary authority. The text of a constitution, enacted statute, currently effective regulation, or judicial opinion is primary legal material. A textbook, agency FAQ, treatise, news article, or class note may help a student understand it, but does not replace it. Even primary materials require context: versions change, definitions may be supplied elsewhere, and a court may interpret a text in a way that matters to the question. The purpose of this distinction is not to make students act as their own lawyers; it is to teach them to state the limits of an answer and to know where a claim came from.

### Guidance is useful information, not a one-word answer about legal force

Administrative guidance includes documents such as interpretive materials, policy statements, manuals, and guidance letters. They can explain how an agency understands or intends to administer a statute or regulation. That makes them practically important: they may identify the agency's current position, describe a program, or point readers to controlling texts. But “guidance” is not a universal label for binding law. Cornell's explanation of federal administrative law distinguishes guidance actions from binding rulemaking, while also showing why the source, authority, procedure, and jurisdiction need checking. State agencies can operate under different statutes and state administrative-procedure rules.

Consider a hypothetical: a café receives an agency webpage suggesting a recordkeeping practice. A student should not answer only, “The webpage is law,” or, “Webpages never matter.” Instead, the student should ask: Which government and agency issued it? Does the page cite a statute or regulation? Is it a binding rule, an interpretation, a policy statement, or practical guidance? Does it apply to this business's location and activity? Is there an effective date or a later update? That method produces a research plan, not compliance advice. Actual decisions require current official sources and, when appropriate, qualified advice for the relevant jurisdiction.

The core skill is source-aware reasoning. Name the jurisdiction; identify the issuer and kind of material; locate any controlling constitutional, statutory, regulatory, or judicial authority; then describe the remaining uncertainty. This helps a student read business-law materials accurately without pretending that a single chart can decide a real legal question.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Jurisdiction:** The legal authority of a government or court over a place, persons, subject, or type of dispute.
- **Constitution:** A foundational legal document that establishes governmental powers, structures, and limits for a particular political system.
- **Statute:** A law enacted by a legislature, such as Congress or a state legislature.
- **Regulation:** An agency rule issued under delegated authority and subject to the applicable legal procedures.
- **Common law:** Law developed through judicial decisions and reasoning from earlier cases rather than enacted legislation.
- **Precedent:** A prior judicial decision that may guide or control a later court's analysis, depending on authority and jurisdiction.
- **Agency guidance:** An agency communication that explains an interpretation, policy, or practice and may differ from a binding regulation.
- **Primary authority:** An official legal source, such as a constitution, statute, regulation, or judicial opinion, used for the governing text.

## Eli-10

Think of law as instructions made by different authorized people. A constitution is the big rulebook that sets the basic limits. A legislature can make statutes, which are laws for a subject. An agency can make detailed regulations when the legislature gave it that job. Courts decide cases and may use earlier decisions to help decide new ones. An agency might also publish guidance that tells people how it reads or plans to use the rules.

The hard part is that a document's title does not answer every question. You need to know who issued it, where it applies, and whether it is a constitution, statute, regulation, court decision, or guidance. That helps you avoid treating one agency webpage as the answer to every business question.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like a school system: the district's governing rules set broad limits, the school board adopts policies, a principal may issue procedures under those policies, and a prior decision about a student situation may guide a similar later decision. A staff memo can explain what the school plans to do, but it may not change the board's rule.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Governments do not work exactly like a school. Constitutions, statutes, regulations, and court opinions have legal consequences that depend on formal authority, jurisdiction, and legal process. The analogy cannot decide whether a real document is binding or tell a business what it must do.

## Worked Example

Hypothetical only: A retailer operating in one state finds a federal agency's online guide about advertising. A careful student first records the guide's issuing agency and date, then follows its citations to any statute and regulation. The student next asks whether the business's conduct falls within federal authority and whether the retailer's state or city has additional rules. If a court decision is mentioned, the student identifies the court and the issue decided rather than assuming every case applies nationwide. The result is a source map: constitution and statutes at the appropriate level, any authorized regulations, relevant judicial decisions, and guidance as an explanation of the agency's position. It is not a conclusion about the retailer's compliance or liability.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a federal source as automatically controlling every state or local question.** Identify the jurisdiction, the valid source of authority, and the possible relationship between federal, state, and local law.
- **Calling an agency webpage a regulation without checking its source and status.** Determine whether it is a codified rule, guidance, policy statement, or explanation, and locate its supporting authority.
- **Assuming any court decision controls every later case.** Check the deciding court, jurisdiction, issue, and later legal developments.
- **Treating common law and statutes as synonyms.** Common law develops through judicial decisions; statutes are enacted by legislatures, and either may affect a topic.
- **Using a classroom source map as legal advice.** Use it to locate and evaluate sources; real disputes and compliance questions need current, fact-specific, jurisdiction-appropriate review.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Statute | Regulation | A legislature enacts a statute; an agency issues a regulation under authority and procedures supplied by law. |
| Regulation | Agency guidance | A regulation can be a binding agency rule; guidance commonly explains interpretation or policy and must be evaluated for its legal effect. |
| Common law | Statute | Common law develops through court decisions; a statute is enacted legislation that may codify, modify, or displace judge-made rules. |
| Primary authority | Explanatory material | Primary authority supplies official legal text or a decision; explanatory material helps interpretation but does not replace governing authority. |

## Key Takeaway

For a U.S. business-law question, begin by identifying the jurisdiction and the source type. Constitutions, statutes, regulations, judicial decisions, and agency guidance have different issuers and possible legal effects, so none should be treated as a universal shortcut.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which legal material is enacted by a legislature?**
   - A. A statute
   - B. A regulation
   - C. An agency guidance letter
   - D. A judicial opinion
   - **Answer: A.** A statute is enacted by a legislature. Regulations are agency rules, guidance letters communicate an agency position, and judicial opinions are issued by courts.

2. **What is the best high-level description of the federal Code of Federal Regulations?**
   - A. A collection of every court opinion in the United States
   - B. A codification of general and permanent rules published by federal departments and agencies
   - C. A list of constitutional amendments adopted by states
   - D. A set of nonbinding agency webpages only
   - **Answer: B.** GovInfo describes the CFR as the codification of general and permanent rules published in the Federal Register by federal departments and agencies. The other options describe different materials or are inaccurate.

3. **A student finds an agency FAQ about a licensing program. What should the student do before calling the FAQ a binding rule?**
   - A. Assume every agency communication has identical legal effect
   - B. Ignore the FAQ because explanations never matter
   - C. Check its issuer, jurisdiction, cited authority, and whether it is a regulation, guidance, or policy statement
   - D. Treat it as a court decision because it appears on an official website
   - **Answer: C.** An agency document's legal role depends on its source, authority, content, and context. An FAQ may be useful guidance, but it is not automatically a binding regulation or judicial decision.

4. **Which statement most accurately distinguishes common law from a statute?**
   - A. Common law is created only by federal agencies
   - B. A statute is an opinion written by a court
   - C. Common law has no relationship to later legislation
   - D. Common law develops through judicial decisions, while a statute is enacted by a legislature
   - **Answer: D.** Common law develops through judicial decisions and precedent; statutes are enacted legislation. Legislatures can codify, alter, or replace common-law rules, so the other claims are wrong.

5. **Why should a researcher identify jurisdiction before relying on a business-law source?**
   - A. Because a source's authority and application can depend on the government, place, subject, and parties involved
   - B. Because federal law can never affect a business
   - C. Because state laws automatically apply in every country
   - D. Because a source title alone proves its legal effect
   - **Answer: A.** Jurisdiction asks which government or legal body has authority over the question. A source may be important yet not control every location, subject, or party; the other answers make false universal claims.
