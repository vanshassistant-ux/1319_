# Discrimination Law Foundations

## In 30 Seconds

Employment-discrimination law uses specific statutes, protected bases, coverage rules, evidence frameworks, procedures, and remedies. In a federal U.S. overview, Title VII addresses discrimination because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin; other federal laws address age, disability, and genetic information. Disparate treatment and disparate impact are different legal theories. Neither label decides a real workplace event, because current law, jurisdiction, coverage, facts, procedure, and proof matter.

## Why This Matters

A discrimination-law foundation helps students replace vague statements such as “that seems unfair” with more precise questions: Which law might be relevant? Who and what does it cover? What protected basis, employment practice, evidence, and process are involved? It also prevents two opposite mistakes: assuming every unequal result proves unlawful discrimination, or assuming a neutral-sounding rule cannot create a legal issue. This U.S. overview cannot assess a particular job, employer, complaint, accommodation, charge, deadline, or lawsuit.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify protected-basis and coverage questions as separate parts of a federal employment-discrimination analysis.
- Distinguish disparate treatment from disparate impact at a high level.
- Explain why a neutral policy, an unequal outcome, and an unlawful practice are not interchangeable labels.
- Describe remedies and filing procedures as statute-, fact-, and jurisdiction-dependent.
- Use a fictional workplace scenario to identify questions without deciding a claim.

## The College Version

### Start with the legal source, protected basis, and coverage

Employment discrimination is not one universal rule against every workplace decision that feels unfair. In a U.S. federal introduction, the first task is to identify the statute and the kind of workplace relationship or action involved. Title VII's employment-practice provision names race, color, religion, sex, and national origin. Other federal statutes enforced by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission address different bases, including age, disability, and genetic information. EEOC materials also describe retaliation for protected discrimination-related activity as a separate concern. The fact that categories appear together in an overview does not mean their wording, procedures, coverage, exceptions, or remedies are identical.

Coverage is likewise not an afterthought. EEOC's business-employer overview explains that many private-employer claims involving race, color, religion, sex, national origin, disability, or genetic information use a 15-employee, 20-calendar-week threshold, while age coverage uses a 20-employee threshold. That is a federal illustration, not a shortcut for deciding whether a particular organization is covered. The employer type, the law, the employee-count rules, the worker's status, the time period, and other statutory conditions can matter. A state statute or municipal ordinance might nevertheless govern an employer outside federal coverage.

The scope of the workplace action matters as well. Hiring, firing, pay, promotion, training, assignments, benefits, harassment, and employment tests can raise different factual questions. A protected basis is not proof that a decision occurred because of that basis. Conversely, an employer's stated rule or reason is not the last word on whether a legal issue exists. The sound classroom move is to name the possible authority, identify the alleged employment practice and protected basis, and reserve any conclusion for the applicable law and evidence. This lesson does not decide whether a real employer, manager, applicant, worker, union, or agency acted lawfully.

### Disparate treatment and disparate impact ask different questions

Disparate treatment is a high-level label for alleged different treatment because of a protected basis. In the EEOC's discussion of employment tests and selection procedures, the inquiry can include whether people were treated differently, whether there is evidence of bias, what reason is offered, and whether an offered reason conceals a prohibited reason. The word “treatment” matters: the framework is concerned with a claimed connection between a decision and a protected basis. It is not simply a comparison of who was pleased or disappointed by a workplace outcome.

Disparate impact is different. Under Title VII's statutory structure, the focus is a particular employment practice that causes a disparate impact on the covered bases named in that provision. The statute then sets out a burden-and-response framework involving job relatedness and business necessity, and, in the stated circumstances, an alternative employment practice. EEOC guidance uses testing and selection procedures as an accessible example: a policy can appear neutral in its words yet warrant a separate analysis if its operation disproportionately excludes a protected group. This is not a rule that every numerical difference establishes an unlawful practice. It is a theory with defined elements, evidence questions, and legal limits.

The contrast helps students avoid false shortcuts. “No one said anything biased” does not by itself resolve every disparate-impact question, because that theory does not begin with an allegation of intentional different treatment. “The outcomes differ” does not by itself establish disparate treatment or disparate impact, because the relevant law requires more than an observed difference. A class discussion can ask what policy was used, what group comparison is being made, what evidence is available, and which statute governs. It should not pronounce a real policy discriminatory or provide a method for defending, challenging, or redesigning one.

### Procedure and remedies are conditional, not automatic

A claim, complaint, charge, investigation, finding, and remedy are different stages or concepts. An allegation reports a concern; it is not a legal finding. The correct administrative route can depend on the employer, statute, employment sector, agency jurisdiction, and state or local process. EEOC explains that many federal employment-discrimination laws require a charge before a private federal-court action, while the Equal Pay Act differs. This lesson does not teach someone how to choose a forum, complete a form, preserve evidence, negotiate, or litigate. For a real concern, current official agency information and qualified local legal advice can be important.

Time limits are especially poor material for a universal slogan. EEOC's current guidance describes a general 180-calendar-day filing period that may be extended to 300 days when a state or local agency enforces a law prohibiting employment discrimination on the same basis. It notes different conditions for age claims and a different federal-sector process. Those statements are procedural orientation, not an individualized deadline calculation. The relevant date, type of alleged conduct, statute, employer, jurisdiction, and procedural history can matter; acting on a memorized number would be unsafe.

Likewise, remedies follow a legally sufficient result under the applicable authority; they are not a menu automatically available whenever someone reports discrimination. EEOC gives conditional examples such as placement, back pay, benefits, changes to a discriminatory practice, fees and costs, and, in some intentional-discrimination cases, compensatory or punitive damages. Availability, limits, and proof vary by statute, respondent, finding, and facts. A student can accurately say that relief is designed to address the proven legal harm without promising that a particular person will receive money, a job, reinstatement, or another outcome.

## Key Vocabulary

- **protected basis:** A characteristic or status that a particular anti-discrimination law identifies as protected in its stated scope.
- **coverage:** Whether a legal rule applies to the employer, person, relationship, conduct, and setting under its own conditions.
- **Title VII:** Federal employment-discrimination law that addresses specified employment practices because of race, color, religion, sex, or national origin.
- **disparate treatment:** A legal theory concerning alleged different treatment because of a protected basis under the law that governs.
- **disparate impact:** A statutory theory that examines whether a particular neutral employment practice produces a prohibited disproportionate effect under specified conditions.
- **employment practice:** A workplace policy, decision, test, procedure, or action that may be relevant under an employment law.
- **charge of discrimination:** A formal administrative allegation used in processes required or administered under particular employment-discrimination laws.
- **remedy:** Relief authorized by applicable law after the required legal showing, subject to statutory and factual limits.

## Eli-10

Think of discrimination law as a rulebook with several chapters, not one giant fairness button. First you ask which chapter might fit: federal law, state law, local law, or more than one. Then you ask who and what it covers. A rule about age can have different details from a rule about disability, for example.

There are also different ways to ask about a workplace rule. Disparate treatment is about whether a person was treated differently because of a protected basis. Disparate impact is about whether one particular rule that sounds neutral produces a prohibited uneven effect under the law. Neither question can be answered just by guessing from a short story.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like checking a school tournament's rulebook. Before saying a team broke a rule, you would identify the sport, the rule that applies, who is in the tournament, what happened, and what evidence exists. A score difference alone does not prove a rule violation, and a complaint is not the referee's final decision.

The analogy has limits. Workplace law is far more complex than a tournament. It has statutes, agencies, deadlines, evidence rules, jurisdiction-specific laws, and potentially serious consequences. It cannot decide a real employer action, complaint, deadline, remedy, or legal right.

## Worked Example

A fictional retail chain uses the same timed online screening exercise for every applicant to one job. A classroom report says the exercise produced different pass rates across a protected group, but it does not state the sample, the job requirements, the law, the employer's coverage, or any explanation for the pattern. A careful student does not declare the test lawful or unlawful. The student distinguishes a neutral policy from a disparate-treatment allegation, identifies that Title VII's disparate-impact framework can require attention to a particular practice and statutory conditions, and asks what evidence and authority would be needed. The exercise does not advise the chain, evaluate a real test, or tell any applicant how to proceed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating every workplace unfairness as the same legal claim.  
  **Correction:** Identify the asserted protected basis, employment practice, statute, coverage, and jurisdiction before assigning a legal label.
- **Mistake:** Assuming a neutral written policy cannot raise a discrimination-law question.  
  **Correction:** Keep disparate treatment distinct from the separate statutory disparate-impact framework and its proof requirements.
- **Mistake:** Assuming a different outcome alone proves discrimination.  
  **Correction:** An outcome can prompt a question, but the applicable legal theory requires specific elements, evidence, and defenses or responses.
- **Mistake:** Treating an EEOC filing number as every person's deadline.  
  **Correction:** Federal, state, local, age-related, and federal-sector procedures can differ; use current official information for a real matter.
- **Mistake:** Promising a remedy once someone makes an allegation.  
  **Correction:** Relief depends on the governing statute, coverage, legal finding, proof, respondent, and applicable limits.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protected basis | Coverage | A protected basis identifies what a law addresses; coverage asks whether that law applies to the people and setting involved. |
| Disparate treatment | Disparate impact | Treatment centers on alleged differential treatment because of a protected basis; impact examines a particular neutral practice and its effect under a statutory framework. |
| Allegation or charge | Finding | An allegation initiates or describes a concern; a finding is a legal or agency conclusion reached through the applicable process. |
| Remedy | Penalty | A remedy is authorized relief after the required legal showing; it is not an automatic punishment triggered by an accusation. |

## Key Takeaway

Federal employment-discrimination law requires disciplined sorting: identify the statute, protected basis, coverage, employment practice, legal theory, evidence, procedure, and possible remedies. Disparate treatment and disparate impact are distinct frameworks, and neither supports a conclusion about a real workplace without current jurisdiction-specific analysis.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which set of bases is named in Title VII's employment-practice provision?**
   - A. Age, disability, and genetic information
   - B. Citizenship, marital status, and political affiliation
   - C. Race, color, religion, sex, and national origin
   - D. Income, education level, and home address
   - **Answer:** C. Title VII identifies the listed bases in that provision.

2. **Why is employer coverage a separate question from the protected basis alleged?**
   - A. Every employer is covered by every federal employment law in the same way.
   - B. Coverage asks whether the statute applies under its own conditions, which can include employer type and size.
   - C. Coverage is decided only after a remedy is awarded.
   - D. A protected basis makes employee-count rules irrelevant.
   - **Answer:** B. A law can identify a protected basis yet apply only if its coverage requirements are met.

3. **A fictional employer uses one written screening test for all applicants. A report shows differing pass rates by a protected group, but gives no other facts. What is the most careful classroom response?**
   - A. The differing rates alone prove intentional disparate treatment.
   - B. The policy is automatically lawful because every applicant took the same test.
   - C. The report means the employer must pay damages immediately.
   - D. Identify a possible disparate-impact question, then ask which particular practice, statute, coverage, evidence, and legal conditions apply.
   - **Answer:** D. The facts can raise a question, but do not decide legality, intent, coverage, proof, or remedy.

4. **Which statement best distinguishes disparate treatment from disparate impact at a high level?**
   - A. Disparate treatment concerns alleged different treatment because of a protected basis; disparate impact examines a particular neutral practice and its effect under stated legal conditions.
   - B. Disparate treatment is a criminal offense, while disparate impact is a contract claim.
   - C. Disparate treatment requires no evidence, while disparate impact always requires intent.
   - D. They are two names for exactly the same legal theory.
   - **Answer:** A. The two theories ask different questions and each requires an applicable legal framework.

5. **What is the most accurate procedural caution about an EEOC charge deadline?**
   - A. Every worker always has exactly 180 days.
   - B. Every worker always has exactly 300 days.
   - C. The relevant period can vary; EEOC describes a general 180-day period that may extend in specified settings, with different rules for some claims and federal-sector matters.
   - D. Deadlines apply only after a court has made a finding.
   - **Answer:** C. A real deadline requires current, fact- and jurisdiction-specific confirmation.
