# Court Systems

## In 30 Seconds

In the United States, federal and state courts are separate but overlapping systems. A court must have authority to hear a particular matter and the relevant people before it can decide the case. Trial courts develop the record; appellate courts review claimed legal error. Earlier decisions can guide later courts, but a decision's binding force depends on the court, jurisdiction, and question involved.

## Why This Matters

Businesses encounter rules, disputes, regulators, and contracts across geographic and legal boundaries. Court-system literacy helps students ask disciplined preliminary questions: Which sovereign's law may matter? Is this a trial-level fact-finding problem or an appeal about a prior decision? What court decisions are controlling in this setting? Those questions are useful for research and communication, while actual forum, jurisdiction, and litigation decisions require current facts and qualified legal advice.

## Learning Objectives

- Describe the broad relationship between U.S. federal and state court systems.
- Distinguish the core functions of trial courts and appellate courts.
- Explain subject-matter jurisdiction and personal jurisdiction at a high level.
- Analyze how court level and geographic jurisdiction affect the force of precedent.
- Apply a court-system framework to identify questions that require further legal research.

## The College Version

### A dual system, not one national court ladder

The United States has a federal court system and separate state court systems. They are connected by the Constitution and by rules about jurisdiction, but neither is simply a lower branch of the other. Federal courts exercise the authority the Constitution and Congress give them. State courts are established under their own states' constitutions and laws. A single event may therefore raise more than one possible legal setting, and the applicable court can depend on the claims, parties, location, and governing law. This is a high-level structural point, not a shortcut for choosing a court in a real dispute.

At the federal level, the familiar general structure is district courts, courts of appeals, and the U.S. Supreme Court. U.S. Courts identifies 94 district courts as federal trial courts and 13 courts of appeals below the Supreme Court. Congress also has created specialized federal tribunals and courts; a simple three-level picture is useful, but incomplete. Federal court authority is limited rather than universal. Federal cases may involve, for example, the Constitution, federal laws, the United States as a party, or particular disputes crossing state or national lines. The precise statutory route matters, so a student should not treat a business's multi-state activity as automatically creating a federal case.

State structures share common patterns but are not identical. A state may have trial courts, one or more intermediate appellate courts, and a court of last resort; names and divisions vary. A court called a state's “supreme court” is commonly its highest state court, not a branch of the U.S. Supreme Court. State courts decide a large share of U.S. cases and ordinarily give the final word on state-law questions, subject to the U.S. Supreme Court's authority over qualifying federal questions. The lesson on sources of business law owns the hierarchy among constitutions, statutes, regulations, and judicial decisions; this lesson instead asks where courts fit and what gives a particular court power to act.

### Trial courts, appellate courts, and jurisdiction

A trial court is generally the place where a case's record is developed. It may receive filings, consider evidence, hear testimony, resolve factual disputes, and apply law to facts. In federal practice, the district courts are the principal trial courts. That does not mean every matter receives a jury trial or that every dispute reaches a trial; procedures and dispositions vary. The point is functional: the trial level ordinarily creates or manages the factual record from which later review proceeds.

An appellate court has a different job. Its central task is to review whether law was applied correctly in a trial court or, in some situations, by a federal administrative agency. Appeals are not normally a second trial with new witnesses and a new factual record. The court's authority, the type of ruling under review, the standard of review, and preservation rules all matter. Those procedural details are deliberately outside this overview. For court literacy, retain the distinction: trial courts primarily develop and decide the initial case; appellate courts review specific challenged decisions according to governing rules.

Jurisdiction means a court's legal authority to hear and decide a matter. Two high-level categories help organize the question. Subject-matter jurisdiction concerns the kind of case or legal issue a court is authorized to hear. Personal jurisdiction concerns a court's authority with respect to the people or entities involved. They answer different questions. A federal court may have subject-matter authority over a federal-law claim yet still need authority over the defendant; conversely, authority over a defendant does not itself supply authority over every kind of claim. Other requirements may also matter, including service, venue, timing, and statutes. None of these concepts can determine where a real case belongs without current, jurisdiction-specific analysis.

For a classroom hypothetical, imagine a retailer based in State A and a supplier based in State B disagreeing about a delivery. It would be a mistake to say “different states means federal court” and stop. A careful student would separate possible federal subject-matter grounds from state-law claims, ask whether the selected court has authority over the parties, and recognize that venue and contract terms could matter. The student has identified research questions, not reached a legal conclusion or advised either business where to file.

### Precedent and the hierarchy of decisions

Precedent is a prior judicial decision used in deciding a later case. The practice of relying on past decisions is often called stare decisis. It supports continuity, but it does not turn every judicial opinion into a nationwide rule. The force of a decision depends on the court that issued it, the system in which the later court sits, the legal issue, and the scope of the earlier holding. Court rules can also distinguish precedential and nonprecedential decisions. Students should therefore avoid treating a search result that mentions a case as automatic controlling authority.

In the federal system, U.S. Supreme Court decisions on federal questions bind lower federal courts. A U.S. court of appeals decision generally binds federal district courts within that circuit on the matters the decision controls. A decision from another circuit may be considered persuasive rather than binding. Trial-court decisions commonly can be informative, but they do not establish binding circuit-wide precedent. These are broad instructional statements; nuances include en banc decisions, later Supreme Court rulings, statutory changes, and rules concerning unpublished or nonprecedential decisions.

State precedent works through each state's own judicial hierarchy. A state court of last resort's interpretation of its own state's law normally governs lower state courts. Federal courts deciding state-law questions often look to the relevant state's highest court. Labels can mislead: “supreme court” may mean the highest court in one jurisdiction, while in another state it may name a trial-level court. The source, court, date, publication status, and jurisdiction must all be checked before characterizing a decision's authority.

This hierarchy is not the law-source hierarchy. A court may interpret a constitution, statute, regulation, or prior decision, but this lesson does not rank those categories or teach a rule of statutory interpretation. Nor does it cover civil versus criminal burdens, parties, remedies, or procedures. Its practical habit is narrower: identify the court system, court level, and jurisdiction before saying what a case means. For a real business dispute, a qualified lawyer licensed or authorized in the relevant jurisdiction, or an appropriate official court or agency resource, can assess current law and facts.

## Key Vocabulary

- **dual court system:** The U.S. arrangement of a federal court system alongside separate state court systems.
- **trial court:** A court that generally develops the record and initially resolves factual and legal issues.
- **appellate court:** A court that reviews challenged decisions for legal error under rules governing appellate review.
- **subject-matter jurisdiction:** A court's authority to hear a particular category of claim or legal issue.
- **personal jurisdiction:** A court's authority to exercise power with respect to a particular party or entity.
- **precedent:** An earlier judicial decision that may guide or control a later court's decision.
- **binding authority:** Legal authority a court is required to follow within the relevant hierarchy and jurisdiction.
- **persuasive authority:** Material a court may consider but is not required to follow as controlling law.

## Eli-10

Think of U.S. courts as two nearby school systems: one run for the whole country and one run by each state. Each has classrooms at different levels. Before a court can help, it must be the kind of court allowed to handle that kind of problem and to make decisions about the people involved. The first courtroom usually gathers the information. A higher courtroom usually checks whether rules were used correctly instead of starting the whole story over.

Past court decisions are like instructions from a principal. An instruction from the top authority must be followed by the people below it. An instruction from a principal in another school may be useful, but it is not automatically an order for your school.

## Eli's Analogy

A court system is like a map with two transit networks and several levels of stations. You need the right network, the right station, and the right route before a train can take you where you need to go.

The analogy has limits. Courts are not transit lines with fixed routes. Their authority comes from constitutions, statutes, rules, and specific facts; different courts and cases can have exceptions and special procedures. The map helps organize questions but cannot choose a court or predict an outcome.

## Worked Example

A student finds a federal appellate opinion from the Ninth Circuit while researching a hypothetical contract conflict that would be heard in a state trial court in Georgia. The student should not call the Ninth Circuit opinion automatically binding. First, the student identifies the issuing court, the later court, and whether the issue is federal or state law. A U.S. Supreme Court decision on a federal question may control more broadly; a Ninth Circuit decision may be persuasive research material elsewhere; and Georgia state law may require checking decisions of Georgia's own highest court. The student has mapped authority without deciding the hypothetical case.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating all U.S. courts as one vertical ladder.** Start by distinguishing the federal system from the relevant state's system and then identify the court level.
- **Assuming an appeal is a complete new trial.** Appellate review generally examines challenged decisions and the existing record under applicable rules.
- **Using jurisdiction to mean only physical location.** Separate a court's authority over the type of case from its authority concerning the parties; other location-related concepts may also matter.
- **Calling any case found online binding precedent.** Check the issuing court, later court, legal question, geographic jurisdiction, and the decision's status.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trial court | Appellate court | A trial court generally develops the initial record; an appellate court reviews challenged decisions under appellate rules. |
| Subject-matter jurisdiction | Personal jurisdiction | The first concerns authority over the kind of case; the second concerns authority regarding the parties. |
| Binding authority | Persuasive authority | Binding authority must be followed by the relevant court; persuasive authority may inform reasoning without controlling it. |

## Key Takeaway

Court-system analysis begins with authority and hierarchy: identify the federal or state system, court level, and relevant jurisdiction before describing what a decision can decide or what precedent controls.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What does subject-matter jurisdiction concern?**
   - A. A court's authority over the kind of case or issue
   - B. The number of judges on a court
   - C. The geographic location of a courthouse
   - D. The amount of evidence in a record
   - **Answer: A.** Subject-matter jurisdiction asks whether a court is authorized to hear that category of matter.

2. **Which statement best describes the usual difference between trial and appellate courts?**
   - A. Trial courts only hear federal cases, while appellate courts only hear state cases.
   - B. Trial courts generally develop the initial record, while appellate courts review challenged decisions.
   - C. Appellate courts always hear new witnesses, while trial courts never receive evidence.
   - D. Trial courts create nationwide precedent, while appellate courts cannot interpret law.
   - **Answer: B.** Trial courts generally manage the initial fact record and decision; appellate courts review alleged error under governing rules.

3. **A student reads a decision from a federal court of appeals in a different circuit from the federal district court now considering a similar federal question. How should the student initially classify that decision?**
   - A. It automatically binds every federal district court nationwide.
   - B. It is irrelevant because appellate decisions never matter outside their circuit.
   - C. It may be persuasive, but its binding force depends on the relevant hierarchy and jurisdiction.
   - D. It replaces any applicable U.S. Supreme Court decision.
   - **Answer: C.** A decision from another circuit can be useful persuasive authority but is not automatically controlling.

4. **Which research sequence is most careful before describing a court decision as controlling in a business-law discussion?**
   - A. Read only the case headline and use the court's title as proof of authority.
   - B. Assume that any decision from a court called “supreme court” controls nationally.
   - C. Identify the parties' preferred forum first and then ignore the legal question.
   - D. Identify the issuing court, later court, legal question, jurisdiction, and decision status.
   - **Answer: D.** All of those features affect whether a decision is binding or only persuasive.

5. **A claim arises under federal law, but a court lacks authority over the defendant. What is the most accurate high-level conclusion?**
   - A. The federal-law claim automatically resolves every jurisdictional issue.
   - B. Authority over the kind of claim and authority regarding the parties are distinct questions, so further analysis is needed.
   - C. Personal jurisdiction matters only after a final appeal.
   - D. The court must have authority because the parties conduct business in different states.
   - **Answer: B.** Subject-matter and personal jurisdiction are distinct concepts.
