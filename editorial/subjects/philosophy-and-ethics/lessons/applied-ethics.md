# Applied Ethics

## In 30 Seconds

Applied ethics is the branch of ethics that examines moral questions as they arise in concrete practices and cases: in medicine, business, technology, the environment, and the professions. It draws on normative theories such as utilitarianism, deontology, and virtue ethics as resources, while particular cases can in turn test and refine those theories. Careful applied analysis states the moral question, checks the relevant facts, names the moral considerations, weighs them, and considers alternatives.

## Why This Matters

Moral questions rarely arrive in the abstract; they arrive embedded in real practices, institutions, and constraints. Applied ethics gives students a disciplined way to analyze such questions without reducing them to personal opinion or expecting a single theory to produce an automatic answer. It matters academically because it connects normative theory to concrete cases, practically because professionals, policymakers, and citizens face these questions regularly, and educationally because it builds the habit of checking facts, stating reasons, and revising conclusions honestly when new information appears.

## Learning Objectives

- Define applied ethics as the branch of ethics that examines moral questions in concrete practices and cases.
- Distinguish applied ethics from normative ethics and metaethics, and explain how theories and cases inform each other.
- Explain reflective equilibrium as a method of adjusting considered judgments and general principles until they cohere, attributing it to John Rawls.
- Apply a structured analysis (state the moral question, gather facts, identify considerations, weigh them, consider alternatives) to an ordinary case.
- Identify the main domains of applied ethics: bioethics, business ethics, environmental ethics, technology ethics, and professional ethics.
- Analyze the limits of applied ethics, including persistent disagreement, the role of facts, and the difference between domain expertise and moral authority.

## The College Version

### From general theory to particular cases

Applied ethics is the branch of ethics that takes up moral questions as they arise within particular practices, institutions, and cases. Where normative ethics asks what general standards should guide conduct, applied ethics asks whether specific actions and practices are morally permissible: whether a particular policy is fair, whether a particular practice is justified, whether a particular decision is right. The distinction is a matter of focus rather than a wall. A question about which principle should govern all policies is normative; a question about this policy, with these facts and these people affected, is applied. Metaethics, the third branch, asks a different kind of question about the meaning and status of moral claims themselves. Applied ethics usually proceeds on the assumption that questions of right and wrong are worth asking, and it examines them where they actually occur.

### Theories as resources, cases as tests

Normative theories supply resources for applied analysis. Consequentialist theories evaluate actions by their outcomes, deontological theories by duties and rights, and virtue-based theories by character and the ideals of a good life; applied ethics consults these approaches across nearly all of its domains. Using a theory is not mechanical. Philosophers debate whether applied questions are settled by deducing an answer from a theory or whether practical reasoning works differently, and many applied arguments draw on several theories at once. The traffic also runs the other way: cases test theories. A carefully constructed case can function as a counterexample to a general view, as the familiar transplant problem does for simple consequentialism. Reflective equilibrium makes this two-way traffic explicit. John Rawls proposed the method in A Theory of Justice (1971): the reasoner treats considered judgments — judgments made under conditions suitable for careful moral deliberation — and candidate general principles as materials to be brought into agreement, revising judgments in light of strong principles and principles in light of firm judgments. Applied and normative ethics thus inform each other rather than running in one direction only.

### A method for analyzing a case

A practical method for a case proceeds through five stages. First, state the moral question precisely: what action, policy, or practice is being evaluated, and by whom. Second, gather the relevant facts. Applied ethics requires considerable empirical knowledge of the practice in question; a conclusion built on a mistaken fact collapses. Third, name the moral considerations — the rights, duties, consequences, values, and commitments that could reasonably bear on the case. Fourth, weigh them honestly, including conflicting considerations rather than hiding them. Fifth, consider alternatives: rarely are only two options available, and institutional or practical alternatives often change the analysis. Facts do not by themselves generate an "ought" conclusion; a moral claim needs a stated reason that connects the facts to a standard. This method makes the reasoning visible and testable. It does not guarantee agreement, but it turns an exchange of conclusions into an exchange of reasons.

### Domains of applied ethics

Applied ethics is organized largely by practice. Bioethics examines moral questions in medicine, health care, and biological research. Business ethics examines commerce, corporate conduct, and the responsibilities of firms to stakeholders. Environmental ethics examines obligations regarding the natural world, non-human animals, and future generations. Technology ethics — including computer and information ethics — examines privacy, algorithmic decisions, and the design and use of digital systems. Professional ethics examines the duties and standards of professions such as law, medicine, and engineering, including the use of codes of conduct. Each domain has its own literature and its own specialists; an expert in one may have little to say in another. Several of these domains are treated in depth in their own lessons.

### Limits: disagreement, facts, and authority

Applied ethics does not promise unanimous answers. Disagreement persists among careful reasoners, and it can stem from different facts, different principles, or different weights assigned to genuine considerations; locating the source of disagreement is itself part of the analysis. Facts matter: empirical knowledge of a practice is part of what makes applied analysis serious. Domain expertise matters too, but it is not moral authority. A physician knows medicine, an engineer knows structures, and a business leader knows markets; none of that knowledge by itself settles the moral question, which requires reasons that others can examine. Finally, this lesson is general education, not individualized advice. Real high-stakes decisions involve specialized evidence, institutional context, and perspectives absent from a classroom scenario, and responsible analysis in such settings identifies what further information and expertise are needed.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Applied ethics:** The branch of ethics that examines the moral permissibility of specific actions and practices within particular domains.
- **Normative ethics:** The branch of ethics that develops and evaluates general principles or standards for moral conduct.
- **Metaethics:** The branch of ethics that investigates the meaning, nature, and status of moral claims and values.
- **Reflective equilibrium:** A method in which a reasoner adjusts considered moral judgments and general principles back and forth until they agree; associated with John Rawls.
- **Considered judgment:** A moral judgment made under conditions suitable for careful moral deliberation, treated as a starting point for reflective equilibrium.
- **Moral consideration:** A feature of a case offered as relevant to its moral evaluation, such as a right, duty, consequence, or value.
- **Bioethics:** The domain of applied ethics concerned with moral questions in medicine, health care, and biological research.
- **Business ethics:** The domain of applied ethics concerned with moral questions in commerce and corporate conduct.
- **Environmental ethics:** The domain of applied ethics concerned with moral questions about the natural environment and non-human life.
- **Professional ethics:** The domain of applied ethics concerned with the moral duties and standards of professions such as law, medicine, and engineering.

## Eli-10

Applied ethics is what happens when ethics meets a real situation: a hospital, a company, a new app, a polluted river. It does not start from scratch. It borrows ideas from the big moral theories — consequences, duties, character — and asks which ones help with this particular problem. And the situation talks back: if a rule leads somewhere clearly wrong, the rule may need to change. Applied ethics is a two-way street between principles and cases, with the goal of reaching a judgment that holds together after checking facts, hearing the people affected, and weighing the reasons.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of applied ethics as test-driving a car. The theories are like the car's features: steering, brakes, engine. You do not choose one feature and ignore the rest; you use what fits the road. The case is the road itself. A sharp turn can reveal that the steering needs adjustment, just as a hard case can reveal that a principle needs refinement. The test drive ends when the car handles the route — when principles and judgments move together smoothly.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** A car comes with a manual that gives one correct setting for every condition, and a test drive has a definite end. Moral cases do not. People can disagree about which feature matters most, the facts can stay uncertain, and there may be no setting that satisfies everyone. The analogy captures the back-and-forth between theory and case, but not a guarantee of a single right answer.

## Worked Example

A campus food pantry run by students is offered a steady supply of day-old bread by a grocery chain — on the condition that the pantry stops posting its occasional public statements criticizing the chain's labor practices. The moral question is whether accepting the bread under that condition is right. The facts matter: how much food the pantry currently has, whether other donors exist, what the statements actually said, and who depends on the pantry. The considerations include feeding people in need, keeping the arrangement honest, and whether staying silent would mislead anyone about the chain's practices. The students weigh these, then consider alternatives: asking for the food without the condition, finding other donors, or accepting and issuing a separate, clearly labeled statement. The group may not reach one obvious answer, but the question, facts, considerations, and options are now visible for discussion.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating applied ethics as choosing one favorite theory and mechanically deducing the answer:** Theories are resources, not vending machines; cases can push back against a principle, and careful analysis often draws on more than one theory.
- **Skipping the facts:** Applied analysis requires reliable empirical knowledge of the practice; a moral conclusion resting on a wrong fact is unsupported.
- **Confusing domain expertise with moral authority:** Knowing a practice deeply informs the analysis, but the moral question is settled by reasons others can examine, not by a practitioner's standing alone.
- **Expecting applied ethics to eliminate disagreement:** Disagreement can persist over facts, principles, or how considerations are weighed; the value of the method is clearer and more honest reasoning, not guaranteed consensus.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Applied ethics | Normative ethics | Examines whether specific actions and practices are morally permissible versus develops and evaluates general principles of conduct. |
| Applied ethics | Metaethics | Asks what should be done in a concrete practice versus asks about the meaning, nature, and status of moral claims. |
| Deductive application of theory to a case | Reflective equilibrium | Moves one way, from principle to verdict, versus moves both ways, adjusting judgments and principles until they agree. |

## Key Takeaway

Applied ethics examines moral questions in concrete practices, drawing on normative theories while letting cases test them; its disciplined method — question, facts, considerations, alternatives — makes disagreement clearer without promising automatic answers.
