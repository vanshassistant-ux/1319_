# Deontology

## In 30 Seconds

Duty-based ethics, or deontology, judges actions by whether they follow moral rules and obligations, not primarily by their results. In Kant's version, the only thing good without qualification is a good will, and an action has moral worth only when done from duty. The categorical imperative tests our rules: act only on maxims you could will as universal law, and treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means. Defenders cite rights protection and consistency; critics raise conflicting duties and rigidity.

## Why This Matters

Deontology is one of the main alternatives to thinking of ethics as a calculation of outcomes. Its insistence that persons have value that results cannot override runs through modern discussions of rights, dignity, and consent. Learning it helps you recognize when a disagreement turns on whether a rule or a consequence should decide a case, and why promises, honesty, and respect can bind even when breaking them would produce better results. It also prepares you to evaluate the objections—conflicting duties, rigidity, rule worship—that shape later debates in applied ethics.

## Learning Objectives

- Define deontology as a duty-based approach to ethics and contrast it with consequence-based reasoning.
- Explain Kant's claim that only a good will is good without qualification and that moral worth requires acting from duty.
- State the universalizability and humanity formulations of the categorical imperative and distinguish them from hypothetical imperatives.
- Apply the universalizability test to an ordinary hypothetical case.
- Analyze standard objections to deontology—conflicting duties, rigorism, and rule worship—as objections rather than settled verdicts.
- Evaluate the contrast between deontology and consequentialist views such as utilitarianism.

## The College Version

### Duty, not outcomes: the basic claim

Deontology comes from the Greek *deon*, meaning duty, and *logos*, meaning study: it is literally the study of duty. A deontological theory evaluates the rightness of an action by whether it conforms to moral rules or obligations, not primarily by what it brings about. The right action is the one that follows the duty the agent has. That is the sharpest contrast with consequentialist theories such as utilitarianism, which judge actions by their outcomes: an action is right when it produces the greatest good for everyone affected. Deontology reverses that priority: what makes an action right is not the goodness of its results. Later deontologists such as W. D. Ross argued that consequences remain morally relevant when considered in light of our duties, but even then, duty rather than outcome is the organizing idea.

### Kant: good will, duty, and the categorical imperative

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) gave deontology its best-known form in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785). His argument begins with commonsense morality: the only thing good without qualification is a good will—a will committed to doing what morality requires. Other good things, such as courage, cleverness, or happiness, are good only under conditions; courage can serve injustice and wit can serve cruelty. A good will is good in itself, not because of what it achieves; Kant writes that it would still shine like a jewel even if it were completely powerless to carry out its aims. From this follows a demanding claim about motivation: an action has moral worth only when it is done from duty, not merely in conformity with duty. A shopkeeper who charges fair prices because honesty is good for business, or a person who helps others because it feels good, acts in conformity with duty but not from duty. This claim is controversial: critics ask whether actions from sympathy or love really lack moral worth, and Kant's defenders reply that his point concerns which consideration is decisive in deliberation, not whether warm feelings are welcome.

Kant divides commands into two kinds. Hypothetical imperatives tell us what to do given an end we have chosen: if you want good grades, study. The categorical imperative commands unconditionally, regardless of what we want; it is the supreme principle of morality. Kant offers several formulations, of which two are most studied. The universal law formulation: "Act only according to that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law." A maxim is the personal rule an agent acts on; the test asks whether you could consistently will everyone to act on it. The humanity formulation: "So act that you use humanity, in your own person as well as in the person of any other, always at the same time as an end, never merely as a means." It forbids treating people as mere instruments—using a person without regard for their own rational ends—while permitting cooperation that respects others, such as paying an agreed price to a driver. Kant used these formulations to derive duties in four categories: perfect duties, such as not making lying promises, admit no exception in favor of inclination; imperfect duties, such as helping others, require action sometimes and to some extent.

### Why consequences are not the primary criterion

If the worth of the will does not depend on what it brings about, and if the moral law is unconditional, then a dutiful action is required even when its results are unfavorable, and forbidden actions remain forbidden even when they would pay. Deontologists need not deny that consequences matter; they deny that consequences alone determine rightness. Two strengths are commonly claimed for this approach. First, rights protection: the humanity formulation gives every rational being equal worth and dignity, so persons cannot be sacrificed merely because harming them would benefit others. Second, consistency: because the universal law formulation applies to everyone, no one may quietly make an exception of herself—the same rule that binds others binds the agent.

### Objections, presented as objections

Critics raise several standard objections. *Conflicting duties*: two duties can apply at once, as when telling the truth would help a stranger who intends harm. Critics argue that rule-based ethics lacks a clear procedure for such cases; Ross's pluralist deontology responds by treating duties as prima facie—binding at first sight—and weighing them to identify the actual duty in the situation. *Rigorism*: if moral rules admit no exceptions, the theory can seem rigid; Ross charged that Kant was wrong to treat moral rules as absolute and exceptionless. *Rule worship*: some critics argue that making obedience central can become devotion to rules for their own sake; defenders reply that Kant grounds rules in rational willing, not in rules as brute facts. These are objections in an ongoing debate, not settled verdicts: whether the categorical imperative handles hard cases, and whether its strictness is a flaw, remain contested questions.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Deontology:** A duty-based approach to ethics that evaluates actions by their conformity to moral rules or obligations rather than primarily by their consequences.
- **Duty:** What morality requires of an agent, regardless of the agent's personal inclination.
- **Good will:** For Kant, a will committed to doing what morality requires; the only thing good without qualification.
- **Inclination:** A desire, interest, or tendency that can move us to act independently of moral reasons.
- **Maxim:** The personal rule or principle on which an agent acts in a given situation.
- **Categorical imperative:** Kant's supreme principle of morality: an unconditional command that applies to all rational agents regardless of their ends.
- **Hypothetical imperative:** A command that applies conditionally, only given an end the agent has chosen.
- **Universal law formulation:** The version of the categorical imperative that tests whether one can will one's maxim as a universal law.
- **Humanity formulation:** The version of the categorical imperative requiring that humanity, in oneself and others, always be treated as an end and never merely as a means.
- **Prima facie duty:** In Ross's ethics, a duty that holds at first sight and can be outweighed by a weightier duty in a particular situation.

## Eli-10

Deontology is the idea that some actions are right or wrong because of the rules behind them, not because of what happens afterward. Think of a promise: on this view, breaking a promise is wrong even if breaking it would work out fine, because a promise is a duty you took on. Kant added two tests. First, could everyone follow your rule? If your rule only works because almost nobody follows it, it fails. Second, are you treating people as ends, not as tools? If you are using someone merely to get what you want, that fails too. On this view, what matters is whether your action honors the duty and the rule, not whether it turns out well.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a referee at a game. The referee calls penalties by the rulebook, not by which team would benefit from ignoring the foul. Even if letting a foul slide would make the game more exciting, the referee's job is the rules. Deontology treats morality like that: the rule—not the scoreboard of consequences—decides the call. Kant's extra step is asking whether the rule is one everyone could play by, and whether the players are treated as people rather than as equipment.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Morality has no printed rulebook, and unlike a referee, agents must decide which rules exist and how to apply them when two rules collide. The metaphor also suggests mechanical obedience, which is exactly the rigidity critics object to. It captures the spirit of deontology—rules bind regardless of results—without settling which rules are correct or how conflicts should be resolved.

## Worked Example

Jordan borrows a classmate's course notes, promising to return them the next morning, but later decides to keep them through the weekend to finish an essay. The maxim is "keep borrowed notes past the agreed return time whenever it is convenient." Universalized—everyone keeping borrowed things past agreed deadlines—the practice of lending notes would collapse, since no one would lend what they could not rely on getting back. The humanity formulation gives the same answer: keeping the notes treats the classmate as a mere means to Jordan's convenience, overriding her standing as someone entitled to her property and to the promise made. Acting from duty, Jordan returns the notes on time even though keeping them is more convenient.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming deontology ignores consequences entirely.** Deontologists deny that consequences are the primary or sole criterion of rightness; many, like Ross, treat consequences as morally relevant in light of duties.
- **Treating any rule-following as deontological.** Prudential rules such as "study hard to get good grades" are hypothetical imperatives conditional on your ends; deontological duties bind regardless of inclination.
- **Reading the humanity formulation as forbidding any use of other people.** It forbids treating people merely as a means; cooperating with someone who can rationally consent, such as paying an agreed price, is permitted.
- **Concluding that moral worth requires being cold or emotionless.** Kant's claim is that duty must be the decisive motive; defenders argue this does not require suppressing feelings, and the interpretation is debated.
- **Treating the rigorism objection as an established fact about the theory.** Whether moral rules admit exceptions is contested; critics press the objection, and defenders reply that Kant grounds rules in rational willing.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Deontology | Consequentialism (e.g., utilitarianism) | Judges the rightness of actions by conformity to duty or rules versus by the goodness of their outcomes. |
| Action from duty | Action from inclination | Motivated by what morality requires versus by desire, interest, or sympathy; for Kant, only the former has moral worth. |
| Universal law formulation | Humanity formulation | Tests whether one can will one's maxim as a universal law versus requires treating persons always as ends, never merely as means. |

## Key Takeaway

Deontology judges actions by duty and rules rather than by results: moral worth requires acting from duty, and the categorical imperative tests whether our rules could be universal and whether persons are treated as ends.

2. **According to Kant, when does an action have genuine moral worth?** When it is done from duty, not merely in conformity with it. **Answer: B.**
