# Equality

## In 30 Seconds

Equality is a contested ideal. People agree it matters but disagree about what it requires: equal treatment under the rules, equal opportunities, or equal outcomes. Philosophers distinguish formal equality from substantive equality, and formal from fair equality of opportunity. They also ask what should be equalized, whether resources, welfare, or capabilities, as Amartya Sen urged. Egalitarian, sufficiency, and priority views offer rival answers, and the leveling-down objection challenges equality of outcome. Even whether equal treatment means identical treatment remains disputed.

## Why This Matters

Equality claims shape scholarship decisions, hiring, access to buildings and programs, and public policy. When people say a rule is unfair because it is unequal, they usually have one of these conceptions in mind, and different conceptions support different conclusions. Learning to separate formal equality, equality of opportunity, and equality of outcome, and to see why the question of what should be equalized matters, turns a rhetorical slogan into a precise disagreement. The same tools carry into later study of justice, rights, and applied ethics, and into evaluating any policy that distributes opportunities or resources.

## Learning Objectives

- Define equality as a contested concept in social and political philosophy.
- Distinguish formal equality from substantive equality.
- Distinguish formal equality of opportunity, fair equality of opportunity, and equality of outcome.
- Explain the capabilities approach to the question of what should be equalized and attribute it to Amartya Sen.
- Contrast egalitarian, sufficiency, and priority views, and explain the leveling-down objection to equality of outcome.
- Analyze whether equal treatment requires identical treatment, using feminist and disability critiques.

## The College Version

### Equality is a contested concept

In ordinary talk, equality sounds straightforward: treat everyone the same. In philosophy, it is among the most contested ideals of social and political thought. As Ronald Dworkin observed, people who praise or disparage equality disagree about what they are praising or disparaging. Equality is not identity: equality names a correspondence between different persons with respect to some feature, while identity means being one and the same thing. A claim that two students are equal is incomplete until it says equal in what respect: before the law, in resources, or in opportunity. Much of the philosophical work on equality is making that respect explicit.

### Formal and substantive equality

The formal principle of equality, often traced to Aristotle, says to treat like cases as like. Students alike in the respects that matter for a grade should receive the same treatment; those who differ in those respects may not. Philosophers disagree about its grounds. Some treat it as a rule of rationality: it is inconsistent to treat equal cases unequally without sufficient reason. Others treat it as a moral principle requiring that unequal treatment be justifiable to those affected. Either way, the formal principle does not answer the hard question of which respects are relevant. Substantive equality goes further. Instead of asking only whether rules are applied consistently, it asks whether people end up with genuinely equal conditions, opportunities, or results. A rule applied identically to everyone can still leave some people unable to participate, and substantive views treat that as an equality problem.

### Equality of opportunity and equality of outcome

Equality of opportunity is a family of views. Formal equality of opportunity requires that positions be open to all, with no legal or coercive barriers, and that selection run as open, merit-based competition. It forbids allocating positions by gender, ethnicity, or similar traits but does not ask why some applicants were better prepared. Fair equality of opportunity, formulated by John Rawls, adds a requirement: people with the same talent and ability and the same willingness to use them should have the same prospects of success regardless of social class of origin. If children in one neighborhood receive far weaker schooling, fair opportunity fails even though applications are open to everyone. Substantive equality of opportunity goes further, addressing both the procedures of competition and the preparation of candidates. Equality of outcome is the more demanding rival: it assesses equality by the results people actually reach. Most egalitarians do not advocate equality of outcome; they defend some form of equality of opportunity, partly because they take individual responsibility seriously.

### Equality of what? Resources, welfare, capabilities

Whatever equality is sought, there remains the question of its metric. Equality of welfare aims at equal well-being but faces the expensive-tastes problem: people with costly preferences would consume a disproportionate share of resources. Equality of resources, associated with Ronald Dworkin, aims at equal holdings of income, wealth, and other resources, but the same resources do different work in different lives. Amartya Sen pioneered the capabilities approach to address this gap. Sen argues that what matters is not the resources people hold but their real freedoms to achieve functionings, the doings and beings that make up a life. In his 'Equality of What?' example, two people with identical resources are not equal if one must spend much of them on a wheelchair just to get around. Because people convert resources into functionings at different rates, equal resources do not guarantee equal capabilities. Martha Nussbaum and others have since developed it in their own directions.

### Egalitarianism, sufficiency, and priority

Theories of distribution also differ over which value should guide us. Egalitarianism holds that equality itself has significant value: other things equal, a more equal distribution is better. The sufficiency view, associated with Harry Frankfurt, holds that what matters is not that everyone has the same but that each has enough; once everyone has enough, remaining differences matter less. The priority view, associated with Derek Parfit, holds that benefiting people matters more the worse off they are, even apart from any comparison. Prioritarians and egalitarians may favor similar policies but disagree about why: priority cares about absolute position, while equality cares about relation to others. The views face different objections, and the choice between them remains a live dispute.

### The leveling-down objection, and equality and difference

One influential objection targets equality of outcome directly. Parfit asks us to imagine a policy that makes everyone equal by making the better-off worse off, without benefiting anyone; intuitively, this seems objectionable, since nobody is helped and somebody is harmed. The objection suggests that what really moves people is not equality but priority, giving extra weight to the worse off. Egalitarians reply that equality can be intrinsically valuable, so a more equal distribution is better in one respect even when it helps no one; some add that for positional goods, such as a vote, leveling down may be appropriate. A separate debate asks whether equal treatment requires identical treatment. Feminist critics of purely procedural equality observe that 'like cases alike' leaves open which respects are relevant. Difference feminists such as Martha Minow argue that equality can require attending to physical, historical, and social differences, and describe dilemmas of difference: taking a difference into account can seem required for equality yet appear to single people out. Disability-focused critiques make a similar point: a rule requiring everyone to climb the same stairs treats everyone identically while excluding a wheelchair user; identical resources can fail people whose conversion of resources into functionings differs. These are contested positions, presented as the debate, not a settled verdict.


## Key Vocabulary

- **Formal equality:** The principle that people who are alike in normatively relevant respects should be treated alike, often traced to Aristotle's "treat like cases as like."
- **Substantive equality:** An approach holding that equality requires attention to actual conditions and outcomes, not only to consistent application of rules.
- **Equality of opportunity:** The ideal that positions and advantages should be open to all, ranging from formal open competition to fair opportunity that equalizes background conditions.
- **Fair equality of opportunity:** John Rawls's requirement that people with the same talent and willingness to use it have the same prospects of success regardless of social class of origin.
- **Equality of outcome:** An approach that assesses equality by the results people actually reach, such as income, welfare, or capabilities, rather than by access to competition.
- **Capabilities approach:** Amartya Sen's framework for evaluating well-being by people's real freedoms to achieve doings and beings, rather than by the resources they hold.
- **Egalitarianism:** A family of views holding that equality has significant value and that distributions should be evaluated by how equal they are.
- **Sufficiency view:** The position, associated with Harry Frankfurt, that what matters morally is that each person has enough, not that all have the same.
- **Priority view:** The position, associated with Derek Parfit, that benefiting people matters more the worse off those people are, even apart from equality.
- **Leveling-down objection:** The objection that a policy which reduces inequality by making the better-off worse off without benefiting anyone is morally objectionable.

## Eli-10

Equality sounds simple: treat everyone the same. But philosophers quickly notice that "the same" is slippery. The same rule can hurt people who are differently situated, and the same resources can do different amounts of work in different lives. So equality debates are really about which comparisons matter: rules, opportunities, outcomes, or what people can actually do and be. Different answers lead to different policies, and reasonable people disagree about which answer is right. The goal of this lesson is not to settle that disagreement but to make it precise.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a school giving every student the exact same pair of shoes, in the same size. That is equal treatment in one sense: everyone gets the same thing. But it is not equal in the sense that matters to the students, because the shoes fit some, pinch others, and fit almost no one well.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Shoes are a simple good with an obvious fit test, so the right response seems clear: match the shoe to the foot. In real equality debates there is no obvious test. People disagree about which differences are relevant, about how much responsibility we bear for our choices, and about whether equality or something else, like sufficiency, is the value that should guide us.

## Worked Example

A community theater sells tickets to a play. Everyone pays the same price and follows the same rules, so formal equality is satisfied. But the theater's only entrance has a flight of stairs, so a patron who uses a wheelchair cannot get in. The ticket policy treats everyone alike, yet it does not give everyone the same real opportunity to attend. Now imagine two applicants for the theater's internship: both may apply, but only one could afford a university theater course that the selection panel rewards. Formal equality of opportunity holds, because the application is open and selection is merit-based. Fair equality of opportunity arguably does not, because social background shaped the applicants' prospects. Which inequalities the theater should address, and with which metric in view, is exactly what the competing conceptions dispute.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming equality means identical treatment in every respect:** Formal equality requires treating like cases alike in relevant respects; it does not require sameness where people are relevantly different.
- **Conflating equality of opportunity with equality of outcome:** Equality of opportunity concerns access and fair prospects in competition; equality of outcome concerns the results people actually reach. Most egalitarians defend forms of the former.
- **Treating formal equality as automatically sufficient for substantive equality:** A rule applied consistently can still exclude people who are differently situated; substantive views ask about background conditions and actual results.
- **Thinking the leveling-down objection refutes every egalitarian view:** The objection is a serious challenge, but egalitarians reply that equality can be intrinsically valuable or grounded in other values; the lesson presents the debate rather than a verdict.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formal equality | Substantive equality | Formal equality requires consistent treatment of like cases under the rules; substantive equality looks beyond rules to conditions, opportunities, and results. |
| Formal equality of opportunity | Fair equality of opportunity | Formal opportunity requires open, merit-based competition; fair opportunity (Rawls) also requires that social class not shape the prospects of people with equal talent and effort. |
| Sufficiency view | Priority view | Sufficiency (Frankfurt) cares that each person has enough; priority (Parfit) cares that benefits matter more the worse off people are. |

## Key Takeaway

Equality is a contested ideal, not a single formula. Careful thinking separates formal from substantive equality, opportunity from outcome, and asks which metric, whether resources, welfare, or capabilities, should be equalized.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **The formal principle of equality, often traced to Aristotle, is usually stated as which of the following?** Treat like cases alike, and different cases differently in the relevant respects. **Answer: B.**
2. **A city announces that a training program is open to all residents and selects participants by a written test. In practice, only applicants who could afford private test-preparation courses pass. Which statement best describes this situation?** Formal equality of opportunity is satisfied, but fair or substantive equality of opportunity may not be. **Answer: A.**
3. **Two students receive identical scholarships. One student has a disability and must spend a large share of the money on equipment just to get around. Which claim best captures Amartya Sen's point in his "Equality of What?" argument?** Equal resources can still yield unequal capabilities, because people differ in converting resources into doings and beings. **Answer: C.**
4. **The leveling-down objection targets policies that would do which of the following?** Reduce inequality by making the better-off worse off without benefiting anyone. **Answer: D.**
5. **A community center requires all members to reach the pool by stairs. The rule applies to everyone, but a member who uses a wheelchair cannot enter at all. Which of the following best captures the philosophical critique of this arrangement?** Equal treatment may not require identical treatment: rules that ignore relevant differences can exclude people who are differently situated. **Answer: B.**
