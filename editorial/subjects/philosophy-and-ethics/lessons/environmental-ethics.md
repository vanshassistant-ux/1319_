# Environmental Ethics

## In 30 Seconds

Environmental ethics is the philosophical study of human obligations regarding the natural world. It asks whether nature matters only because it serves human ends, or also for its own sake, and who counts morally: people alone, individual organisms, or entire ecosystems. Landmark views — Leopold's land ethic, Carson's warnings about pesticides, Naess's deep ecology — frame these questions. The field also asks what we owe future generations, and how the burdens of environmental change should be shared.

## Why This Matters

Nearly every environmental decision — a wetland saved or drained, an energy source chosen, a resource used or conserved — carries a moral question beneath the factual one. Environmental ethics supplies the tools to say what that question is and to compare the reasons people give for different answers. It matters academically as one of the liveliest areas of applied philosophy, practically because citizens, professionals, and policymakers face these choices constantly, and educationally because it trains students to keep values, facts, and interests distinct instead of letting them blur together.

## Learning Objectives

- Define environmental ethics as the philosophical study of human obligations regarding the natural world.
- Distinguish anthropocentrism, biocentrism, and ecocentrism, and attribute landmark statements to their authors (Leopold's land ethic, Naess's deep ecology).
- Explain the distinction between instrumental and intrinsic value and apply it to cases involving nature.
- Describe Silent Spring's documented role in raising public concern about pesticides and its place in the policy history leading to the 1972 DDT cancellation order.
- Explain the Brundtland Report's definition of sustainable development and identify intergenerational justice as a contested area.
- Distinguish ethical questions about climate responsibility and burden-sharing from climate science questions.

## The College Version

### What environmental ethics studies

Environmental ethics is the discipline in philosophy that studies the moral relationship of human beings to the environment, and the value and moral status of the environment and its non-human contents. Although thinkers had reflected on nature and human duty for centuries, environmental ethics emerged as a distinct academic discipline in the 1970s, after a decade of growing public awareness of pollution, resource use, and population growth. The field asks two questions that are easy to run together: what duties do human beings have with respect to the natural world, and why do we have them? The second question usually has to be answered first, because different answers generate different ethics. If the reason to protect a forest is that people need it, the resulting obligations look different from the obligations that follow if the forest matters for its own sake.

### Value and moral standing: three families of views

A central distinction in environmental ethics is between instrumental and intrinsic value. Instrumental value is the value something has as a means to further ends; intrinsic value is the value something has as an end in itself, regardless of its usefulness. A wetland has instrumental value if it filters water that people drink; whether it also has value in its own right is what separates the major positions. Anthropocentrism — human-centeredness — is the traditional view in much Western philosophy: moral standing belongs to human beings, and nature is valuable chiefly as a means to human ends. William Baxter's *People or Penguins* (1974) defends an unapologetically anthropocentric environmental ethic: pollution matters because of how it affects people. Biocentrism extends moral standing to all individual living organisms. Paul Taylor argued that every living thing is a teleological center of life with a good of its own that deserves moral respect, and that all such individuals have equal intrinsic value. Ecocentrism extends moral standing to holistic entities such as rivers, species, and ecosystems — the direction classically associated with Aldo Leopold's land ethic, which treats the biotic community itself as the object of ethical concern. These families are contested, and philosophers within each disagree about details; the map matters because it shows what is at stake: who counts, and why.

### Landmark figures and texts

Aldo Leopold, a forester and ecologist, argued in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949) that ethics should be extended to the land. Its central statement: "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." Leopold himself offered no systematic ethical theory to support the land ethic, which later philosophers treated as a challenge to be met. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) drew wide attention to the dangers of the commercial pesticides then in common use, such as DDT. The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency records that its publication stimulated widespread public concern over improper pesticide use and the need for better controls, and in 1972 the agency issued a cancellation order for DDT based on its adverse environmental effects. Carson's book did not act alone — regulators had already begun acting in the 1950s and 1960s — but it is the best-documented catalyst of public concern. Arne Naess, a Norwegian philosopher, coined the term "deep ecology" in 1973, contrasting it with what he called the shallow ecology movement, focused on pollution and resource depletion for human benefit. Deep ecology endorses biospheric egalitarianism: all living things have value in their own right, independent of their usefulness to others, and humans should understand themselves as parts of, not separate from, the natural world.

### Sustainability, intergenerational justice, and climate ethics

The Brundtland Report, *Our Common Future* (1987), defined sustainable development as development "that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." The definition raises questions it does not settle. Is it acceptable to replace natural resources with human-made substitutes? Weak sustainability says yes, provided the substitutes function equivalently; strong sustainability says no — a critical stock of natural systems must be preserved. And whether present generations genuinely owe duties to people who do not yet exist is itself contested; philosophers debate problems such as the non-identity problem, which asks how actions that determine who will exist can be said to harm those future people. Climate change raises the same family of questions. Climate ethics asks who is responsible for greenhouse gas emissions and how the costs of mitigation and adaptation should be shared — between countries, industries, and generations. Because emissions come from countless dispersed agents and no single global institution coordinates them, philosophers disagree about how responsibility should be assigned and burdens divided. Those are ethical debates; the scientific facts about climate change belong to the earth and space sciences. Environmental ethics supplies the question "what do we owe, and to whom," not the measurements.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Environmental ethics:** The branch of philosophy that studies human beings' moral obligations regarding the natural world and the moral standing of its non-human contents.
- **Anthropocentrism:** Human-centeredness: the ethical view that only human beings have moral standing in their own right, so nature matters mainly as a means to human ends.
- **Biocentrism:** The view that all individual living organisms have moral standing and value in their own right, as in Paul Taylor's teleological-center-of-life account.
- **Ecocentrism:** The view that ecological wholes — species, ecosystems, rivers, the biotic community — have moral standing, as in Leopold's land ethic.
- **Instrumental value:** The value something has as a means to further ends, such as a forest's timber or a wetland's water filtration.
- **Intrinsic value:** The value something has as an end in itself, independent of its usefulness to anyone.
- **Land ethic:** Aldo Leopold's proposal, in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), to extend ethics to the land and the biotic community.
- **Deep ecology:** The position associated with Arne Naess that all living things have value in their own right and that humans are parts of, not separate from, the natural world.
- **Sustainable development:** Development that meets present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs; the Brundtland Report's definition.
- **Intergenerational justice:** Fairness in the distribution of benefits and burdens across generations, including contested duties owed by present to future people.

## Eli-10

Environmental ethics is about one question: what do we owe the natural world, and why? Some people answer "because nature helps us" — that is anthropocentric thinking. Others answer "because living things matter on their own" — biocentrism. Still others answer "because whole systems, like forests, rivers, and species, matter" — ecocentrism. None of these is just a feeling; each is a reasoned position with famous defenders. The field also asks about time: do we owe anything to people who will live after us? And it asks about sharing: when the environment changes, who should carry the costs? You do not need to pick a side to study it. You need to be able to say what each side claims and what would count as evidence for or against it.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a family deciding what to do with a large old tree in the yard. One person values it for the shade it gives the house — that is instrumental value. Another values it because it is a living thing with a life of its own — biocentric reasoning. A third values it as part of the neighborhood's network of trees and wildlife — ecocentric reasoning. The same tree, three different accounts of why it matters. Environmental ethics is that family conversation, slowed down, with the reasons made visible and compared.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The family analogy can make the views sound like tastes, as if any answer is fine. In philosophy, these positions are argued for and against with reasons, evidence, and counterexamples, and they can conflict in ways a family conversation need not resolve. Also, a yard tree is present and visible, while much environmental ethics concerns distant people and large systems, which the analogy hides.

## Worked Example

A small town must decide whether to allow a construction company to drain a wetland to build a parking lot near a shopping center. The main views frame the decision differently. An anthropocentric analysis asks what the wetland does for people — flood protection, clean water, recreation — and whether the parking lot's benefits outweigh those. A biocentric analysis asks whether the plants and animals living there have goods of their own that draining would destroy. An ecocentric analysis asks what draining does to the wetland as a system and to the species and processes that depend on it. A complete discussion also looks forward: does the decision leave the town's children with options the town itself enjoyed? The facts — flood risk, species present, construction costs — are the same for everyone; the moral questions differ.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating environmental ethics as a debate about facts.** Science tells us what is happening — pollution levels, species counts, climate trends; ethics asks what we owe and to whom. Mixing the two turns a values question into a data question, and vice versa.
- **Using "anthropocentrism" to mean "caring about the environment at all."** Anthropocentrism names a specific position: only human beings have moral standing in their own right. A person can care deeply about nature while holding that view.
- **Treating deep ecology as just another word for conservation.** Deep ecology is a specific philosophical position associated with Arne Naess — all living things have value in their own right and humans are part of nature — not a label for any pro-environment stance.
- **Confusing instrumental and intrinsic value.** Instrumental value is value as a means to an end; intrinsic value is value as an end in itself. A thing can have both: a forest can be useful timber and valuable for its own sake.
- **Assuming sustainability is one settled idea.** Sustainability is contested: weak and strong versions disagree about whether natural resources can be replaced by human-made equivalents, and duties to future generations are debated.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anthropocentrism | Biocentrism | Grants moral standing to human beings only versus granting it to every individual living organism. |
| Instrumental value | Intrinsic value | Value as a means to further ends versus value as an end in itself, regardless of usefulness. |
| Weak sustainability | Strong sustainability | Allows natural capital to be replaced by equivalent human-made capital versus insists a critical stock of natural systems be preserved. |

## Key Takeaway

Environmental ethics asks what we owe the natural world and to whom: people alone, individual organisms, ecological wholes, or future generations. Its landmark voices — Leopold, Carson, Naess — frame questions the discipline still argues about, and none of those questions is settled.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In environmental ethics, what does 'anthropocentrism' name?** A human-centered approach that grants moral standing primarily or solely to human beings. **Answer: A.**
2. **A philosopher says a wetland has value 'as an end in itself, regardless of its usefulness to anyone.' Which distinction is she drawing on?** The distinction between intrinsic and instrumental value. **Answer: C.**
3. **A city planner argues that a forest should be protected because it cleans the air residents breathe and stores water the city uses. Which approach does this reasoning exemplify?** Anthropocentrism, because it justifies protection through benefits to human beings. **Answer: B.**
4. **The Brundtland Report defines sustainable development as development that 'meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.' Why does this definition raise ethical questions rather than settle them?** Because it leaves open contested matters — what counts as a need, and how present and future interests should be weighed — which philosophers continue to debate. **Answer: D.**
5. **Residents of a coastal town disagree about how to respond to flooding linked to changing climate. Which question belongs to environmental ethics rather than to climate science?** Who should bear the costs of reducing emissions and adapting — residents, industry, or future generations? **Answer: B.**
