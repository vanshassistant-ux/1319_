# Incidence

## In 30 Seconds

Incidence is the number of new cases of a disease that appear in a population over a set time period — the working definition used by MedlinePlus and the CDC. An incidence rate puts the count in perspective: new cases per population per time, like 5 new cases per 1,000 people per year. Incidence shows how fast disease spreads. It is not prevalence, which counts existing cases — that is a sibling topic. Incidence is a speedometer; prevalence is a snapshot.

## Why This Matters

Incidence answers the question public health most needs answered: is a disease gaining ground or losing it? When new cases climb, the disease is spreading faster; when they fall, it is slowing. That movement is the earliest signal of a problem — an outbreak taking off, a prevention program working, a vaccine campaign paying off. A count of all existing cases cannot show that movement, because it mixes old and new illness together. Incidence isolates the new cases, and new cases are where action happens. Every outbreak alert, and every success story, starts with new-case counts.

## The College Version

### What incidence is

Incidence is the number of new cases of a condition that develop in a population during a specific time period. MedlinePlus, the consumer health reference from the U.S. National Library of Medicine, defines incidence as the number of new cases of a condition, symptom, death, or injury that develop during a specific time period, such as a year. The CDC's Principles of Epidemiology course uses the same core idea: incidence refers to the occurrence of new cases of disease or injury in a population over a specified period of time. Two words in that definition do the work — new and period. New separates a case that began inside the window from one that already existed when the window opened. Period fixes how long the window is: a week, a season, a year. Change either word and the number changes. Incidence is never a free-floating count; it is a count tied to a population and a time period.

### The incidence rate: new cases per population per time

The raw incidence count answers "how many new cases?" but not "how fast, relative to how many people?" That second question is what an incidence rate answers. The CDC describes a rate as a measure of how quickly disease occurs in a population, and the incidence rate expresses new cases per population per time. Original example: two towns each report 20 new cases of a skin infection over one year. The counts are identical, but Town A has 2,000 residents and Town B has 20,000. Town A's incidence rate is 20 per 2,000 per year — 10 new cases per 1,000 people per year. Town B's is 20 per 20,000 per year — 1 new case per 1,000 people per year. Same count, ten times the speed. The rate is the fair comparison because it puts new cases on a per-person scale and pins them to a period. Epidemiologists often build the period directly into the denominator as person-time, but for everyday reporting, new cases per population per time carries the same idea in simpler arithmetic.

### Incidence versus prevalence: new versus existing

Incidence and prevalence are the two classic frequency measures of illness, and the CDC's course introduces them side by side: measures of morbidity frequency characterize the number of persons who become ill (incidence) or are ill at a given time (prevalence). Prevalence counts existing cases — everyone with the condition at a point in time, new and preexisting together. Incidence counts only cases that began during the period. Original example: a neighborhood clinic tracks a chronic condition. On January 1, 120 residents have it — a point-in-time count of existing cases. During the year, 30 more residents develop it for the first time — that is the year's incidence. The 120 and the 30 answer different questions: how many people carry the condition now, versus how fast new people are joining them. Prevalence is a full sibling topic of its own in this course; this lesson claims only the distinction.

### Why it matters — and why the period matters

Incidence matters because new cases are the earliest signal of change. When incidence rises, a disease is spreading faster; when it falls, spread is slowing. That is why outbreak detection and the evaluation of prevention programs lean on new-case counts. The period is not a detail; it is part of the measurement. Original example: a college clinic counts new cases of a respiratory illness. In the first two weeks of September, 30 students develop it — 30 new cases in 14 days. Over the whole 15-week semester, 120 new cases appear. Both numbers are incidence, but they describe different windows. Reading them as directly comparable would say the semester was "four times worse," when the pace was actually 15 new cases per week in September versus 8 per week across the semester. Same campus, same illness, two different speeds — the period is what makes the speed visible.

### Measuring it — and the honest framing

Measuring incidence follows a general practice: count the new cases, set them against the population, and attach the period to both. The CDC adds an important refinement: the denominator should be the population at risk — the people who could actually develop the disease. A count of new ovarian cancer cases, for example, belongs against a denominator of women, not the whole town. The counts themselves come from case reports, clinics, laboratories, and disease surveillance systems — the machinery that feeds the numerator. Then the honest framing: incidence is a speedometer, and prevalence is a snapshot. A speedometer shows how fast new cases are appearing; a snapshot shows how many cases exist at a moment. A speedometer cannot tell you how full the road is, and a snapshot cannot tell you how fast traffic is moving. Public health needs both, and the honest note is that each answers one question well and the other not at all.

## Key Vocabulary

- **incidence** — The number of new cases of a disease that develop in a population during a specific time period; the working definition used by MedlinePlus and the CDC.
- **new case** — A person who develops the disease for the first time within the counting period; a case that already existed when the period began is not new.
- **incidence rate** — New cases divided by population and by time, expressed like 5 new cases per 1,000 people per year; the fair way to compare spread across different-sized populations.
- **time period** — The window the count covers — a week, a season, or a year; changing the period changes the incidence number, so it is always stated with the count.
- **population at risk** — The people who could actually develop the disease and therefore belong in the denominator, such as women for ovarian cancer counts; the CDC's term for the correct denominator.
- **case count** — The raw number of new cases recorded during the period, before it is set against the population and the time to build a rate.
- **denominator** — The population, and often the period, that the new cases are divided by when an incidence rate is calculated.
- **prevalence** — The number of existing cases — new and preexisting together — at a point in time; the sibling measure that incidence is contrasted with in this lesson.

## Eli-10

Incidence is a simple idea: it counts NEW cases of a disease. If a town has 40 new cases of flu this week, the incidence for the week is 40. It only counts people who got sick for the first time inside the window — not people who were already sick before it started. The time period is part of the definition: new cases in a week, a month, or a year are three different incidence numbers. To compare towns fairly, public health turns the count into a rate — new cases per 1,000 people per year — so a small town and a big city can be compared on the same scale.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a bathtub filling with water. Incidence is the flow from the faucet — how much NEW water pours in each minute. Prevalence is the water already in the tub — everything that has accumulated. You cannot tell how fast the tub is filling by staring at the water level alone, because the level also depends on the drain. If you want to know whether the tub is filling faster, you watch the faucet, not the level. That is what incidence does for public health: it watches the new flow, and the rate tells you how fast the flow is per minute — per person, per year.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The bathtub is not a perfect picture of disease. Water does not change people's lives, while illness does, and a person who recovers is not "drained away" in a way that erases the event — the new case still counted. Incidence also counts episodes, not just people: one person can catch the same illness twice, and each episode is a new case. And "new" depends on where you draw the line — a case is new only relative to the period you chose. Still, faucet-versus-level captures the core difference: flow versus accumulation.

## Worked Example

Meadowbrook is a town of 10,000 people. At the start of the year, no one in town has a certain foodborne illness. Over the next 12 months, 25 residents develop it for the first time. The incidence for the year is 25 new cases. Now build the incidence rate: divide the new cases by the population and by the time — 25 new cases ÷ 10,000 people ÷ 1 year. That equals 2.5 new cases per 1,000 people per year. The count (25) tells you how much illness appeared; the rate (2.5 per 1,000 per year) tells you how fast it appeared on a scale that any town, of any size, can compare against. Same number of new cases — the rate makes Meadowbrook comparable to everywhere else.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Counting old cases as new.** Incidence counts only cases that began during the period. Someone diagnosed last year is not a new case this year, even if they still have the illness.
2. **Reporting a raw count as if it were a rate.** A count without the population denominator cannot be compared across towns — 40 new cases means something different in a town of 800 than in a town of 80,000.
3. **Ignoring the time period.** New cases over one week and new cases over one year are different numbers. Comparing them directly misreads how fast a disease is spreading.
4. **Dropping the period from the rate.** 5 new cases per 1,000 people per year is not the same as 5 per 1,000 per month. The period is part of the rate and must be stated with it.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Incidence** vs **prevalence**: incidence counts new cases that begin during a period; prevalence counts existing cases — new and preexisting together — at a point in time. New flow versus current level.
- **Case count** vs **incidence rate**: a case count is the raw number of new cases; a rate divides new cases by population and time so different-sized populations can be compared fairly.
- **Speedometer** vs **snapshot**: incidence is a speedometer — it shows how fast new cases are appearing; prevalence is a snapshot — it shows how many cases exist at a moment. Each answers one question well and the other not at all.

## Key Takeaway

Incidence is the count of new cases of a disease in a population over a time period — new cases, per population, per time. It is the speedometer of disease spread; prevalence, the count of existing cases, is the snapshot.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **A county health department announces that the incidence of a respiratory illness this season is 120. Which statement correctly describes that number?**
   - A. 120 people in the county developed the illness for the first time during this season
   - B. 120 people in the county currently have the illness, including people who had it before the season
   - C. 120 out of every 1,000 residents developed the illness this season
   - D. 120 people in the county died from the illness this season
   - **Answer: A.** Incidence counts new cases — people who developed the illness for the first time during the period. Counting everyone who currently has it (including preexisting cases) describes prevalence; the per-1,000 version is a rate; and deaths are a mortality measure, not incidence.

2. **Lakeview has 8,000 residents and recorded 40 new cases of a stomach illness over one year. Which of the following states the incidence rate correctly?**
   - A. 40 new cases per year, with no comparison to population
   - B. 40 new cases per 1,000 people per year
   - C. 5 new cases per 1,000 people per year
   - D. 5 new cases per 8,000 people per year
   - **Answer: C.** The incidence rate divides new cases by population and by time: 40 new cases ÷ 8,000 people ÷ 1 year = 5 new cases per 1,000 people per year. Option A drops the population, option B misdivides (40 per 1,000 would need 40 cases in a population of 1,000), and option D keeps the wrong scale.

3. **In January, a clinic counts 60 people with an ongoing skin condition, including 10 who were newly diagnosed that month. Which statement is correct?**
   - A. The incidence for January is 60, because all of them have the condition
   - B. The incidence for January is 10 new cases; the 60 is closer to a point-in-time count of existing cases
   - C. The incidence for January is 50, because the 10 new cases should be subtracted
   - D. Incidence can only be calculated for a full year, not a month
   - **Answer: B.** Incidence counts cases that began during the period — the 10 newly diagnosed. The 60 includes everyone with the condition at that point, new and preexisting together, which is the prevalence-style count. Incidence works for any stated period, including a month.

4. **A school reports 30 new cases of a mild illness in the first two weeks of September and 120 new cases across the full semester. Why can the two numbers not be compared directly?**
   - A. The first count came from a smaller population than the second
   - B. Both counts are incidence, so the periods do not affect them
   - C. The 120 includes cases from before September
   - D. The time periods differ — two weeks versus a semester — so the counting windows are not the same
   - **Answer: D.** Both are incidence counts, but they cover different windows, so they measure different speeds. Comparing 30 and 120 directly would say the semester was four times worse, when the weekly pace was actually 15 per week in September versus 8 per week across the semester. The period is part of the measurement.

5. **A public health officer says, "Incidence is a speedometer; prevalence is a snapshot." Which statement best matches that idea?**
   - A. Incidence shows how many cases exist at a moment, while prevalence shows how fast they are appearing
   - B. Incidence counts all cases ever recorded, while prevalence counts only this year's cases
   - C. Incidence shows how fast new cases are appearing, while prevalence shows how many cases exist at a moment
   - D. Incidence and prevalence are two names for the same count
   - **Answer: C.** The speedometer captures incidence: it measures the flow of new cases over time. The snapshot captures prevalence: the level of existing cases at a point in time. The other options swap the two or treat them as identical — incidence and prevalence answer different questions.
