# Social Determinants of Health

## In 30 Seconds

Social determinants of health are the conditions where people are born, grow, live, work, and age, plus the wider forces that shape daily life — the WHO's working definition. These non-medical conditions include neighborhoods, education, income and jobs, food access, social ties, and health care access. They matter because they help drive patterns in health outcomes: health tends to improve step by step with advantage, at every income level. Medical care is essential, but it is not the whole story.

## Why This Matters

When people get sick, the first question is usually about medical care — but the deeper question is why some groups of people get sick more often in the first place. The WHO reports that most of our health is determined by non-medical root causes: the conditions where we live, learn, work, and age. That means a neighborhood with safe housing and fresh food, a job with paid sick leave, and a school that helps students stay on track are health influences, not just background detail. Understanding social determinants reframes health from a purely medical story into a bigger one — and explains why improving daily conditions is part of improving health.

## Learning Objectives

- State the WHO working definition of social determinants of health: the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, plus the wider forces that shape daily life.
- Name the six domains covered in this lesson — neighborhood and built environment, education, income and jobs, food access, social context, and health care access — and give one original example of each.
- Explain why social determinants matter: these non-medical conditions help drive patterns in health outcomes and can influence health as much as or more than health care access alone, per the WHO.
- Describe the social gradient in one or two sentences: health tends to improve step by step with advantage at every level of income, as the WHO documents.
- Connect determinants to disparities: unequal conditions tend to produce unequal health outcomes, and note that health disparities are covered in their own sibling topic.
- Give an honest reading of the "ZIP code beats genetic code" slogan: it is a simplification with a real core, not a literal claim.

## The College Version

### What social determinants of health are

The WHO's working definition opens its fact sheet: social determinants of health are "the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live and age, and the wider forces that shape the conditions of daily life." Its health-topics page adds the second half of the picture: people's access to power, money, and resources. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention has adopted this same definition and describes social determinants as the nonmedical factors that influence health outcomes. Healthy People 2030, the U.S. national framework led by HHS, phrases it slightly differently — the conditions in the environments where people are born, live, learn, work, play, worship, and age — but the idea is the same. These are the conditions of daily life: where you sleep, what your street looks like, whether your paycheck covers the month, what food is reachable, who is around you, and whether a doctor is easy to see. Notice what the definition is not. It is not a list of personal choices, and it is not genetics. Those matter, but the determinants are the settings in which choices and bodies do their work.

### Six domains, one idea

This lesson organizes the determinants into six working categories, each with an example. Neighborhood and built environment: the physical places people inhabit — housing, sidewalks, parks, transportation, and pollution. Example: two apartment buildings on the same street, one with a working elevator and a shaded courtyard, the other with broken steps and no outdoor seating; residents of the second building move less every day without anyone choosing differently. Education: the quality and reach of schooling and literacy. Example: two districts in the same region, one with after-school tutoring and college advising, the other with neither; students start adulthood with different options. Income and jobs: wages, employment stability, and benefits such as paid leave. Example: a worker with paid sick days stays home with a fever; a coworker without them comes in, and the illness travels. Food access: how close, affordable, and available nutritious food is. Example: a block where the nearest full grocery store is a forty-minute bus ride away, so fresh produce runs out by Thursday. Social context: community support, safety, and the experience of discrimination. Example: a retiree whose neighbors bring meals after surgery recovers with help; an isolated one manages alone. Health care access: insurance, clinics, language services, and the timing of appointments. Example: a father skips a dental visit because the only clinic hours conflict with his shift and the next opening is months away. One honesty note: Healthy People 2030 groups these conditions into five official domains — economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context. Food access is not one of the five names; this lesson keeps it as its own category for clarity, while the official framework folds it into domains such as economic stability.

### Why they matter, and the social gradient

The core argument is factual and direct: these conditions help drive patterns in health outcomes. The WHO states it plainly — most of our health is determined by these non-medical root causes of ill health, which include quality education, access to nutritious food, and decent housing and working conditions. It goes further: research shows that social determinants can outweigh genetic influences or health care access in influencing health outcomes. Read that carefully. "Outweigh" is not "replace." Genetics and medical care remain powerful; the point is that daily-life conditions carry at least as much weight in shaping the patterns we observe. The second big idea is the gradient. The WHO describes it in one sentence: at all levels of income, health and illness follow a social gradient — the lower the socioeconomic position, the worse the health. The pattern is step by step, not just top versus bottom: each step up in income, education, or housing stability tends to go with better average health, and the same pattern repeats inside every income level. The WHO's Commission on Social Determinants of Health made this gradient the centerpiece of its 2008 report, Closing the gap in a generation.

### Determinants, disparities, and the ZIP code reality check

Put the two ideas together and the link is direct: unequal conditions produce unequal outcomes. Healthy People 2030 states that social determinants also contribute to wide health disparities and inequities. Determinants are the inputs — the conditions themselves; disparities are the unequal outcomes those conditions help produce. The measurement, documentation, and history of disparities belong to the sibling topic health disparities, so this lesson only draws the connection. Finally, the slogan you may have heard: "Your ZIP code matters more than your genetic code." The Kaiser Family Foundation notes the growing recognition that zip code is understood to be a stronger predictor of a person's health than their genetic code. The honest reading keeps both halves. ZIP codes are postal delivery routes, not neighborhoods — they are a rough stand-in. Genetics still matter enormously, and so does medical care. What is real in the slogan is the bundle: a ZIP code concentrates conditions — housing, jobs, food, safety, schools — and those conditions are strongly associated with health. It is a simplification with a real core.

## Key Vocabulary

- **social determinants of health** — The conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, plus the wider forces that shape daily life, in the WHO's working definition.
- **social gradient** — The pattern, documented by the WHO, in which health improves step by step as social and economic advantage increases, at every level of income.
- **domain** — One of the named categories of daily-life conditions, such as education or neighborhood and built environment, used to organize social determinants of health.
- **neighborhood and built environment** — A domain covering the physical places people inhabit — housing, sidewalks, parks, transportation, and pollution — and their effects on health.
- **economic stability** — A Healthy People 2030 domain covering income, employment, and related financial conditions that shape health.
- **food access** — The ease of reaching and affording nutritious food, including grocery store proximity, price, and transportation.
- **upstream factors** — Conditions that shape health before illness appears, usually outside the health care system, such as housing and schooling.
- **health disparities** — Unequal health outcomes closely linked with social or economic disadvantage; covered in depth by the sibling topic health disparities.

## Eli-10

Social determinants of health are the everyday conditions that shape how healthy people are — where you live, how much schooling you get, whether your job is stable, whether fresh food is nearby, who supports you, and how easy it is to see a doctor. The WHO's one-line definition: the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age. The big finding is that these conditions matter a lot: the WHO says most of our health is set by non-medical factors like these, and health improves step by step with advantage — each step up in stable conditions tends to go with better health.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of health like a garden and medical care like the watering can. A watering can saves a wilting plant, but the garden's real fate is set by the soil, the sun, and the drainage — the conditions that were there before planting. Two gardens from the same seed packet grow differently in rich soil versus rocky shade. Neighborhood, schooling, income, food, and support are the soil; the doctor is the watering can. Both matter — but the garden does not live by the watering can alone.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The garden analogy is too passive. Plants cannot dig themselves better soil, but people and communities can — people move, organize, and change their conditions. And medical care does far more than sprinkle water: surgery, vaccines, and antibiotics transform outcomes even when conditions are poor. The analogy also risks sounding like a person's fate is fixed by their ZIP code, which is exactly the oversimplification the honest framing section pushes back against.

## Worked Example

Two families live in the same city, and in the same week a chest cold moves through both households. The Vasquez family: steady income, employer health plan, a grocery store ten minutes' walk away, and a school with an after-school program. The Okafor family: hourly work without paid leave, no car, the nearest full grocery store a forty-minute bus ride away, and an apartment with a broken elevator. The cold lands differently. Mr. Vasquez gets a same-day telehealth visit and his kids' flu shots stay on schedule. Ms. Okafor cannot miss a shift to see a doctor; she waits for the Saturday urgent-care walk-in, and by then two children have the cold. Nobody chose worse care on purpose. Income, jobs, food access, housing, and health care access — the social determinants — patterned the outcomes before either family picked up a phone.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Reading the ZIP code slogan literally.** "Your ZIP code matters more than your genetic code" is popular shorthand, not a measured claim. ZIP codes are postal routes, and genetics and medical care still matter a great deal. The real core is that neighborhood conditions are strongly associated with health.
2. **Treating social determinants as "everything that isn't medicine."** The term has a specific meaning: the conditions of daily life and the wider forces that shape them, per the WHO — not a grab bag of unrelated factors.
3. **Thinking the gradient is only about rich versus poor.** The WHO describes a step-by-step pattern at every level of income: each step up in advantage tends to go with better health, not just the jump from the bottom to the top.
4. **Mixing up determinants with disparities.** Determinants are the conditions — the inputs. Disparities are the unequal outcomes those conditions help produce. The sibling topic health disparities owns the outcome side.
5. **Concluding that medical care does not matter.** The WHO says social determinants can outweigh health care access in influence — not that care is useless. Both matter, and daily conditions deserve attention too.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Social determinants of health vs health care access** — Health care access is one determinant domain among several; the determinants are the broader conditions of daily life, of which the care system is only one part.
- **The social gradient vs a two-group story** — The gradient is a step-by-step pattern at every level of income, rather than a simple split between the advantaged and the disadvantaged.
- **A ZIP code vs the conditions it stands for** — The postal code is a rough stand-in; the real influences are the housing, jobs, food, safety, and schools that a place concentrates.
- **Social determinants vs health disparities** — Determinants are the conditions; disparities are the unequal outcomes they help produce. Each is its own topic in this subject.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **The WHO's working definition describes social determinants of health as:**
   - A. the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, plus the wider forces that shape daily life
   - B. the medical treatments and technologies available in a country's clinics and hospitals
   - C. the personal habits people choose, such as how much they exercise or sleep
   - D. the genetic traits people inherit from their parents

   **Answer: A.** The WHO defines social determinants of health as the conditions in which people are born, grow, work, live, and age, plus the wider forces shaping daily life. Clinics and treatments are health care access; personal habits and genetics are related influences but are not the definition.

2. **Healthy People 2030 groups social determinants of health into five official domains. Which pair of domains is on that list?**
   - A. Genetic makeup and climate zones
   - B. Restaurant density and fashion trends
   - C. Neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context
   - D. Hospital size and prescription volume

   **Answer: C.** Healthy People 2030's five SDOH domains are economic stability, education access and quality, health care access and quality, neighborhood and built environment, and social and community context. The other pairs are not among the five.

3. **Lena's block has a corner store with canned goods but no fresh produce, and the nearest full grocery store is a forty-minute bus ride away. Which social determinant domain does this best illustrate?**
   - A. Health care access
   - B. Social and community context
   - C. Education access and quality
   - D. Food access

   **Answer: D.** This is a food access example: how close, affordable, and available nutritious food is. Healthy People 2030 lists access to nutritious foods among its SDOH examples and folds such conditions into its five official domains; this lesson treats food access as its own working category.

4. **Amara and DeShawn do the same factory job. Amara earns $18 an hour with paid sick days; DeShawn earns $14 an hour with no paid leave. Based on the social gradient idea the WHO describes, what would researchers most expect to observe?**
   - A. No health difference between them, since both do identical work
   - B. Health differences that run step by step with advantage, with the same pattern repeating at every income level
   - C. DeShawn will be healthier because his job demands more effort
   - D. Their health will be determined by genetics alone, with income playing no role

   **Answer: B.** The social gradient is the pattern in which health improves step by step as social and economic advantage increases, at all levels of income — not only between the richest and poorest. It describes patterns in groups, not a guarantee for any one person.

5. **A campaign poster reads: "Your ZIP code matters more than your genetic code." What is the honest way to interpret that slogan?**
   - A. Genes have no effect on health; only the mailing address matters
   - B. ZIP codes directly cause illness, the way a virus does
   - C. The claim is literally true for every individual person
   - D. It is a simplification: a ZIP code stands in for a bundle of neighborhood conditions associated with health, while genetics and medical care still matter

   **Answer: D.** The slogan compresses a real finding — place conditions are strongly associated with health — into a catchy line. KFF notes zip code is increasingly understood as a stronger predictor of health than genetic code. The honest reading treats it as shorthand, not a literal claim.

## Key Takeaway

Health is shaped by the conditions where people are born, grow, live, work, and age — and those daily-life conditions, not medicine alone, drive the patterns we see in health outcomes.
