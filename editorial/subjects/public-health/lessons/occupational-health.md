# Occupational Health

## In 30 Seconds

Occupational health is the branch of public health that promotes and protects the well-being of people at work. The WHO working definition calls it the area of public health that promotes and maintains the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations. It sorts workplace dangers into five families of hazards, fixes them at the source first, and runs everyday programs like training, protective equipment, and health checks. Occupational health nurses, safety engineers, employers, and regulators all share the job.

## Why This Matters

Most adults spend roughly a third of their waking hours at work, so the conditions of that time shape health as much as diet or exercise do. The WHO fact sheet on protecting workers' health notes that good working conditions protect people from physical and psychosocial hazards while supporting income and dignity, and that work-related health problems cost most countries an estimated 4-6% of GDP. Understanding occupational health lets you see any workplace clearly: why a warehouse installs ventilation, why a hospital tracks needlesticks, why a call center rotates shifts. It also explains a hard global truth: specialized occupational health services reach only about 15% of the world's workers, and why that gap matters.

## Learning Objectives

- Define occupational health using the WHO working definition.
- Name the five families of workplace hazards and give an original example of each.
- Explain the hierarchy of controls idea, including why controlling a hazard at its source beats protecting workers from it.
- Describe the everyday workplace programs, training, protective equipment, and health monitoring, and what each does.
- Identify the main actors in occupational health, nurses, safety engineers, employers, and regulators, and the role of each.
- Analyze a workplace situation to classify its hazards and propose controls in the order the hierarchy prefers.

## The College Version

### What occupational health is

Occupational health is the field that studies and protects the health of people at work. The working definition used by the World Health Organization describes it as an area of work in public health to promote and maintain the highest degree of physical, mental and social well-being of workers in all occupations. Its objectives: maintain and promote workers' health and working capacity; improve working conditions so they are conducive to safety and health; and build work organizations and cultures that support safety and health. The WHO notes the field draws on several disciplines at once, including occupational medicine, nursing, ergonomics, psychology, hygiene, and safety. The setting matters as much as the science: economically active people spend roughly one third of their time at the workplace.

### The five families of hazards

Workplace dangers usually sort into five families. Physical hazards are forms of energy or environment that can injure, such as noise, heat, cold, vibration, or radiation. Original example: the roar of a stamping press slowly wearing down operators' hearing. Chemical hazards are substances that can poison, burn, or irritate, such as solvents, dusts, or fumes. Original example: a furniture refinisher breathing solvent fumes all day. Biological hazards are living things that can infect, such as bacteria, viruses, or parasites. Original example: a hospital phlebotomist exposed to a patient's blood. Ergonomic hazards come from how the body is used, such as repetitive motion, awkward posture, or heavy lifting. Original example: a packing line where workers make the same cut thousands of times a shift. Psychosocial hazards come from the social and psychological demands of work, such as stress, long hours, harassment, or violence. Original example: a call-center agent facing angry calls all day with no breaks. The WHO fact sheet lists heat, noise, dust, hazardous chemicals, unsafe machines, and psychological stress among workplace health risks. The families overlap: a loud machine is a physical hazard, but the tension it creates is psychosocial.

### The prevention idea: control at the source first

The field's central habit is prevention: stop harm before it happens and control the hazard as close to its source as possible. NIOSH and OSHA describe the ordering as the hierarchy of controls. From most to least effective: elimination (remove the hazard entirely), substitution (replace it with something safer), engineering controls (isolate people from it), administrative controls (change how people work), and personal protective equipment (shield the worker with gear). NIOSH explains that elimination is preferred because no exposure can occur. Original example: a print shop that switched from solvent-based inks to plant-based inks removed the hazard instead of handing out respirators. The general idea of prevention, including its three levels, belongs to the sibling topic Prevention; occupational health is where those ideas get applied to the workplace.

### The workplace programs

Prevention shows up in everyday programs. Training teaches workers to recognize hazards and handle them safely: OSHA's Recommended Practices list education and training as a core element of a safety and health program, and federal law gives workers the right to training in a language they understand. Protective equipment puts a physical barrier between the worker and the hazard: earplugs, respirators, gloves, hard hats. Health monitoring checks workers' bodies for early signs of harm: the WHO fact sheet calls for medical surveillance for early detection of occupational diseases, and OSHA maintains screening and surveillance requirements for certain hazards. A noisy plant does all three: training on hearing protection, fitted earplugs, and annual hearing tests.

### Who does the work

Occupational health is a team effort. Occupational health nurses run health monitoring, treat work-related problems, and educate workers; the WHO lists nursing among the field's disciplines. Safety engineers and safety professionals design hazards out of equipment, processes, and buildings, the engineering controls at the heart of the hierarchy. Employers carry the legal duty: OSHA states that your employer must keep the workplace free from known safety and health hazards. Regulators set and enforce the rules: OSHA's mission is to ensure safe and healthful working conditions by setting and enforcing standards and providing training and outreach, while NIOSH, the research agency created by the Occupational Safety and Health Act of 1970, studies worker safety and health and recommends protections.

### The honest note: work should not make you sick

The field exists because work does make people sick, and the honest picture is incomplete. The WHO fact sheet reports that specialized occupational health services reach only about 15% of workers worldwide, and that more than 85% of workers in small enterprises, the informal sector, agriculture, and among migrants have no occupational health coverage at all. Chronic respiratory disease, musculoskeletal disorders, noise-induced hearing loss, and skin problems are among the most common occupational diseases. None of this is inevitable: effective interventions, such as ventilation, noise control, and substituting dangerous chemicals, are known and affordable. 'Work should not make you sick' is not a description of how things are; it is the standard the field is trying to meet.

### The honest framing: survivable and dignified

Occupational health is the field that makes work survivable and dignified. Survivability is the floor: fewer injuries, less illness, fewer workers sent home damaged. Dignity is the ceiling: a job that does not quietly cost a worker their hearing, their lungs, or their sleep, and where the person doing the work is worth protecting. The WHO definition reaches exactly this far: physical, mental, and social well-being, not merely 'not injured.' Most of this work is unglamorous, ventilation ducts, training sessions, hearing tests, but those are the machinery of a basic promise: a paycheck should not come with a price tag on a worker's body.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Occupational health:** The area of public health that promotes and maintains the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations (WHO working definition).
- **Hazard:** Anything in the workplace that can harm a worker's health or safety.
- **Physical hazard:** A hazard from energy or the environment, such as noise, heat, cold, vibration, or radiation.
- **Chemical hazard:** A hazard from a substance, such as a solvent, dust, fume, or pesticide, that can poison, burn, or irritate.
- **Biological hazard:** A hazard from a living thing, such as a bacterium, virus, or parasite, that can infect workers.
- **Ergonomic hazard:** A hazard from how the body is used on the job, such as repetitive motion, awkward posture, or heavy lifting.
- **Psychosocial hazard:** A hazard from the social and psychological demands of work, such as stress, long hours, harassment, or violence.
- **Hierarchy of controls:** The ordered list of ways to reduce workplace hazards, from removing the hazard entirely down to shielding the worker with personal protective equipment.
- **Personal protective equipment (PPE):** Gear workers wear to shield themselves from hazards, such as earplugs, respirators, gloves, or hard hats.
- **Health monitoring:** Regular health checks, also called medical surveillance, that catch work-related harm early.

## Eli-10

**The simple version.** Occupational health is the field that keeps work from hurting the people who do it. It looks at everything at a job that could harm someone, loud machines, strong chemicals, germs, awkward movements, stress, and groups them into five families of hazards. Then it fixes them, starting with the strongest fix: get rid of the hazard. If you cannot, put a safer thing in its place, then separate workers from it, then change how the job is done, and only last give workers gear to wear. On top of that, workplaces run programs: training so people know the risks, equipment so hazards do not reach the body, and health checks so harm is caught early. Nurses, safety engineers, employers, and government regulators each play a part. The goal is simple even when the work is not: your job should not make you sick.

## Eli's Analogy

**The analogy.** Think of a cook in a kitchen with a greasy floor. The strongest fix is elimination: cook differently so the grease never forms. Next, substitution: a less slippery oil. Then engineering: a textured floor mat. Then administration: a rule that spills get mopped within a minute. Last, personal protection: slip-resistant shoes. A good kitchen uses the first fixes and keeps the shoes as backup, not as the whole plan. That is the hierarchy of controls in one room.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** A kitchen is a single room the cook can see, while a workplace is a web of processes, people, and schedules, and many hazards cannot be removed outright: a hospital cannot eliminate germs, only contain them. The hierarchy is also a preference order, not a guarantee: even the best controls need training, health monitoring, and honest inspection to actually work.

## Worked Example

The Rivertown Cabinet Shop refinishes furniture with solvent-based strippers that fume up the shop, sanders that roar, and long days of lifting. The owner, a safety engineer, and an occupational health nurse worked through the hierarchy. They replaced the solvent stripper with a water-based product (substitution), added a downdraft table that pulls dust away from workers (engineering control), and set up two-person lifting teams for heavy pieces (administrative control). For the dust that remains, workers wear fitted respirators (PPE). The nurse runs annual lung-function and hearing tests, and everyone gets a training session on the new chemicals each quarter. Two years later the shop's injury log is down and hearing-test results are stable: the hazard list did not disappear, but the harm stopped compounding.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Occupational health is just about accidents on the job.**
   - *Correction:* It also covers illness from chemicals, noise, germs, repetitive motion, and stress. The WHO fact sheet lists chronic respiratory disease, musculoskeletal disorders, hearing loss, and skin problems among the most common occupational diseases.
2. **Personal protective equipment is the best protection.**
   - *Correction:* The hierarchy puts removing the hazard at the source first. PPE leaves the hazard in place and depends on the gear being right, worn, and working, so it is the last line, not the first.
3. **Work-related harm only happens in factories.**
   - *Correction:* Offices, hospitals, farms, and warehouses carry their own hazards: stress, germs, repetitive motion, noise, and chemicals are everywhere people work.
4. **Health monitoring is optional paperwork.**
   - *Correction:* Medical surveillance catches harm early, when the workplace can still be changed and permanent damage can be stopped.
5. **Occupational, environmental, and community health are the same field.**
   - *Correction:* Occupational health is organized around the workplace and its workers; environmental health and community health (sibling topics) are organized around the wider environment and the places where people live.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Occupational health vs. Environmental health:** Occupational health focuses on hazards people face at work, inside the workplace; environmental health (the sibling topic) focuses on hazards in the wider environment, air, water, soil, that affect whole communities. They overlap where hazards cross the fence line, like factory dust drifting into a neighborhood.
- **Elimination vs. Personal protective equipment:** Elimination removes the hazard so exposure cannot happen at all, which NIOSH calls the preferred fix; PPE leaves the hazard in place and shields the worker, and it fails if the gear is wrong, worn out, or not worn.
- **Occupational health vs. Community health:** Occupational health is organized around a defined workplace and its workforce; community health (the sibling topic) is organized around a place and everyone who lives there. A factory's worker health program is occupational; a county's restaurant inspection program is community health.

## Key Takeaway

Occupational health is public health at work: hazards sorted into five families, fixed at the source first, and watched over by training, equipment, and health checks, so that a job protects a worker's body and dignity instead of spending them.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **A workplace wellness committee is writing a one-sentence introduction to occupational health for new employees. Which sentence matches the WHO working definition?**
   - A. Occupational health is the study of diseases that pass from animals to humans on farms and in markets.
   - B. Occupational health is the branch of public health that promotes and maintains the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations.
   - C. Occupational health is the emergency treatment of workers injured on the job.
   - D. Occupational health is the management of wages, schedules, and benefits to keep employees satisfied.

   **Answer: B.** The WHO describes occupational health as an area of work in public health that promotes and maintains the highest degree of physical, mental, and social well-being of workers in all occupations. Option A describes zoonotic disease study, option C describes emergency medicine, and option D describes human-resources management, none of which matches the WHO definition.

2. **A consultant reviews a delivery company. She finds drivers who spend long, solo hours fighting traffic and loaders who repeat the same lifting and twisting motion hundreds of times a day. Which pairing correctly classifies these two hazards?**
   - A. Long solo driving is a psychosocial hazard; repetitive lifting and twisting is an ergonomic hazard.
   - B. Long solo driving is a chemical hazard; repetitive lifting and twisting is a biological hazard.
   - C. Long solo driving is a biological hazard; repetitive lifting and twisting is a physical hazard.
   - D. Long solo driving is an ergonomic hazard; repetitive lifting and twisting is a psychosocial hazard.

   **Answer: A.** Psychosocial hazards come from the social and psychological demands of work, such as stress and long hours, which fits solo driving in traffic. Ergonomic hazards come from how the body is used, such as repetitive motion and awkward lifting, which fits the loaders' task. The other options assign the wrong families.

3. **A screen-printing shop's solvent-based ink gives workers headaches and irritated lungs. Following the hierarchy of controls, which step should the shop take first?**
   - A. Require workers to wear respirators whenever they print.
   - B. Post warning signs and rotate workers through shorter printing shifts.
   - C. Run a training session explaining the risks of the ink.
   - D. Switch to a plant-based ink that does the same job without the solvents.

   **Answer: D.** The hierarchy orders controls by general effectiveness, with elimination and substitution at the top; NIOSH notes that elimination is preferred because no exposure can occur. Switching to a plant-based ink substitutes a safer product at the source, the strongest option here. Respirators (PPE), signs and rotation (administrative controls), and training all sit lower in the hierarchy.

4. **A small metal-stamping plant wants to build a real occupational health effort. Which assignment of roles matches the lesson?**
   - A. The regulator trains workers on every machine; the employer inspects the plant for violations; the nurse sets production quotas; the safety engineer runs payroll.
   - B. The employer keeps the workplace free of known hazards; the safety engineer designs hazards out of equipment; the occupational health nurse runs health monitoring and training; the regulator sets and enforces standards.
   - C. The nurse writes safety regulations; the safety engineer treats injured workers; the employer monitors community air; the regulator pays workers' compensation claims.
   - D. The safety engineer treats work-related illness; the nurse designs the building; the employer enforces federal law on itself; the regulator only collects fines.

   **Answer: B.** OSHA states that the employer must keep the workplace free from known hazards; safety engineers and safety professionals produce the engineering controls that design hazards out; occupational health nurses run the clinical side, including health monitoring; and OSHA's mission is to set and enforce standards. The other options scramble the roles.

5. **An occupational health service begins working with a metal plating plant: annual hearing tests, fitted ear protection, and training on hazard reporting. Two years later, hearing-test referrals are down. Why does the lesson's framework treat this as the field working as intended?**
   - A. Because the plant has now eliminated every hazard, and occupational health only counts when hazards are gone.
   - B. Because the improvements only matter when a regulator is on site, and one was.
   - C. Because the plant combined health monitoring, protective equipment, and training, the everyday programs that make work less harmful, and the trend suggests they are helping.
   - D. Because hearing loss is genetic, and the tests prove the plant has no further duties.

   **Answer: C.** The lesson describes workplace programs as training, protective equipment, and health monitoring; combining all three and seeing a better trend is exactly the field working as intended. Option A overclaims, since the hierarchy reduces hazards but rarely eliminates all of them, option B misreads regulation as the only driver, and option D is false: noise-induced hearing loss is a documented occupational disease.
