# Evidence in Public Health

## In 30 Seconds

Evidence-based public health is the practice of using the best available research and data to guide decisions about programs, policies, and services. The evidence comes in three main forms: surveillance data that track disease, studies that compare groups, and program evaluations that ask whether an effort worked. Some studies are stronger than others, but a good question matters more than a study's rank. Evidence informs decisions; it does not make them alone. Think of evidence as a flashlight, not a map.

## Why This Matters

Every health decision a department, a legislature, or a community makes rests on what people believe the evidence shows. Understanding evidence-based public health teaches you where that belief comes from: which data are counted, which studies carry weight, and which questions remain open. In courses, it explains why some programs are adopted and others dropped, and why two experts can look at the same numbers and disagree about policy. In practice, it is the difference between a program that sounds good and one that is known to work. Learning to weigh evidence also builds a habit that outlasts any single job: asking what we actually know before deciding what to do.

## Learning Objectives

- Define evidence-based public health as using the best available research and data to guide decisions, attributing the working definition to CDC.
- Name three kinds of evidence — surveillance data, studies, and program evaluations — and give one line on what each contributes.
- Distinguish cohort studies, case-control studies, and randomized trials by their basic approach.
- Explain the evidence-hierarchy idea and the caveat that a well-asked question matters more than a study's rank.
- Describe what evidence can and cannot do, including how it informs rather than decides.
- Apply the evidence lens to a realistic program or policy decision.

## The College Version

### What evidence-based public health is

Evidence-based public health is the practice of using the best available research and data to guide decisions about the health of populations. The working definition used in this lesson follows CDC's framing of evidence-based practice: decisions about programs, policies, and services should rest on the best available evidence rather than on habit, fashion, or the loudest opinion in the room. CDC's own gateway pages make the same point in practice, compiling the tools and resources — from vaccine recommendations to the Guide to Community Preventive Services — that decision makers are expected to consult. The word "best" does real work here. Evidence is not whatever study is easiest to find or whatever claim is repeated most often; it is the research and data that survive scrutiny. The Community Preventive Services Task Force, whose evidence reviews make up The Community Guide, describes its recommendations as the gold standard for what works to protect and improve population health. That standard is the target: public health actions chosen because the weight of evidence says they work, not because they sound reasonable.

### The three kinds of evidence

Public health draws on three broad kinds of evidence, and each answers a different kind of question. Surveillance data are the systematic, continuous collection and analysis of health information, watching how disease moves through a population over time — which is why CDC's classic definition calls surveillance the continued watchfulness over the distribution and trends of disease. Surveillance tells you what is happening: how much, where, and whether it is getting better or worse. Studies compare groups of people to test why something happens — whether an exposure is linked to an outcome, and how strong the link is. Program evaluations ask whether an organized effort — a clinic, a campaign, a policy — actually worked in the real world, measuring effectiveness and, often, cost. A health department deciding what to do next typically needs all three: surveillance to see the problem, studies to understand its causes, and evaluations of past programs to know which solutions have delivered results.

### The study types

When the question is whether something causes or prevents disease, the study types named in CDC's Principles of Epidemiology course carry the analysis. In a cohort study, researchers record whether each participant is exposed or not, then follow the group over time and compare disease rates between the exposed and unexposed — which is how the famous Framingham study established risk factors for heart disease. In a case-control study, researchers start with people who have the disease and a comparison group who do not, then look backward to compare their past exposures — a design that proved ideal for tracing a hepatitis A outbreak to a contaminated food. In a randomized trial, the investigator controls the exposure, randomly assigning participants to treatment or comparison groups — which is how new vaccines and drugs are tested for efficacy. The one-line version: cohort studies watch forward, case-control studies look back, and randomized trials assign.

### The evidence hierarchy, and its caveat

Not all studies carry the same weight, and public health has a rough ordering of designs, often drawn as a pyramid: randomized trials generally sit near the top because random assignment controls for differences between groups; cohort studies come next, then case-control studies, then weaker designs such as cross-sectional snapshots. CDC's own teaching notes that a cross-sectional study is analytically weaker than a cohort or case-control study because it cannot disentangle what causes disease from what merely accompanies it. The honest caveat is that rank is not destiny. A sloppy randomized trial can mislead more than a careful cohort study, and a study of the wrong question is worthless no matter how strong its design. The hierarchy tells you which evidence deserves more trust, but the question being asked matters more than the rank of the design that answers it.

### What evidence can and cannot do

Evidence informs decisions; it does not make them. The Community Preventive Services Task Force weighs both effectiveness and economic impact when it issues recommendations, and even then a community must decide whether a proven program fits its budget, its values, and its circumstances. Two officials can read the same study and disagree about what to do — one pointing at the health gain, the other at the cost — and both can be reasoning honestly. Evidence narrows the range of defensible choices, which is its real power, but it cannot settle questions about values, fairness, or how much a community is willing to pay. The discipline of evidence-based public health is to make the evidence visible and then let the full decision, values included, happen in the light.

### Evidence and policy

Data guide policy choices, and policy choices in turn shape health. CDC publishes guidelines and recommendations specifically to help decision makers choose courses of action — from immunization schedules to clinical preventive services — and the findings of The Community Guide exist to inform decisions about programs, services, and policies at the community level. When a state considers a smoke-free law or a school district weighs a vaccination requirement, the arguments on both sides lean on evidence: coverage rates, exposure studies, and evaluations of similar policies elsewhere. Evidence does not dictate the law, but it sets the terms of the debate, and a policy that ignores strong evidence usually pays for it later. The machinery of turning evidence into law — how rules are made, enforced, and challenged — is the territory of the sibling topic public-health-policy; this lesson's point is the connection: policy choices are stronger when they ride on evidence, and weaker when they ignore it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Evidence-based public health:** The practice of using the best available research and data to guide decisions about programs, policies, and services for populations, following the working definition attributed to CDC.
- **Evidence:** Information gathered through systematic methods, such as surveillance data, studies, or program evaluations, that can be weighed when making a decision.
- **Surveillance data:** Health information collected continuously and systematically to watch the distribution and trends of disease in a population over time.
- **Cohort study:** A study that groups people by whether they are exposed or not and follows them over time to compare who develops the outcome of interest.
- **Case-control study:** A study that starts with people who have a condition and a comparison group without it, then compares the two groups' past exposures.
- **Randomized trial:** A study in which the investigator randomly assigns participants to treatment or comparison groups, so the groups differ only by chance at the start.
- **Program evaluation:** A systematic assessment of whether a program, service, or policy worked as intended and whether it was worth its cost.
- **Evidence hierarchy:** The rough ordering of study designs by how much trust their results usually deserve, with randomized trials generally at the top.
- **Policy:** A decision, rule, or law adopted by an authority, such as a legislature or agency, to guide action on a public issue.

## Eli-10

**The simple version.** Evidence-based public health is a simple habit: before a city, a state, or an agency does something about health, it looks at what is actually known. That means checking the numbers on how much disease is out there, reading studies that compare groups of people, and asking whether past programs worked. The idea is not that data are the only voice — it is that decisions should not be made in the dark. Some studies are stronger than others, and knowing which is which matters, but the most important thing is the question you are asking. A good question studied honestly beats a fancy study of the wrong question.

## Eli's Analogy

**The analogy.** Evidence is a flashlight, not a map. A flashlight does not draw the whole route or tell you where to go; it shows you the next few steps and the obstacles in front of you. It can point at a hazard you would have missed, and it makes walking safer. But you still choose the direction, and you still have to walk. In public health, the flashlight is the research and the data: they illuminate the situation, and the walking is the decision.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The flashlight analogy breaks down because a flashlight only reveals what is directly ahead, while evidence can be gathered from many directions at once — surveillance, trials, evaluations — and can even change the destination, not just the path. A beam can also be misaimed: evidence can be over-interpreted, cherry-picked, or given more weight than it can carry. Evidence does not only light the way; it can reshape the question itself.

## Worked Example

The city of Marlow noticed that asthma-related emergency visits had risen for three winters in a row, so its health department set out to decide whether to fund a home-visit program for families of children with asthma. First it pulled surveillance data from local hospitals and clinics to confirm the trend and see which neighborhoods were hit hardest. Then it read the studies: a randomized trial showed home visits cut emergency visits by a third, while a case-control study suggested the effect was smaller, and a cohort study found the benefit lasted two years. Finally, it reviewed a program evaluation from a neighboring city that had run the same model, which showed real-world savings. The evidence pointed clearly at the program, but the council still had to weigh its cost against other needs before voting to fund it. The data lit the way; the decision still had to be walked.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Believing one study settles a question.**
   - *Correction:* Single studies are pieces of a larger picture. Weight comes from the body of evidence, and a strong design generally deserves more trust than a weak one.
2. **Treating all studies as if they weighed the same.**
   - *Correction:* Randomized trials usually carry more weight than observational designs, and CDC notes that cross-sectional snapshots are weaker than cohort or case-control studies. But the question asked matters more than the rank of the design.
3. **Assuming evidence makes the decision by itself.**
   - *Correction:* Evidence informs choices; it does not decide them. Values, budgets, and community preferences are legitimate parts of the decision, and the task force that reviews evidence weighs cost alongside effectiveness.
4. **Confusing surveillance with research.**
   - *Correction:* Surveillance watches what is happening in a population over time; studies compare groups to test why. Both are evidence, but they answer different questions.
5. **Reading "evidence-based" as "proven beyond doubt."**
   - *Correction:* It means the decision rests on the best available evidence, which is often incomplete, contested, or evolving. Honesty about limits is part of the practice.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Cohort study vs. Case-control study:** A cohort study starts with exposure and follows people forward to see who develops the outcome; a case-control study starts with the disease and looks backward at past exposures. Cohort studies can take decades; case-control studies are faster but more vulnerable to faulty recall.
- **Randomized trial vs. Observational study:** In a randomized trial the investigator assigns who gets the exposure, so the groups start out alike; in an observational study, exposure happens naturally and the investigator only records it. Random assignment is why trials usually sit at the top of the evidence hierarchy.
- **Surveillance data vs. Program evaluation:** Surveillance continuously tracks disease trends so you know what is happening; a program evaluation asks whether a specific program changed those trends. Surveillance can flag a problem; evaluation shows whether a chosen fix worked.

## Key Takeaway

Evidence-based public health means letting the best available research and data guide decisions, while remembering that evidence informs a choice and does not make it. Evidence is a flashlight, not a map.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **A health department intern asks her supervisor what evidence-based public health means. Which answer matches the working definition used in this lesson?**
   - A. Choosing whichever programs other cities happen to run.
   - B. Using the best available research and data to guide decisions about programs, policies, and services.
   - C. Trusting expert opinion over any data.
   - D. Publishing health statistics without acting on them.

   **Answer: B.** The working definition, following CDC's framing, is using the best available research and data to guide decisions. Option A is imitation, not evidence; option C inverts the idea by placing opinion above data; and option D describes reporting without deciding, which is the opposite of using evidence to act.

2. **A county collects weekly reports of flu-like illness from clinics and emergency rooms to watch whether cases are rising. Which kind of evidence is this?**
   - A. A program evaluation.
   - B. A randomized trial.
   - C. Surveillance data.
   - D. A case-control study.

   **Answer: C.** Systematic, continuous collection of health reports to watch disease trends is surveillance, which CDC defines as continued watchfulness over the distribution and trends of disease. Option A evaluates whether a program worked, option B randomly assigns treatments, and option D compares past exposures of sick and well people.

3. **Researchers enroll 4,000 adults, record which ones work night shifts and which work days, then follow everyone for eight years to compare rates of sleep disorders. What kind of study is this?**
   - A. A case-control study.
   - B. A cohort study.
   - C. A randomized trial.
   - D. A program evaluation.

   **Answer: B.** The researchers observe who is exposed, do not assign it, and follow the groups forward to compare outcomes, which is the defining structure of a cohort study. Option A would start with people who already have sleep disorders and look backward; option C would require random assignment to shifts; option D assesses whether a program worked.

4. **A mayor's office is choosing between two anti-smoking programs: one backed by a large randomized trial and one backed only by a single case-control study. An evidence-literate adviser would most likely say what?**
   - A. The case-control study is automatically wrong because it is observational.
   - B. Only randomized trials can ever be trusted, so the choice is obvious.
   - C. Rank the two studies, and if the ranks differ, follow the higher rank without further thought.
   - D. The trial's evidence generally carries more weight, but the best choice also depends on the question, the setting, and the program's cost.

   **Answer: D.** Randomized trials usually sit above case-control studies in the evidence hierarchy, so the trial's support counts for more, but rank is not destiny: the right choice also depends on whether the study matches the question and setting, and on cost, which the evidence review weighs alongside effectiveness. Options A and B overstate the hierarchy into a rule, and option C forgets that good questions matter more than rank.

5. **A state task force finds strong evidence that a school-based vaccination campaign raises coverage rates, but the legislature is weighing the cost and community concerns. What does evidence-based public health say the evidence should do?**
   - A. Inform the decision, while cost, values, and community preferences also carry weight.
   - B. Settle the matter, because evidence is the only legitimate input.
   - C. Be ignored whenever lawmakers disagree with it.
   - D. Be withheld until after the vote to avoid bias.

   **Answer: A.** Evidence informs decisions but does not make them alone; reviews weigh effectiveness and economic impact, and communities add values, resources, and preferences. Option B overstates evidence into the sole arbiter, option C discards the evidence entirely, and option D hides information, which is the opposite of the transparency evidence-based practice is meant to provide.
