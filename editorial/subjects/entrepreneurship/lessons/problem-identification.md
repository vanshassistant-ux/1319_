# Problem Identification

## In 30 Seconds

Problem identification turns a vague complaint into a bounded, testable description of a customer’s situation. An entrepreneur asks who has the problem, when it occurs, what gets in the way, and what consequence follows. A complaint or a decline in sales may be a useful signal, but it is not automatically the cause. Treat early explanations as hypotheses, then use appropriate evidence to decide whether to revise, narrow, or reject them.

## Why This Matters

A venture can build a polished solution for a problem that is too vague, affects the wrong group, or has been misdiagnosed. Clear problem identification makes later work more disciplined: it gives customer discovery and market research a focused question, helps separate evidence from assumptions, and prevents a team from mistaking a preferred product for a demonstrated need. The skill also transfers beyond startups, because it is a way to reason carefully before committing resources to an explanation or response.

## Learning Objectives

- Define problem identification as a bounded description of a customer context and an unmet need or harmful gap.
- Distinguish an observable symptom from a plausible underlying cause.
- Frame a problem hypothesis that identifies a customer, situation, consequence, and assumption to check.
- Analyze what a piece of evidence can support and what it cannot establish.
- Apply problem framing to revise an overly broad entrepreneurial claim.

## The College Version

### A problem is a gap in a particular context

In entrepreneurship, problem identification is the work of describing an important gap before proposing a remedy. The gap is between a present condition and a desired condition for a particular customer or user. OpenStax describes clarification as recognizing the gap between the current and desired states, including a gap between customer or social needs and actual circumstances. That idea matters because the phrase “people need a better app” is not yet a problem definition. It does not say which people, what they are trying to do, or what “better” means.

A usable problem description sets boundaries. Name the affected group, the situation or task, the obstacle, and the consequence. For example: “Independent tutors who schedule several weekly sessions lose time reconciling changes made by families in separate text-message threads, leading to missed updates and duplicate follow-up.” This statement does not claim that a calendar application will solve the issue. It identifies a setting in which a burden may exist. Its boundaries make it possible to investigate the claim rather than defend a favorite solution.

### Symptoms are signals, not explanations

A symptom is an observable result or warning sign: abandoned carts, late deliveries, repeat support requests, or falling renewal rates. It can show that something deserves attention, but it does not by itself identify why it happened. The same symptom may arise from different mechanisms. If customers abandon an online checkout, possible contributors could include an unexpected delivery charge, a payment error, a confusing form, a requirement to create an account, or a mismatch between the offer and the customer’s intent. Choosing one cause because it is convenient turns a signal into an unsupported story.

Looking beneath a symptom does not mean pretending that every problem has one hidden, permanent root cause. AHRQ’s systems-oriented discussion of root-cause analysis emphasizes underlying and contributing factors rather than stopping at an individual mistake. In a business context, the useful transfer is modest: ask what conditions and steps could plausibly produce the observed result, and be ready for more than one contributing factor. The goal here is not to run a formal safety investigation. It is to avoid designing a solution for the first explanation that comes to mind.

### Turn an explanation into a problem hypothesis

A problem hypothesis is a provisional claim that can be checked. One practical form is: “We believe [a defined group] experiences [a concrete obstacle] when [a situation occurs], which leads to [a meaningful consequence], because [a proposed condition or cause].” The bracketed cause is deliberately an assumption, not a conclusion. In the tutor example, a team might hypothesize that the trouble comes from families using several channels without a shared change record. Another plausible explanation is that scheduling policies are unclear. These are competing explanations, so the team should not present either as settled fact.

This framing keeps a problem statement separate from a solution pitch. “Tutors need our all-in-one scheduling platform” contains a solution and offers no evidence that the platform addresses the burden. “Tutors lose track of schedule changes across separate channels” is a claim about a situation; a later investigation can ask how frequent, costly, and widespread it is, and whether existing alternatives already handle it adequately. The U.S. Small Business Administration similarly directs market research toward questions about demand, market size, location, saturation, and what potential customers pay for alternatives. Those questions test the context around a proposed problem; they do not make the problem true merely by being asked.

### Use evidence at the strength it can bear

Evidence should be matched to the claim. A public data source can describe population, income, employment, or industry conditions, but it may be too broad to establish a niche customer’s workflow. The SBA notes that existing sources can efficiently answer general, quantitative questions but may not be specific to an audience, while direct research can yield more nuanced information about a particular business or customer. Thus, a local demographic count can support a claim about the size or characteristics of a place; it cannot prove that every person there feels a particular frustration.

Likewise, a few observations can produce a useful hypothesis but cannot establish prevalence or causation. A team should record what it saw, what it inferred, and what remains unknown. It can then state a narrow next question, such as whether the scheduling burden recurs across tutors who use different coordination systems. This lesson does not prescribe interview technique or a sampling plan; those belong to customer-interview and market-research work. The central habit is intellectual honesty: revise the problem definition when evidence points to another segment, context, severity, or contributing factor. A well-framed problem is not a promise of a business opportunity. It is a clearer question for subsequent investigation.

## Key Vocabulary

- **problem identification:** The process of describing a specific gap, obstacle, or unmet need in a defined customer context before choosing a solution.
- **customer context:** The circumstances in which a particular group is trying to complete a task or make a decision, including relevant constraints and alternatives.
- **symptom:** An observable result or warning sign that suggests a problem may exist but does not, by itself, establish its cause.
- **contributing factor:** A condition or action that may help produce an observed outcome without necessarily being the only cause.
- **problem hypothesis:** A provisional, checkable claim about who experiences an obstacle, when it occurs, and what consequence it has.
- **evidence limit:** A boundary on what particular information can reasonably support, such as a limit on specificity, representativeness, or causal inference.

## Eli-10

Suppose a school hallway is always crowded after lunch. “The hallway is crowded” is a useful clue, but it does not tell you what to change. Maybe one door is locked, two classes end at the same time, or students stop at a water fountain in the middle. Each possibility needs checking. In a business, “customers leave the website” works the same way: it is a clue, not the whole answer. It tells you where to look next, not which answer must be right.

## Eli's Analogy

Problem identification is like a doctor writing down the symptoms, when they happen, and who has them before choosing a treatment. The notes help the doctor test explanations instead of handing out medicine based on one guess.

The analogy has limits. Businesses are not medical clinics, and customer problems often involve preferences, trade-offs, and changing alternatives rather than a diagnosis with one correct treatment. The comparison is only about starting with careful observation and keeping explanations provisional.

## Worked Example

A student notices that several classmates complain about missing changes to group-project meetings. Her first statement is, “Students need a better group-work app.” That jumps to a solution and treats complaints as proof. She reframes the issue: “Students in large, multi-course project teams may miss schedule changes when updates are split among email, chat, and course platforms, causing wasted trips or delayed work.” The phrase “may” marks a hypothesis. Missed meetings are the symptom; split update channels are a possible contributing factor. The student could later learn that the main issue is instead unclear team roles, low notification use, or a school policy. Public enrollment data could show how many potential users are present, but it could not show why these particular students missed changes. The next work is to gather evidence that can distinguish among these explanations, not to declare an app validated.

## Common Mistakes

- **Naming a product as the problem.** Describe the customer’s situation and consequence before proposing a product, feature, or channel.
- **Treating one complaint or metric change as proof of a cause.** Use it as a signal, list plausible explanations, and label the preferred explanation as a hypothesis.
- **Writing for everyone.** Specify the group and situation so that the claim can be investigated and later revised.
- **Using broad statistics to prove a narrow experience.** Use broad data for broad claims and seek more specific evidence for claims about a particular context.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Symptom | Underlying or contributing factor | A symptom is an observable outcome; a contributing factor is a proposed condition that may help explain that outcome and requires support. |
| Problem hypothesis | Conclusion | A hypothesis is deliberately provisional and testable; a conclusion is warranted only after sufficient evidence for the stated claim. |
| Customer context | Target market size | Context describes the situation in which a burden occurs; market size estimates how many potential customers may be in scope. |

## Key Takeaway

A strong entrepreneurial problem statement specifies a customer context and a harmful gap without smuggling in a solution. Symptoms and early observations are evidence to investigate, not permission to assume a single cause or a validated opportunity.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement is the best example of a bounded problem hypothesis?**
   - A. Independent tutors may lose track of schedule changes when families use several unconnected channels, causing missed updates.
   - B. Tutors need a brilliant new scheduling app.
   - C. Scheduling is bad for everyone.
   - D. A calendar feature will certainly double tutor income.
   - **Answer: A.** A names a defined group, situation, obstacle, and consequence while remaining provisional. B and D start with a solution or unsupported promise; C is too broad to investigate.

2. **A retailer sees a rise in abandoned online checkouts. What is the most accurate interpretation of that observation?**
   - A. It proves that the checkout form is the cause.
   - B. It is a symptom that warrants investigation of several possible explanations.
   - C. It proves that no customer wants the retailer’s products.
   - D. It means the retailer should immediately build a new mobile app.
   - **Answer: B.** The rise is an observable result, not a demonstrated cause. It can prompt investigation, but it does not establish the form, demand, or a particular solution as the answer.

3. **Which claim is appropriately limited by the evidence described? A city report shows that a neighborhood has many residents aged 20–34, but provides no information about their commuting routines.**
   - A. Every resident aged 20–34 wants a new commuter service.
   - B. The neighborhood has already validated a commuter-service business.
   - C. The report can support a demographic description but not prove a commuting frustration.
   - D. The report proves that existing commuting options are inadequate.
   - **Answer: C.** The report directly supports a demographic description. It does not measure routines, dissatisfaction, demand, or whether alternatives are inadequate, so A, B, and D go beyond the evidence.

4. **Why should a team list more than one plausible contributing factor for a customer problem?**
   - A. Because every symptom has exactly four causes.
   - B. Because a longer list automatically validates the business idea.
   - C. Because listing causes eliminates the need for further evidence.
   - D. Because the same observable outcome can arise from different conditions, and the first explanation may be wrong.
   - **Answer: D.** D states the reason to preserve competing explanations. A invents a fixed rule, while B and C confuse brainstorming with validation.

5. **A team observes three project groups miss meeting changes and learns that each used a different combination of chat, email, and course tools. Which conclusion is justified now?**
   - A. Split communication channels are a plausible hypothesis to investigate further.
   - B. A new collaboration platform is proven to be the best solution.
   - C. All students experience the same scheduling burden.
   - D. The team has measured the size of its market.
   - **Answer: A.** The observations make split communication a plausible explanation, not a proven cause, universal pattern, market-size estimate, or validated solution. The other options claim more than the evidence shows.
