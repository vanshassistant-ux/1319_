# Pitching

## In 30 Seconds

A pitch is a short, decision-focused explanation of a proposed offering and the response the speaker hopes to receive. Its job is not to perform every piece of business analysis or to make an audience agree. A responsible pitch identifies its audience and purpose, explains a problem and proposed response in a clear sequence, distinguishes evidence from assumptions, and makes a proportionate next-step request. Feedback then becomes material for revision rather than a verdict on the person or idea.

## Why This Matters

Pitching is a useful communication discipline because it forces a team to decide what it knows, what it is still testing, and what it wants from a particular conversation. A customer may need a concrete benefit and a reason to try a prototype; an advisor may need the decision context and unresolved risks; a collaborator may need clarity about a role or experiment. Those are different purposes, even when the underlying idea is the same. Clear, evidence-conscious pitching also helps prevent a tempting mistake: using polished language to conceal uncertainty, omit material limitations, or turn an untested assumption into a promise. This is general communication education, not a solicitation of investment, a sales process, financial advice, or legal guidance.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a pitch as an audience- and decision-focused explanation with a specific next-step request.
- Distinguish a pitch's evidence, assumptions, and open questions.
- Organize a concise pitch around problem, audience, proposed response, support, limits, and next step.
- Apply ethical disclosure practices by avoiding unsupported or misleading claims.
- Use specific feedback to revise a pitch without treating one reaction as proof of market demand.

## The College Version

### A pitch is a designed conversation, not a compressed business plan

A pitch is a concise explanation intended to help a particular audience make or prepare for a particular decision. It may be spoken, written, visual, or a combination, but the format is secondary to the decision context. OpenStax notes that pitches vary with audience and goal, while recurring components include a problem-solution statement, value proposition, and key features. That observation is more useful than a universal slide count. A two-minute classroom presentation, a conversation with a prospective user, and a request for an advisor to review a research plan should not be copies of one another because the listeners are deciding different things.

Start by naming three items before drafting: the audience, the decision or response sought, and the boundary of the conversation. An audience can be a defined group of prospective users, a collaborator, a mentor, a program reviewer, or an internal team. The response might be permission to conduct a small voluntary test, feedback on whether the problem statement is clear, an introduction to a domain expert, or agreement to examine a prototype. The boundary says what the pitch is not asking for. For example, a student team seeking comments on a prototype is not asking listeners to predict the venture's success, commit money, or treat preliminary observations as market proof.

This framing separates a pitch from adjacent entrepreneurship work. Market research develops and interprets evidence about a market. A value proposition states a proposed benefit for a defined audience. A business model describes how an organization creates, delivers, and captures value. Financial projections examine quantified assumptions under stated conditions. A pitch may draw selectively on each, but it cannot replace them. A short presentation that merely repeats a business-model label, a market-size figure, or a revenue claim is not automatically informative. The pitch has to show why that information matters to this listener and what its limits are.

The audience is not a target to be manipulated. It is a group of people entitled to enough context to make the requested next decision. That principle changes the tone. Rather than trying to make an idea sound inevitable, a careful speaker makes the proposal inspectable: Who experiences the stated problem? What is being proposed? What observation or source supports the statement? What is still unknown? What can the listener reasonably do next? Concision comes from choosing decision-relevant material, not from hiding qualifications.

### Build a narrative that lets the listener inspect the reasoning

A practical general-purpose structure has six moves. First, define the audience and situation: identify the people, setting, and task under discussion. Second, state the problem as an observable condition or unmet need rather than as a dramatic slogan. Third, describe the proposed response and its value proposition: what it would help the defined audience do, avoid, or obtain. Fourth, present the most relevant support. This can include a verified observation, a research finding with its method and scope, a prototype demonstration, or a plainly labeled hypothetical. Fifth, name material assumptions, limits, and next questions. Sixth, make one proportionate next-step request.

The order can change when the audience's decision requires a different entry point. A prospective user may first need to recognize a situation they experience, then see a concrete prototype. A mentor may first need the team's uncertainty and evidence plan. An internal collaborator may need the operating constraint and the work requested. What should not change is the chain of reasoning. The listener should be able to connect the problem to the proposed response, the response to the evidence, and the evidence to the request. If a connection is missing, adding animation, more adjectives, or a larger number will not repair it.

Evidence and assumptions deserve visibly different labels. Evidence is information actually observed or obtained from a source, described with enough context to assess its relevance. For example: “In six voluntary interviews with evening students, four described difficulty obtaining food after their shift; these conversations were exploratory and do not estimate prevalence.” An assumption is a statement used to plan or test but not yet established: “We assume that a pickup window near transit could reduce that inconvenience.” A proposal is an intended action: “We would like permission to test one pickup window with a small volunteer group.” These are all useful statements, but they have different evidentiary status.

Avoid pseudo-precision. A speaker who says “our market is huge” has supplied neither a defined population nor evidence. A speaker who says “our tool will save everyone two hours” has converted an outcome hypothesis into a guarantee. In contrast, a bounded statement identifies what is known: “Our desk research describes a local concentration of evening workers; we have not yet measured how many would use this service.” This can feel less dramatic, but it makes the pitch more useful for a decision. The relevant audience can ask a better question, challenge an assumption, or suggest a feasible test.

### Ethical disclosure means accuracy in context, not a disclaimer ritual

Ethical pitching asks whether the overall impression a reasonable listener could take is supported by what is shown. U.S. Federal Trade Commission guidance for advertising states that claims should be truthful, non-deceptive, and supported by evidence; it also warns that context, implied messages, and material omissions matter. A classroom pitch is not automatically an advertisement, and requirements can vary by setting and jurisdiction. Still, the communication lesson is broadly useful: do not use a fine-print qualification to contradict a bold claim, and do not imply certainty that the evidence cannot support.

This matters especially for performance, price, safety, environmental, health, or financial claims. Unless verified evidence directly supports a statement in the stated conditions, phrase it as a question, an assumption, or a planned test. “We will learn whether the prototype reduces the time needed for this task” is not the same as “the prototype cuts time by half.” “A respondent reported this difficulty” is not the same as “most customers have this difficulty.” “This is a hypothetical scenario” is not the same as “these are expected results.” The difference is not timid wording; it is a difference in what the speaker is entitled to claim.

Disclose limitations where they affect the next decision. A limitation may concern a small or self-selected sample, a preliminary prototype, an incomplete cost estimate, a comparison that uses a different setting, or a possible conflict of interest. The goal is relevance. A short pitch does not need every footnote from a research packet, but it should not omit the condition that would reverse the listener's interpretation. If a chart comes from a source, identify it. If a figure is an estimate, label its basis and date. If a demonstration uses simulated data, say so before the audience infers that the result is operational evidence.

Ethical disclosure also protects the relationship needed for learning. A listener who discovers that a limitation was buried may reasonably discount later communication. Conversely, a clear statement of uncertainty gives feedback a useful object: the audience can respond to the actual assumption rather than to an inflated version of it. This lesson does not teach securities law, term sheets, contracts, or regulated offering rules. For a real legal or regulatory decision, the applicable official authority and qualified local advice matter; here, the standard is honest, source-aware educational communication.

### Feedback is data for revision, not a score for the idea

A first pitch is a testable communication artifact. OpenStax's discussion of pitch feedback emphasizes seeking both quantitative and qualitative information and considering why people respond as they do. In practice, feedback is more useful when the speaker asks for a specific kind of response. “What did you think?” often produces encouragement or vague criticism. “After hearing this, what problem do you think we are trying to address?” tests clarity. “Which claim sounded unsupported?” tests evidence. “What would you need to know before joining a small pilot?” tests whether the request is proportionate.

Record feedback in a way that preserves distinctions. Note who gave it and in what role; capture the question or observation; identify whether it concerns clarity, evidence, feasibility, ethics, audience fit, or presentation; and decide what follow-up would test the point. A user who cannot restate the problem may signal a communication issue. A domain expert who asks for a missing safety constraint may signal a research or design issue. A friend who likes the idea may provide encouragement, but that reaction alone does not establish demand or validate a business model. Treating all reactions as equally diagnostic creates noise.

Revision should change the smallest element that the feedback actually challenges. If listeners confuse the user group, revise the audience definition before redesigning the entire deck. If they mistake an assumption for a finding, add a label and source context. If they ask why the proposed response addresses the problem, strengthen the causal explanation or return to customer discovery. If a question reveals a material uncertainty, do not invent an answer for the next presentation; place it in the research plan. This revision loop links pitching to disciplined learning rather than performance theater.

A pitch can become clearer and still receive a negative answer to its request. That response may reflect timing, priorities, capacity, evidence, or disagreement about the problem. It is information, not proof that the speaker has failed or that the idea is worthless. The educational objective is to improve the accuracy and usefulness of the next conversation: clarify the decision, preserve the evidence boundary, state the uncertainty, and ask for a next step the audience can reasonably evaluate.

## Key Vocabulary

- **pitch:** A concise, audience-specific explanation of a proposal that seeks a defined response or next step.
- **audience:** The particular people or group whose decision, understanding, or action a communication is designed to inform.
- **decision context:** The situation that identifies who must decide what, by when, using which relevant information and constraints.
- **value proposition:** A clear statement of the benefit a proposed offering intends to provide for a defined audience in relation to a problem or need.
- **evidence:** Information observed or drawn from a source that is described with enough method, scope, and context to assess a claim.
- **assumption:** A proposition used in planning or testing that has not yet been established as a fact for the stated situation.
- **material limitation:** A condition or uncertainty whose absence could cause an audience to interpret a claim or decision differently.
- **next-step request:** A specific, proportionate action the speaker asks the audience to consider after the pitch.
- **feedback:** Information about how a communication, prototype, or proposal was understood or evaluated that can guide a revision or a new question.

## Eli-10

A pitch is like explaining a class project to someone who can help with one small next step. You do not need to tell them every fact you have ever found. You do need to tell them who the project is for, what problem you noticed, what you are trying, what you have actually learned, and what you still do not know. Then you ask for something clear, such as feedback on a mockup or permission to try a small test.

The helpful habit is to put labels on your statements. “We saw this happen in six conversations” is evidence with a limit. “We think this change may help” is an assumption. “We want to test it with volunteers” is a plan. Those labels keep a listener from mistaking a hope for a fact. If someone gives critical feedback, it is not a grade on you. It is a clue about what was confusing, unproven, or worth checking next.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a pitch as giving a guide a map before asking which trail to try. The guide needs to know where you are, where you hope to go, which paths you have already checked, and what obstacle you are unsure about. A clear map also makes it easier for the guide to point out a missing bridge instead of guessing what you meant.

The map analogy has limits. A map usually shows stable places, but a pitch concerns people, choices, and uncertain outcomes that can change. The analogy also cannot show the ethical duty to avoid misleading claims or the need for evidence behind statements about a real offering.

## Worked Example

Harbor Study is a hypothetical student team exploring a late-evening campus pickup window for basic groceries. It will speak to a campus operations advisor, not sell anything or ask for funding. The team opens with the decision context: “We are deciding whether a small voluntary usability test is appropriate.” It states an evidence boundary: “In six exploratory conversations with evening students, four described difficulty getting groceries after their shifts; this does not estimate how common the problem is.” It then labels its assumption: “We think a pickup window near the transit stop may reduce that difficulty for some students.” The proposed next step is narrow: “May we show a paper prototype to eight volunteers and ask whether the pickup instructions are understandable?” The team also discloses that it has not tested price, demand, or operational capacity. The advisor says the problem is clear but asks how volunteers would be recruited. Rather than claiming that recruitment will be easy, the team records the question, revises the next pitch to explain voluntary recruitment and data minimization, and treats that revision as a communication and research improvement—not as evidence that the service will succeed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using the same pitch unchanged for every listener.** Define the audience's decision and tailor the evidence, detail, and next-step request to that context while keeping the underlying facts consistent.
- **Presenting an assumption, estimate, or prototype result as an established fact.** Label its status, state the evidence boundary, and explain what test or source could strengthen or challenge it.
- **Hiding a material limitation in a footnote or vague disclaimer.** Place the relevant qualification near the claim and revise the main claim if the limitation changes its meaning.
- **Treating compliments or one negative reaction as proof of demand or failure.** Record who responded, what they understood, what evidence they used, and what specific revision or research question follows.
- **Making the request larger than the evidence supports.** Ask for a proportionate next step, such as feedback, an introduction, or permission for a bounded test, rather than a commitment based on preliminary information.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Evidence vs. assumption:** Evidence is observed or sourced information described with context; an assumption is a proposition that still needs testing or verification.
- **Pitch vs. business plan:** A pitch selects decision-relevant information for a particular conversation; a business plan is a broader planning document whose format and detail can serve different needs.
- **Feedback vs. market validation:** Feedback describes a response to a communication or prototype; it does not by itself demonstrate demand, feasibility, or a successful business model.

## Key Takeaway

A responsible pitch makes the next decision easier to evaluate: it fits a defined audience, connects a problem to a proposed response, distinguishes facts from assumptions, discloses material limits, and requests a proportionate next step. Its revision loop turns feedback into clearer questions rather than exaggerated certainty.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which description best defines a responsible entrepreneurship pitch?**
   - A. A concise, audience-specific explanation that supports a defined next decision or request.
   - B. A guarantee that a proposed venture will succeed if the presenter is confident.
   - C. A complete substitute for market research, a business model, and financial analysis.
   - D. A script that must use the same information and request for every listener.
   - **Answer:** A. A pitch is a concise communication designed for a particular audience and next step.

2. **A team has interviewed six volunteers, four of whom mentioned a commute-related problem. Which wording most accurately uses that evidence in a pitch?**
   - A. Most people in the city have this problem, so demand is proven.
   - B. In six exploratory conversations, four volunteers mentioned the problem; this suggests a question to investigate but does not estimate how common it is.
   - C. The interviews prove that every volunteer will use the proposed service.
   - D. Because the sample is small, the conversations are useless and should not be mentioned.
   - **Answer:** B. The wording reports both the observation and its boundary.

3. **Which element most directly helps an audience inspect the reasoning in a short pitch?**
   - A. A larger set of adjectives describing the idea as revolutionary.
   - B. A claim that uncertainty should be ignored so the speaker sounds decisive.
   - C. A clear link among the defined problem, proposed response, supporting evidence, material limits, and next-step request.
   - D. A disclaimer placed far from the claim it is meant to qualify.
   - **Answer:** C. This sequence lets the listener assess how the requested action follows from the stated information.

4. **A prototype has only been demonstrated with simulated data. Which practice is most appropriate when presenting it to prospective users?**
   - A. Describe the demonstration as proof of real-world performance because the interface worked during the presentation.
   - B. Omit the simulation detail because it might make the pitch less exciting.
   - C. State that all users will obtain the same outcome once the prototype launches.
   - D. Label the data as simulated, explain what the demonstration can show, name what remains untested, and ask for feedback appropriate to that early stage.
   - **Answer:** D. The audience needs the limitation in order to interpret the demonstration accurately.

5. **After a mentor says, “I cannot tell who this is for,” what is the most useful first revision?**
   - A. Add a larger market-size figure without changing the opening.
   - B. Revise the audience definition and problem situation, then ask a follow-up question to check whether the mentor can identify the intended group.
   - C. Treat the comment as proof that no one needs the offering.
   - D. Delete every limitation so the pitch becomes more confident.
   - **Answer:** B. The revision addresses the particular clarity failure and tests whether it worked.
