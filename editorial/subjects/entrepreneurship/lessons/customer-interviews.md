# Customer Interviews

## In 30 Seconds

Customer interviews are planned conversations with people who have relevant experience of a task, problem, or decision. They help a team learn how people describe what they actually do, what gets in the way, and what context matters. The method is exploratory: a small, deliberately chosen set of interviews can uncover patterns and sharpen questions, but it cannot tell you how common a view is across a whole market.

## Why This Matters

Interviews can keep an entrepreneurial team close to real experiences instead of its own assumptions. Done well, they produce better questions, expose differences between participants, and reveal where a workflow or language is misunderstood. Done carelessly, they can pressure people, collect unnecessary personal data, or turn a polite reaction into false proof of demand. The transferable skill is disciplined listening: obtain voluntary participation, ask about concrete past behavior, preserve the difference between an observation and an interpretation, and report the limits of what a small qualitative sample can support.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a customer interview and explain its exploratory evidence limit.
- Plan an interview round around a specific learning question and relevant participant criteria.
- Distinguish open, neutral questions from leading, hypothetical, or solution-pitch questions.
- Apply listening and follow-up techniques that seek concrete examples without putting words in a participant's mouth.
- Explain how consent, privacy, and careful note analysis protect participants and improve the quality of findings.

## The College Version

### What an interview can—and cannot—tell you

A customer interview is a structured conversation used to learn from someone with relevant experience. For an entrepreneurial team, its value is usually depth and context: what the person was trying to do, what happened, what alternatives they considered, what language they use, and which conditions changed the experience. The interview is not a sales call disguised as research, and it is not a referendum on whether a team likes its own idea. A participant may be a current user of an existing process, a likely user of a proposed service, or another person who directly experiences the workflow the team needs to understand. The selection must follow the learning question. If the question concerns how independent tutors manage schedule changes, interviewing people who merely know a tutor is weaker evidence than interviewing tutors who coordinate recurring sessions.

A useful interview round begins with one or two decisions the team needs to inform and a short discussion guide. The guide gives every participant a comparable core set of topics, while leaving room to pursue a relevant detail. GOV.UK's guidance on in-depth interviews recommends open, neutral questions and attention to stories and real examples. That is why a prompt such as “Tell me about the last time a schedule changed” is often more informative than “Would you use our schedule-change app?” The first asks for a remembered event; the second introduces a proposed answer and invites courtesy, imagination, or a quick yes.

Interview evidence is qualitative and exploratory. A small set of deliberately recruited participants can surface recurring patterns, contrast situations, and generate a better next question. It does not calculate a population percentage, establish a market size, or prove that a particular product will sell. GOV.UK contrasts small rounds commonly used for interviews with survey-style methods that need much larger samples for clear quantitative findings. The precise number appropriate for an interview round depends on the question, heterogeneity of the people involved, access constraints, and what has already been learned. Rather than borrow a magic number, state who was interviewed, why they were relevant, what differences were included or missed, and what the sample cannot represent. Market sizing and statistically representative research require their own design.

### Plan recruitment and questions without steering the answer

Recruitment is part of the method, not an administrative afterthought. Write inclusion criteria that connect to the behavior or situation under study: for example, tutors who independently schedule at least several recurring sessions, or families who recently changed a session. Deliberately seek meaningful variation when it could change the experience—such as a new versus experienced tutor, a different scheduling arrangement, a disability or access need, or a different tool setup. This is not a claim of statistical representativeness. It is a way to avoid letting the most convenient or enthusiastic contacts stand in for everyone. Record the recruiting route and likely sources of bias. Friends, early supporters, and people recruited from a founder's network may be useful to learn from, but their relationship to the project can shape what they say.

Use a guide with an opening, a small number of topic areas, and a closing. Begin by explaining the purpose in plain language and confirming consent; then ask broad questions before narrower ones. Open questions commonly start with “how,” “what,” “tell me about,” or “walk me through.” Neutral wording does not contain the preferred conclusion. Compare “What happened when you needed to reschedule?” with “How frustrating was it when the confusing system made you reschedule?” The second question assumes both that the system was confusing and that frustration occurred. It pressures the participant to agree with the interviewer’s framing. Avoid stacking two questions together as well: “How do you schedule and pay and communicate?” gives no clear answer target.

Questions about an actual episode are usually more useful than questions about a hypothetical future purchase. “What did you do next?” “What else did you try?” and “What made that option workable or not?” can expose constraints, workarounds, and alternatives. A participant's claim that they would buy something is still a statement about a possible future, not evidence that they will do so. The point is not to catch people being wrong. It is to understand their present practice before treating a solution as the center of the conversation. This lesson therefore does not build a customer-discovery framework or teach a final value proposition; it teaches the interview craft that can supply bounded evidence to those later activities.

### Listen, record, and analyze with care

Good moderation makes the participant, not the interviewer, do most of the explaining. Listen for the sequence of a real event: trigger, goal, actions, tools, obstacles, choices, and outcome. Pause rather than filling silence with an answer. A brief reflection can check understanding—“It sounds as if the text message was easy to miss because you were teaching”—but frame it as a check, not a correction. Then ask a simple, non-leading follow-up: “Can you tell me more about what happened after that?” or “What made that difficult?” Follow the participant's relevant detail while returning gently to the guide when needed. Do not debate, promise a solution, or treat a participant’s politeness as approval.

Before a session, make voluntary participation and consent practical, not ceremonial. Explain who is conducting the interview, its purpose, what will happen, whether anyone will observe, whether you will take notes or record, what information will be collected, who may receive it, how long it will be kept, and how a participant can decline or stop. Seek separate, clear permission for a recording instead of assuming that joining the call authorizes it. Government guidance is written for its own setting and legal regime, so it should not be read as universal legal advice. Still, these disclosures express a sound ethical baseline. Applicable privacy, consumer-protection, employment, accessibility, and research rules vary by location, industry, participant, and data type; obtain appropriate local advice when the stakes or data warrant it.

Keep the data footprint small. Do not collect a full name, address, sensitive history, or recording merely because it might be convenient later. Separate contact details from research notes when possible, restrict access, use secure storage appropriate to the setting, and remove identifiers from findings when identity is not necessary. Notes and recordings can contain personal information even when the discussion seems routine. A person must understand the intended use before agreeing to it.

Analysis begins soon after the round. Preserve an observation separately from the interpretation it may suggest. “Participant 04 checked three messaging apps before finding the update” is an observation. “Tutors need a new messaging platform” is an interpretation and a proposed solution; it does not follow automatically. Extract relevant observations from notes or an authorized recording, label the participant context, and sort similar observations into provisional themes. Then write a bounded finding: for example, “Several interviewed tutors described missing schedule changes when updates were spread across more than one channel.” Report contrary cases too. Finally, identify what the evidence supports, what it does not establish, and the next question. That discipline turns interview notes into an auditable learning record rather than a collection of memorable anecdotes.

## Key Vocabulary

- **customer interview:** A planned conversation with a person who has relevant experience, used to learn about their context, actions, language, and constraints.
- **discussion guide:** A short, flexible plan of topics and prompts that keeps interviews comparable without requiring a rigid script.
- **open question:** A question that invites a participant to describe an experience in their own words rather than select a fixed or yes-or-no answer.
- **leading question:** A question whose wording suggests a preferred answer or assumes an unverified conclusion.
- **purposive recruitment:** Selecting participants because their experience or characteristics are relevant to a defined learning question, not because they form a random sample.
- **informed consent:** A participant's voluntary agreement after receiving understandable information about the activity, data handling, recording, and intended use.
- **observation:** A record of what a participant said or did, kept distinct from the researcher's explanation of its meaning.
- **theme:** A provisional pattern or shared topic formed by organizing related observations across interview notes.

## Eli-10

A customer interview is like asking someone to show you how they pack for a trip. Instead of saying, “Would you like my amazing suitcase?” you ask, “Tell me about the last trip you packed for. What did you bring? What was hard?” Their story can show what they actually do and what gets in the way. You listen, ask a few gentle follow-up questions, and write down what happened.

You must also be fair. Tell the person why you are asking, whether you are recording, and what will happen to their information. They can say no or stop. Do not collect private details you do not need. After several conversations, look for patterns, but do not pretend that a few people speak for every traveler in the world.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of the interviews as several flashlight beams in a dark room. Each beam helps you see one part of the room and may reveal a pattern, but a few beams do not measure the whole building. You still need to say where you looked and where you did not.

The analogy has limits. People are not objects to inspect, and an interview is a conversation that needs consent and respect. Also, a pattern in notes is not automatically a business decision or proof that a proposed solution is wanted.

## Worked Example

Mina is considering a service that helps independent tutors track schedule changes. Her interview goal is not “prove tutors want my service.” It is “understand what tutors do when a recurring session changes and what makes the process difficult.” She recruits five tutors who currently schedule recurring sessions, noting that three use text messages, two use calendar links, and one works with an assistant. Before each call, Mina explains that participation is voluntary, she will take notes but not record unless the person separately agrees, and she will remove names from her summary. She asks, “Walk me through the last time a family changed a session.” When one tutor says, “I usually lose the update,” Mina asks, “What happened after you noticed?” rather than “Would reminders solve that?” Her notes show that three tutors described updates spread across channels; two did not experience that issue because they use a shared calendar. Mina reports a pattern among these interviewees, the counterexamples, and a next question about the conditions that make a shared calendar workable. She does not claim that five interviews establish how common the burden is or that her service is validated.

## Common Mistakes

- **Recruiting only friends, fans, or the easiest contacts and treating them as the market.** Set criteria tied to the learning question, seek relevant variation, and disclose the sample's likely bias and limits.
- **Asking participants to approve a solution or predict a future purchase.** Ask for a concrete past episode, actions taken, alternatives used, and conditions that shaped the outcome.
- **Turning silence or a vague answer into an opportunity to explain the preferred answer.** Pause, ask a neutral follow-up, or move on; the interviewer's job is to learn, not persuade.
- **Recording or retaining personal information without clear disclosure and permission.** Explain data and recording plans, collect only what is needed, protect access, and follow applicable local requirements.
- **Writing a solution claim as though it were an observation.** Keep notes on what was said or done separate from interpretations, themes, and proposed next steps.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer interview | Statistically representative survey | An interview seeks contextual depth and exploratory patterns; a representative survey is designed to estimate responses in a defined population. A small interview sample cannot substitute for the latter. |
| Open, neutral question | Leading question | An open, neutral question invites the participant's account; a leading question embeds the interviewer's preferred answer or an unsupported assumption. |
| Observation | Interpretation | An observation records what happened or was said; an interpretation proposes what it may mean and must remain open to revision. |

## Key Takeaway

Customer interviews are an ethical, exploratory way to learn from relevant people's real experiences. Plan around a focused question, listen without steering, protect participants and their data, and report themes as bounded evidence—not proof of a market or solution.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best describes the evidence limit of a small, purposively recruited customer-interview round?**
   - A. It can reveal contextual patterns and sharpen questions, but it does not estimate how common a view is across a market.
   - B. It proves the percentage of all potential customers who will buy a product.
   - C. It eliminates the need to state who was interviewed.
   - D. It makes statistical sampling unnecessary for any later question.
   - **Answer: A.** Small interview rounds can produce useful qualitative insights, but they do not provide a population estimate. The other options incorrectly turn exploratory evidence into representative measurement or remove the need for transparent sampling limits.

2. **A founder wants to learn about last-minute schedule changes. Which opening question is most neutral and likely to elicit a concrete experience?**
   - A. Don't you think schedule-change apps are frustrating to use?
   - B. Tell me about the last time a schedule changed. What happened?
   - C. Would you pay for an app that fixes schedule chaos?
   - D. How frustrating and expensive was the confusing process?
   - **Answer: B.** B asks for a real episode without assuming a problem, cause, or solution. A and D lead the participant; C asks for a hypothetical purchase response rather than an account of current behavior.

3. **During a session, a participant says, “I missed the update because I was teaching.” What is the best immediate follow-up?**
   - A. So automatic reminders would solve it, right?
   - B. Why did you ignore the update?
   - C. Can you tell me more about what happened after you were teaching?
   - D. You should switch to our calendar system.
   - **Answer: C.** C invites detail about the participant's experience without supplying an explanation or a solution. A and D steer toward a product; B is accusatory and assumes fault.

4. **Which practice best respects informed participation and privacy when an entrepreneur plans to record interviews?**
   - A. Start recording before the participant joins so the introduction is captured.
   - B. Record everyone by default because an invitation implies consent.
   - C. Collect every available personal detail in case it becomes useful later.
   - D. Explain the purpose, recording, data use, sharing, and retention in understandable terms; obtain voluntary permission and collect only what is needed.
   - **Answer: D.** D combines clear, voluntary consent with data minimization. The other practices assume permission, conceal recording, or collect unnecessary personal information.

5. **After five interviews, three tutors describe checking multiple channels before finding schedule updates, while two rely on a shared calendar and do not report that issue. Which finding is best supported?**
   - A. Several interviewed tutors described a multi-channel update burden, while the pattern did not appear in every interview; investigate the conditions behind the difference.
   - B. All tutors need a new messaging platform.
   - C. A majority of the entire tutor market has the same problem.
   - D. The team has proven its proposed service will be purchased.
   - **Answer: A.** A reports the observed pattern, includes the counterexamples, and sets a bounded next question. B, C, and D overgeneralize from a small qualitative sample and leap to a solution or market-wide conclusion.
