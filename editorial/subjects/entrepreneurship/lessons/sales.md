# Sales

## In 30 Seconds

Sales is a structured conversation and decision process through which a seller and a potential customer determine whether an offering fits a real need and whether an exchange makes sense. A responsible process identifies a plausible fit, learns before recommending, presents only supportable benefits, makes the next step clear, and respects a person’s choice to decline or stop receiving follow-up. Sales is not a substitute for research, pricing, or marketing.

## Why This Matters

Sales work turns broad assumptions about a customer into a specific, testable conversation. It can reveal whether a problem is important enough to act on, whether an offering fits the situation, and where the seller’s evidence is thin. The same process also carries ethical obligations: a recommendation should not outrun the facts, pressure should not replace consent, and follow-up should honor a recipient’s preferences. Basic stage metrics help a team learn from its process without treating people as numbers or promising a result.

## Learning Objectives

- Define sales as a customer-value exchange process rather than a pressure tactic.
- Explain the role and limits of qualification in deciding whether to continue a conversation.
- Use discovery questions and summaries to understand a potential customer’s situation before recommending an offering.
- Match a presentation claim to a documented feature, condition, or evidence boundary.
- Interpret simple, clearly defined sales-stage metrics without overstating what they show.

## The College Version

### Sales is an exchange process, not a script for overcoming people

Sales is the work of helping a potential customer decide whether a particular offering fits a particular situation. Personal-selling texts often describe a sequence that includes finding and qualifying prospects, preparing, approaching, presenting, addressing questions, seeking a decision, and following up. The sequence is useful as a map, not a mandate to force every conversation through the same steps. A customer may need only a factual answer; another may need time, documentation, or a different solution. The responsible aim is a well-informed decision about a possible exchange, including the possibility that the offering is not a fit.

A durable exchange requires a customer to understand material features, limits, commitments, and next steps well enough to decide voluntarily. If the offering cannot solve the stated problem, if the customer is not an appropriate user, or if the seller cannot support a claim, ending or redirecting the conversation can be the most accurate outcome. Marketing, value-proposition design, pricing, and pitch creation are related topics, but they do different work. Marketing reaches and communicates with audiences; value-proposition work clarifies the intended benefit; pricing sets terms; pitching frames a concise case. This lesson concerns the one-to-one or small-group sales process after a potential conversation exists.

A sales process should be documented in plain terms: what stage a person is in, what information has been learned, what evidence was shared, what next step was offered, and what permission exists for follow-up. Documentation is not a license to collect unnecessary personal data. Keep records relevant to the conversation and handle them responsibly under applicable organizational and legal requirements.

### Qualification and discovery reduce mismatch

Qualification asks whether it is worth continuing a conversation and whether the offering could plausibly help. In a basic business setting, relevant questions may concern a real problem, the person’s role in a decision, timing, operational constraints, and whether the seller can serve the situation. Qualification is not a checklist for excluding people because of stereotypes, nor a way to manufacture urgency. It is a mutual-fit screen. A seller can say, in effect, “Here is what we can and cannot help with; does it make sense to explore this further?”

Discovery comes before a detailed recommendation. Use open, non-leading questions to understand the person’s current workflow, goals, constraints, alternatives, and criteria for a satisfactory outcome. Listen for evidence rather than filling gaps with assumptions. A useful habit is to summarize: “You said the delays occur when three locations submit requests at once, and an audit trail matters. Did I get that right?” A summary gives the other person a chance to correct the record. It also prevents a seller from treating one symptom as the whole problem. Customer-discovery research owns the broader work of testing segment-and-problem assumptions; sales discovery uses a current conversation to assess the fit of a specific exchange.

An ethical discovery conversation does not hide its purpose, misrepresent the offering as neutral research, or seek sensitive information that is not needed. The person can decline a question, pause, or decide not to continue. When the best answer is “we do not offer that,” say so plainly. That response may protect trust and produce better information than a weakly matched sale.

### Present evidence, then make a voluntary next step possible

A presentation should connect a documented feature or service capability to a need the customer actually described. It should also name relevant conditions and limits. For example, a team might say that its scheduling tool records request times and assigns tasks by configured rules. It should not turn that feature into a claim that every customer will save a particular number of hours unless it has appropriate support and the conditions are clear. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission states that advertising claims must be truthful, nondeceptive, fair, and evidence-based; those federal standards are a useful general boundary, not individualized legal advice and not a complete description of rules in every jurisdiction.

Questions and objections can reveal a missing fact, an unresolved constraint, or a poor fit. Treat them as information to address accurately, not hurdles to defeat. If an answer requires technical, legal, security, or contractual expertise, involve an appropriate qualified person rather than improvising. A clear request for a decision may be simple: offer a trial, a review of documentation, a later meeting, or no further action. The customer should be able to understand what agreeing means and should be free to say no.

Follow-up is part of service as well as sales. Confirm an agreed next action, give useful information requested, and ask about a reasonable contact preference. Do not keep contacting someone after they have asked you to stop. In the United States, commercial email has specific CAN-SPAM requirements, including accurate routing information, nondeceptive subject lines, a way to opt out of future marketing email, and prompt honoring of opt-out requests. Other channels and jurisdictions can have different rules, so this is a general educational reminder to obtain appropriate guidance for a real program.

### Use metrics as process signals, with a named denominator

Sales metrics can help a team notice where its process is losing fit or clarity, but a metric does not explain its own cause. First define the stages. A team might label an inquiry as qualified only after confirming a relevant use case and a willing decision contact. It might label an opportunity as advanced only after the customer agrees to review a proposal. With those definitions fixed, an advancement rate can be calculated as advanced opportunities divided by qualified opportunities for a stated period. A close rate can be calculated as completed purchases divided by qualified opportunities, again using a stated period and inclusion rule.

Suppose 20 conversations meet a team’s written qualification definition in April, eight move to an agreed proposal review, and three result in completed purchases by the chosen reporting cutoff. The proposal-advancement rate is 8 divided by 20, or 40%; the close rate for that cohort is 3 divided by 20, or 15%. These figures describe the process under those definitions. They do not prove that a salesperson caused the outcome, predict next month’s results, or show whether customers were well served. A very high rate could even reflect a narrow screen rather than a strong process.

Metrics become more useful when paired with qualitative review. Did people decline because the need was absent, the feature did not fit, the evidence was unclear, the timing changed, or the next step was inconvenient? Do not use a metric as a reason to conceal limits or pressure people. Sales learning should improve the accuracy of qualification, discovery, presentation, and follow-up while preserving voluntary, informed choice.

## Key Vocabulary

- **sales process:** A documented sequence for assessing fit, understanding needs, presenting a relevant offering, agreeing on a next step, and following up responsibly.
- **qualification:** An early assessment of whether a potential customer’s situation and an offering plausibly fit well enough to continue a conversation.
- **discovery:** A conversation used to understand a potential customer’s goals, current situation, constraints, and decision criteria before making a recommendation.
- **evidence-backed claim:** A statement about an offering that is supported by appropriate evidence and stated with its relevant conditions and limits.
- **next step:** A specific, voluntary action that both parties understand, such as reviewing documentation, scheduling a meeting, starting a trial, or ending contact.
- **opt-out:** A recipient’s request to stop receiving future marketing messages through a channel covered by the applicable program or law.
- **advancement rate:** The share of items in a defined sales stage that move to a later, explicitly defined stage during a stated measurement period.
- **close rate:** The share of a clearly defined set of sales opportunities that result in completed purchases during a stated period or cohort window.

## Eli-10

Good sales is like helping someone choose a tool, not trying to make them buy the first thing on the shelf. First, you learn what job they are trying to do and what makes it difficult. Then you explain only how a tool actually works, including where it will not help. If it is the wrong tool, saying so is more useful than making a promise you cannot keep.

Afterward, you agree on what happens next. Maybe the person wants a manual, a short trial, another conversation, or no more messages. Their choice matters. Keeping track of how many conversations move to a next step can help a team learn, but the numbers do not tell the whole story. The team still has to listen and be honest.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a librarian helping someone find a book. The librarian asks what the reader needs, checks the catalog, explains what a book covers, and points out if another book is a better match. The reader decides whether to borrow it. A helpful librarian does not hide the title, invent chapters, or follow the reader around after they leave.

The comparison is limited because a book loan is usually free and simple, while a sale may involve money, commitments, contracts, technical limits, and legal rules. A salesperson also represents an organization’s offering, so evidence and accurate communication matter more than the analogy can show.

## Worked Example

A small operations-software team receives an inquiry from a warehouse manager. Before demonstrating anything, the representative explains that the call is about deciding whether the team’s tool might fit and asks permission to take notes about the workflow. The manager describes missed handoffs during shift changes and says an audit trail is required. The representative summarizes the problem, confirms that the manager can invite the IT reviewer, and explains one documented feature: the tool records assigned tasks and timestamps. She does not claim that it will eliminate all missed handoffs. They agree that the manager will review a security document with IT; no purchase is requested on the call. In April, the team has 20 conversations meeting its written qualification rule, eight agreed proposal reviews, and three completed purchases. It reports 8/20 = 40% proposal advancement and 3/20 = 15% close rate for that defined cohort, then reviews why the other conversations did not advance rather than assuming pressure would improve the numbers.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating qualification as a way to label people as worthy or unworthy.** Assess the fit between a stated situation and the offering using relevant, nondiscriminatory information; a poor fit is a valid reason to stop.
- **Presenting a feature as a guaranteed outcome.** State what the offering does, what evidence supports the claim, and the conditions or limits that matter.
- **Answering objections by talking over the customer.** Clarify the concern, answer accurately when possible, and acknowledge uncertainty or a limitation when it exists.
- **Continuing follow-up after a person has asked to stop.** Record the preference, honor it promptly, and apply the rules that govern the relevant channel and jurisdiction.
- **Comparing sales metrics with different stage definitions or time windows.** Name the numerator, denominator, stage rule, and reporting period before interpreting a rate.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Qualification vs. discovery:** Qualification asks whether continuing is plausibly useful; discovery explores the customer’s situation and decision criteria before a recommendation.
- **Feature vs. outcome claim:** A feature describes what an offering does; an outcome claim describes an effect for a customer and needs appropriate evidence and conditions.
- **Advancement rate vs. close rate:** Advancement rate tracks movement to a defined later stage, while close rate tracks completed purchases from a defined opportunity set.

## Key Takeaway

Responsible sales helps a customer make an informed, voluntary decision about a possible exchange. Qualify for fit, discover before recommending, support claims with evidence, respect follow-up preferences, and interpret metrics only with clear definitions and limits.

## Practice Question Bank

1. What is the best description of qualification in a responsible sales process?
   - A. Assessing whether the customer’s situation and the offering plausibly fit well enough to continue a conversation.
   - B. Pressuring every inquiry to schedule a demonstration.
   - C. Predicting exactly which customer will buy.
   - D. Setting the final price for an offering.
   - **Answer: A.** Qualification is a mutual-fit assessment.

2. During discovery, which response best checks a seller’s understanding before a recommendation?
   - A. Move directly to a product demonstration so the conversation stays short.
   - B. Summarize the stated goal and constraint, then ask the customer to correct or confirm the summary.
   - C. Promise that the offering will solve every concern before learning the details.
   - D. Ask for unrelated personal details to make the customer feel known.
   - **Answer: B.** A summary checks understanding and lets the customer correct the record.

3. A seller can document that its software timestamps assigned tasks, but has no study showing a universal productivity gain. Which presentation is most responsible?
   - A. Every customer will save ten hours each week.
   - B. The timestamps prove that no task will ever be missed.
   - C. The tool records task assignments and timestamps; whether that helps your workflow depends on your process and should be evaluated in context.
   - D. Most organizations choose it, so the buyer does not need evidence.
   - **Answer: C.** State a documented feature without claiming an unsupported outcome.

4. A recipient asks not to receive more promotional email. What is the most appropriate general response?
   - A. Keep sending messages if the product might be useful later.
   - B. Transfer the address to another seller so someone else can follow up.
   - C. Require the recipient to explain why they opted out before recording the request.
   - D. Record the preference and honor it promptly; apply the requirements that govern the relevant message type and jurisdiction.
   - **Answer: D.** Respect the preference and follow applicable channel rules.

5. A team defines 20 qualified opportunities for one month and records 8 that advance to proposal review. What is the proposal-advancement rate for that defined cohort?
   - A. 40%
   - B. 15%
   - C. 60%
   - D. 250%
   - **Answer: A.** 8 divided by 20 equals 40%.
