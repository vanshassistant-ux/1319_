# Financial Projections

## In 30 Seconds

A financial projection is a conditional model, not a forecasted fact. It turns clearly stated assumptions—such as units, collection timing, payroll, or supplier payment timing—into estimated financial statements. A useful projection keeps the income statement, cash-flow statement, and balance sheet consistent, then tests what changes under alternative assumptions. It cannot promise profit, cash, funding, valuation, or business success.

## Why This Matters

Projections make a venture's financial story inspectable. Instead of saying “sales will cover costs,” a student can show the assumptions, calculate the result, and ask what would happen if a key assumption changes. That exposes cash-timing problems that a profit figure can hide and helps a team recognize which uncertainty deserves more evidence. In a course, projections are evidence for discussion and revision—not individualized financial, investment, accounting, tax, or funding advice, and not a prediction of a real venture's outcome.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a financial projection as an assumption-driven planning model rather than a prediction.
- Distinguish the high-level purposes of the income statement, statement of cash flows, and balance sheet.
- Explain why a profitable period can differ from a cash-positive period.
- Trace how a stated transaction can affect more than one financial statement.
- Apply scenario and sensitivity reasoning to a hypothetical model without treating the result as a recommendation.

## The College Version

### Projection purpose: make assumptions visible

A financial projection answers a conditional question: if a venture operates under stated assumptions, what financial pattern would the model produce? It makes inputs visible so that another reader can inspect them. Typical inputs include units delivered, price per unit, wages, materials, collection timing, supplier-payment timing, equipment purchases, and starting cash. A vendor quote, signed contract, pilot observation, published fee, and unexplained guess do not have the same level of support, so their basis should be recorded.

This is not a prophecy. A projection cannot prove a venture will succeed, establish a valuation, or tell someone whether to borrow, invest, raise money, or use personal savings. It is a model whose usefulness depends on its assumptions, arithmetic, and limits. Label the period, currency, source or basis for each input, and unresolved uncertainty. Separate observed facts from values used only in a hypothetical scenario.

Consistency is the goal. If a model assumes 100 units of sales, the revenue line, customer collections, direct costs, and supplier-payment assumptions should describe the same operating picture. If a date moves, related cash timing may move as well. Startup costs, pricing, funding, and business models supply different inputs or decisions; financial projections own the model connecting operating assumptions to financial consequences.

### Three statements answer different questions

The income statement reports performance over a period. In a simplified model, revenue minus expenses equals net income or net loss. It answers whether recognized revenues exceeded recognized expenses during a month, quarter, or year. That differs from asking how much cash is available on the last day of the period.

The statement of cash flows reports cash entering and leaving during a period. A teaching model can start with opening cash, add cash collected, subtract cash paid, and calculate ending cash. More complete statements classify cash movements, but the central issue is timing: cash refers to when it is actually received or paid. A venture can have net income while customer payments are still outstanding, while it buys equipment, or while it repays an obligation. It can also receive owner contributions or borrowed cash without treating that receipt as revenue.

The balance sheet is a snapshot on a specific date, reporting assets, liabilities, and equity. Assets are resources controlled by the business, liabilities are obligations it owes, and equity is the residual interest after liabilities. In a basic model, assets equal liabilities plus equity. Cash is one asset, not the entire balance sheet. Receivables, inventory, equipment, and prepaid items may be assets; supplier obligations can be liabilities. Period income or loss affects equity, while cash and other transactions change the balances at the end date. Exact reporting treatment can depend on the entity, jurisdiction, and accounting framework; this is general conceptual education, not regulated-statement preparation guidance.

### Cash and profit differ because timing differs

Under accrual accounting, a sale may be recognized when work is completed even if the customer pays later. Expenses can also be recognized when resources are used or an obligation is incurred, even if payment occurs in another period. An income statement can therefore show net income while collections lag. The cash-flow statement makes the timing visible.

Consider a hypothetical design studio that recognizes $2,400 of April revenue and $1,900 of April expenses. Its simplified income statement shows $500 net income. It collects only $1,800 of customer cash, pays $1,650 of operating cash costs, and buys $200 of equipment for cash. Its cash change is negative even though the model shows a profit. The uncollected amount can be modeled as accounts receivable; the equipment purchase can create an asset rather than an ordinary April expense in this illustration. The example has no tax calculation and says nothing about a real venture's viability.

Neither cash nor profit is “better.” They answer different questions. Reconcile them through explicit assumptions about collections, payments, purchases, leases, and what remains owed at period end. Unknown timing is an uncertainty to label, not an invitation to enter a confident-looking number.

### Scenarios and sensitivities test dependence, not destiny

A base case is one coherent assumption set, not the future by definition. Scenario analysis changes several linked inputs to represent a named condition, such as a delayed opening or a slower collection cycle. Sensitivity analysis changes one input at a time to show its effect on an output such as ending cash or net income. Both make uncertainty discussable; neither predicts actual customers or recommends a business decision.

For the hypothetical design studio, lower recognized revenue by 10 percent from $2,400 to $2,160 while holding expenses at $1,900. Net income becomes $260. In a collection-delay scenario, keep recognized revenue at $2,400 but collect only $1,500; with $1,650 of operating payments and a $200 equipment purchase, cash change becomes negative $350. These results show the model's dependence on revenue and collection timing. They do not endorse a price, recommend financing, or tell someone how much personal cash to contribute.

Record the changed assumption, recompute affected lines, and state the remaining uncertainty. Do not add an unexplained buffer merely to make a total look prudent. Name the uncertainty and identify evidence that could improve the assumption. Honest revision is more useful than a complicated spreadsheet that conceals its inputs.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Financial projection:** An assumption-driven model of possible financial statements over a stated period; not a prediction or guarantee.
- **Assumption:** A stated input a model uses when an outcome is not already known.
- **Income statement:** A report of revenues, expenses, and resulting net income or loss over a period.
- **Statement of cash flows:** A report of cash inflows and cash outflows during a period.
- **Balance sheet:** A snapshot of assets, liabilities, and equity on a specific date.
- **Net income:** The excess of recognized revenues and gains over recognized expenses and losses for a period.
- **Accounts receivable:** Amounts customers owe for goods or services already provided.
- **Scenario analysis:** Testing a model with a named alternative set of assumptions.
- **Sensitivity analysis:** Testing how an output changes when one specified input changes while others stay fixed.

## Eli-10

A projection is like making a map for a trip you have not taken yet. You write down your best current assumptions: where you might go, how far it is, and when you expect to stop. The map can help you notice a missing bridge or an unrealistic schedule, but it cannot promise there will be no traffic.

A business projection works similarly. One page asks whether the model earned more than it used up during a month. Another asks when actual cash arrived and left. A third is a snapshot of what the business has, owes, and is worth on one date. Those questions are related but not identical. A business might have a profit on paper while a customer has not yet paid, so cash is tight.

Changing an assumption is a way to learn. If fewer customers pay this month, what happens to cash? The result describes that made-up scenario; it does not tell a real person to invest, borrow, or take a risk.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of three views of a school fundraiser. The income statement is the scorecard for the whole event: money earned minus costs. The cash-flow view is the envelope count: when cash actually entered or left. The balance sheet is a photograph at closing time: cash, unsold supplies, unpaid bills, and the amount left for the group.

The fundraiser is simpler than a business and may not use formal accounting. This analogy cannot decide how a real business should record a transaction, choose funding, or meet tax and legal requirements.

## Worked Example

Harbor Sketch is a hypothetical design studio. Its April model assumes $2,400 of recognized service revenue and $1,900 of recognized April expenses, so net income is $2,400 − $1,900 = $500. The same model assumes $1,800 collected in April, $1,650 of operating cash paid, and $200 paid for equipment. Cash movement is $1,800 − $1,650 − $200 = −$50. The $600 not yet collected can be modeled as accounts receivable, and the equipment as an asset in this simplified illustration.

For sensitivity reasoning, reduce recognized revenue by 10 percent to $2,160 while holding expenses at $1,900: net income becomes $260. For a collection-delay scenario, keep recognized revenue at $2,400 but collect only $1,500: cash change becomes $1,500 − $1,650 − $200 = −$350. These are arithmetic checks on assumptions, not forecasts, valuations, or advice about funding, pricing, investing, or a real decision.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling a projection a prediction.** Label assumptions, period, and uncertainty; models show conditional results.
- **Treating net income as ending cash.** Check collections, payments, equipment purchases, and obligations separately.
- **Changing one assumption without related lines.** Recalculate every affected revenue, cost, receivable, payable, cash, or balance-sheet line.
- **Using an unexplained scenario as proof a decision is safe.** Name the changed assumption and the uncertainty that remains.
- **Using a model to recommend borrowing or investing.** Keep it general education and seek qualified context-specific help for real decisions.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Income statement | Statement of cash flows | The first reports recognized performance; the second reports actual cash movement. |
| Balance sheet | Income statement | The balance sheet is a point-in-time snapshot; the income statement covers a period. |
| Scenario analysis | Sensitivity analysis | A scenario changes a coherent set of inputs; sensitivity analysis changes one named input. |

## Key Takeaway

Financial projections are transparent, conditional models. Keep assumptions visible, make the statements consistent, distinguish profit from cash timing, and use scenarios to learn where uncertainty matters—not to make promises or recommendations.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What is the most accurate description of a financial projection?**
   - A. A guaranteed estimate of a venture's future value.
   - B. An assumption-driven model of possible financial results over a stated period.
   - C. A recommendation to obtain a particular source of funding.
   - D. A replacement for bookkeeping and financial statements.
   - **Answer:** B. It translates stated inputs into conditional results; it does not guarantee value or prescribe funding.

2. **Which statement is designed to show cash inflows and outflows over a period?**
   - A. Balance sheet
   - B. Statement of owner's equity
   - C. Statement of cash flows
   - D. Income statement
   - **Answer:** C. It focuses on cash received and paid during the period.

3. **A model recognizes $2,400 of April revenue, but customers pay $1,800 during April. Which conclusion is best?**
   - A. The model must show a $600 loss.
   - B. The $600 must be ignored until a later year.
   - C. Recognized revenue and cash collections are always identical.
   - D. The model can report revenue while separately showing $1,800 of cash collected if its timing assumptions support that treatment.
   - **Answer:** D. Recognition and collection can occur in different periods.

4. **In the Harbor Sketch hypothetical, which calculation gives April net income?**
   - A. $2,400 recognized revenue − $1,900 recognized expenses = $500.
   - B. $1,800 cash collected − $1,650 cash paid − $200 equipment = −$50.
   - C. $2,400 recognized revenue − $1,800 cash collected = $600.
   - D. $1,800 cash collected + $200 equipment = $2,000.
   - **Answer:** A. The negative $50 figure is cash change, not net income.

5. **Which sensitivity test is most transparent?**
   - A. Change several numbers until the outcome looks favorable.
   - B. Hold other inputs fixed, lower a stated revenue assumption by 10 percent, and recalculate outputs.
   - C. Treat the base case as proof cash will be available.
   - D. Choose funding before identifying the cash driver.
   - **Answer:** B. It identifies one input change and its model effect.
