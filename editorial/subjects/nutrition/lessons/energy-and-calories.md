# Energy and Calories

## In 30 Seconds

A calorie is a unit of energy. The number on a food label tells you how much energy one serving of that food provides. Carbohydrates and protein each supply about 4 calories per gram, fat about 9, and alcohol about 7. Your body spends that energy on everyday functions, movement, and growth, stores what it does not use right away, and tips the balance of your weight when energy in and energy out drift apart. The calorie is a measuring tool, not a verdict on you.

## Why This Matters

Calories are the most used number in nutrition and also the most misread. People see the number on a label and treat it as a score: good foods, bad foods, guilt, praise. That misses what the number actually is, a simple measurement of how much energy a serving holds. Understand that, and a label stops being a judgment and becomes information you can use: to compare portions, to notice that a small snack can carry as much energy as a meal, and to see why a gram of fat packs more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate. It is also the foundation for energy balance, the bigger idea that shapes weight over time.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a calorie as a unit of energy and explain what the calorie number on a food label measures.
- State the energy values of carbohydrate, protein, fat, and alcohol (4, 4, 9, and 7 calories per gram) and identify fat as the most energy-dense.
- Explain that a food-label calorie is a kilocalorie and why the everyday label simply calls it a calorie.
- Describe the three broad uses of food energy in the body: immediate use, storage, and building.
- Apply calories-per-gram values and serving sizes to estimate the energy in a food from its label.

## The College Version

### A calorie is a measure of energy

A calorie is a unit of energy, the same way a liter is a unit of volume or a minute is a unit of time. In nutrition, the calories in food describe how much energy that food can deliver to the body. OpenStax's nutrition text gives the working definition used throughout this lesson: calories measure the amount of heat it takes to raise 1 kilogram of water by 1 degree Celsius, and they indicate the amount of energy a person takes in or needs each day. You do not need to picture heated water to use the idea; the practical point is simpler. Every food and drink contains a certain amount of stored energy, and the calorie number attached to it is that amount, expressed in one shared unit. Because it is a unit, the calorie has no moral content. It no more judges a food than a liter judges a bottle.

### Where food energy comes from: the 4-4-9-7 rule

The body gets energy from four sources in food: carbohydrates, proteins, fats, and alcohol. They are not equal. Carbohydrates supply about 4 calories per gram, proteins about 4 calories per gram, fats about 9 calories per gram, and alcohol about 7 calories per gram. OpenStax's macronutrient table lists carbohydrate 4, fat 9, protein 4, and alcohol 7, and MedlinePlus independently confirms that one gram of protein supplies 4 calories. The striking fact is fat: a gram of fat carries more than twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate or protein, which is why fatty foods can be calorie-dense even in small portions. Alcohol sits between them, delivering energy but, as OpenStax notes, none of the vitamins, minerals, or fiber that foods carry; its calories are often called empty. Water, by contrast, provides no energy at all.

### What the body does with food energy

The energy from food has three broad destinations, and every calorie lands in one of them. The first is immediate use: your body burns energy constantly to keep your heart beating, your lungs moving, and your body temperature steady, and it burns more when you walk, run, think hard, or even digest the meal itself. OpenStax summarizes this as needing calories to function, digest food, grow, and move. The second destination is building: energy and the nutrients that carry it go into growth, repair, and maintenance, from healing a scraped knee to rebuilding muscle after exercise. The third is storage: when you take in more energy than your body uses at the moment, the surplus is set aside, largely as body fat, for times when food energy is needed and not coming in. None of these uses is good or bad on its own; they are simply what the body does with fuel.

### Energy in, energy out

The single most important relationship in this lesson is also its simplest: energy in versus energy out. Energy in is the calories you take in from food and drinks. Energy out is the calories your body uses, through physical activity and daily living. The FDA puts it plainly: to achieve or maintain a healthy body weight, balance the number of calories you eat and drink with the number of calories your body uses. MedlinePlus says the same thing from the other side: you gain weight when you take in more calories than you use up. Notice what this idea does not do: it does not say how the body adjusts its energy use, why people differ, or how fast weight changes. Those questions belong to the energy-balance topic, which is its own lesson. Here, the principle itself is the takeaway.

### Reading calories on a label: a worked example

Rosa picks up a box of granola bars and reads the Nutrition Facts label. Serving size: 1 bar (40 g). Servings per container: 6. Calories: 190. First she checks the arithmetic with the per-gram values. The bar contains 10 g of fat, 22 g of carbohydrate, and 3 g of protein. Fat: 10 x 9 = 90. Carbohydrate: 22 x 4 = 88. Protein: 3 x 4 = 12. Total: 90 + 88 + 12 = 190, matching the label. Now the portions, because the label's 190 describes one bar, not the box. Two bars: 190 x 2 = 380. The whole box: 6 x 190 = 1,140. One and a half bars: 190 + 95 = 285. One label, four different answers, all correct, because the calorie number only means something together with the serving size. The FDA makes the same point with its own example: one serving shown on a label, 220 calories; two servings, 440.

### A tool, not a moral score

The calorie number is a measurement, and measurements are tools. A ruler does not praise or scold the thing it measures, and a calorie count does not praise or scold the food it describes, or the person who eats it. A 190-calorie bar is not a bad bar and a 120-calorie bar is not automatically a good one; the right choice depends on serving size, on what else you have eaten, on your activity, and on what the bar actually contains besides energy. Labels even remind us that claims like fat-free or no added sugars do not mean calorie-free. The honest way to use the number is to compare like with like, notice when portions quietly double the energy, and let the calorie inform a decision instead of making it alone.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Calorie** — A unit of energy; on food labels, the amount of energy one serving of the food provides.
- **Kilocalorie** — The unit behind the food label: the heat energy needed to raise 1 kilogram of water by 1 degree Celsius; one label Calorie is one kilocalorie.
- **Food energy** — The usable energy a food supplies to the body, measured in calories or kilocalories.
- **Carbohydrate** — A nutrient that supplies about 4 calories per gram and is a main fuel for the body.
- **Protein** — A nutrient that supplies about 4 calories per gram and provides material for building and repair.
- **Fat** — A nutrient that supplies about 9 calories per gram, the most energy-dense of the three.
- **Alcohol** — A source of about 7 calories per gram that provides energy but little else nutritionally.
- **Serving size** — The amount of food a label's nutrition information refers to, such as one bar or one cup.
- **Energy balance** — The relationship between the calories taken in from food and drinks and the calories the body uses.

## Eli-10

Imagine you and Eli are looking at a snack wrapper. A calorie is just a way to count energy, like liters count water. The number on the package tells you how much energy is sitting inside one serving. Big numbers mean lots of fuel; small numbers mean less. Your body burns some of that fuel right away to keep you going, saves some for later, and uses some as raw material for building and repair. You never have to count calories to understand this lesson, but knowing what the number means is like knowing how to read your own fuel gauge.

Think of your body like a phone and food like its charger. Carbohydrates and protein are a normal charger that adds a little battery per minute; fat is a fast charger that adds more than twice as much. The calorie number on the package is the battery percentage each serving delivers. If you leave the phone plugged in all day, the battery sits full and the extra charge goes nowhere useful. Your body does something similar when it stores energy it does not need right now.

The analogy has limits: a phone battery tops out and stops accepting charge, while a body keeps storing surplus energy and keeps needing fuel even at rest. People also use fuel at different rates, and the analogy hides that food is building material, not just fuel: energy is only part of what your body takes from a meal.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **On a Nutrition Facts label, the number listed as "Calories" tells you which of the following?**
   - A. The amount of energy in one serving of the food
   - B. The weight of one serving of the food in grams
   - C. The number of servings in the package
   - D. The amount of fat in one serving of the food

   **Answer: A.** The label's Calorie number is the total energy delivered by one serving, from carbohydrate, fat, protein, and alcohol together. It is not the serving's weight, the package's serving count, or any single nutrient.

2. **In calories per gram, the energy values for carbohydrate, protein, fat, and alcohol are:**
   - A. 4, 9, 4, 7
   - B. 4, 4, 9, 7
   - C. 9, 4, 4, 7
   - D. 4, 4, 7, 9

   **Answer: B.** Carbohydrate and protein each supply about 4 calories per gram, fat about 9, and alcohol about 7. The other orders swap fat, alcohol, and the two 4s.

3. **A snack label lists 8 grams of fat, 20 grams of carbohydrate, and 5 grams of protein per serving. How many Calories are in one serving?**
   - A. 132
   - B. 172
   - C. 212
   - D. 117

   **Answer: B.** Fat supplies 9 calories per gram and carbohydrate and protein supply 4 each: 8 x 9 = 72, 20 x 4 = 80, and 5 x 4 = 20, for a total of 172. The other totals come from using wrong per-gram values or dropping a nutrient.

4. **Priya eats half a bag of trail mix. The label says serving size 1/4 cup, 140 Calories per serving, and about 8 servings per container. How many Calories does the half bag contain?**
   - A. 140
   - B. 280
   - C. 1,120
   - D. 560

   **Answer: D.** Half the bag is 4 servings (8 divided by 2), and 4 x 140 = 560. The other options are one serving (140), two servings (280), and the whole bag (1,120).

5. **Two friends compare granola bars: one has 190 Calories per bar, the other has 120. Which of the following is the most reasonable conclusion?**
   - A. The calorie number is one piece of information; the better choice also depends on serving size, what else was eaten that day, and what the bars provide besides energy
   - B. The 120-Calorie bar is always the healthier choice, because fewer calories always mean better nutrition
   - C. The 190-Calorie bar is a bad food, and eating it means the day is ruined
   - D. Calorie numbers on labels are random guesses and should be ignored

   **Answer: A.** A calorie number measures only the energy in a serving. It cannot by itself rank foods as good or bad, and label numbers are measurements, not guesses. The honest use is to compare portions and weigh the whole picture.
