# Pronouns

## In 30 Seconds

Pronouns are words that stand in for nouns or noun phrases, so a sentence does not have to repeat the same name over and over. The main families include personal pronouns (I, me, she, him, they, us), possessive forms (my, mine, your, yours), reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself), demonstratives (this, that, these, those), indefinite pronouns (someone, everything, none), and relative pronouns (who, which, that). Each pronoun should match the noun it replaces, its antecedent, or readers will not know who or what is meant.

## Why This Matters

Pronouns carry real weight in writing and speech. Used well, they keep sentences from repeating the same nouns, and choosing the right form — he or him, they or them, my or mine — is part of sounding precise and natural. Used carelessly, they can derail a paragraph: when readers have to pause and ask what *it* or *they* means, the point is lost. In academic work, where every sentence should be easy to follow, clear pronoun reference keeps an argument readable. Learning the main pronoun families also prepares you for later topics — sentence structure, verb agreement, and questions — which all depend on knowing what a pronoun is doing.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a pronoun as a word that substitutes for a noun or noun phrase.
- Distinguish subject, object, and possessive forms of personal pronouns and place them in the correct positions.
- Explain the difference between possessive pronouns (*mine*, *yours*) and possessive determiners (*my*, *your*).
- Apply reflexive, demonstrative, indefinite, and relative pronouns correctly in original sentences.
- Analyze sentences for pronoun-antecedent agreement and clarity, and revise vague or ambiguous pronoun references.

## The College Version

### Substitution: what pronouns are for

Pronouns are words that substitute for nouns or noun phrases. Their job is to save repetition while keeping the sentence readable. Without them, even a simple account becomes a tongue twister: "When Marcy woke this morning, Marcy had a headache, so Marcy went to Marcy's medicine cabinet and took one of Marcy's headache pills." With pronouns: "When Marcy woke this morning, she had a headache, so she went to her medicine cabinet and took one of her headache pills."

The noun a pronoun replaces is its **antecedent**, and the antecedent usually appears earlier in the sentence or in a nearby sentence. In "The car was far away; Jessica could not see what model it was," the antecedent of *it* is *car*. Good pronoun use is a reading skill as much as a writing skill: a reader must be able to trace every pronoun back to the noun it stands for. If the trail breaks, the sentence may still look grammatical, but the meaning leaks out.

### Case: subject, object, and possessive forms

Personal pronouns change form according to their job in the clause, a pattern grammarians call **case**. Subject forms — *I, you, he, she, it, we, they* — fill the subject position, the person or thing the clause is about. Object forms — *me, you, him, her, it, us, them* — fill the object position, the person or thing affected by the verb or linked to a preposition.

The pair *I* and *me* shows the pattern: "Jasmine and I ordered pizza" uses *I* because it is part of the subject, while "The pizza was delivered to Jasmine and me" uses *me* because it is the object of the preposition *to*. The same logic covers *him* and *her*, *we* and *us*, and *they* and *them*.

Possessive forms show ownership and do two different jobs. Forms like *my, your, her, our,* and *their* stand before a noun and are called **possessive determiners**; forms like *mine, yours, hers, ours,* and *theirs* stand alone as **possessive pronouns**. Compare "That is my coat" with "That coat is mine." The choice is positional: does the word carry a noun along with it, or does it replace the noun entirely?

### Reflexive, demonstrative, indefinite, and relative pronouns

Beyond personal pronouns, four families are worth naming.

**Reflexive pronouns** — *myself, yourself, himself, herself, itself, ourselves, yourselves, themselves* — end in *-self* or *-selves* and are used when the subject and the object of a verb are the same person or thing: "Sam taught himself to play the guitar." When they are not the same, the reflexive form is wrong: "Contact Priya or me," not "Contact Priya or myself."

**Demonstrative pronouns** — *this, that, these, those* — point directly at specific items: "These are the best cookies." The same four words can also work as determiners before a noun, as in "this bus."

**Indefinite pronouns** — *someone, everybody, everything, none*, and similar words — refer to people or things that are not specific or not named: "Everything on the shelf belongs to someone."

Finally, **relative pronouns** — *who, which,* and *that* — introduce a clause that connects back to a noun: *who* for people, *which* for animals and things, and *that* for both in informal writing. How those clauses are assembled is the work of the sentence-structure topic; here it is enough to recognize the connectors and the nouns they attach to.

### Agreement and clarity: making the reference land

A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number, and where the language marks it, in gender: plural "The girls brought their umbrellas" pairs a plural noun with a plural pronoun, while "Toby has one daughter; she is studying art" pairs singular with singular. Beyond agreement, the reference must be clear.

The classic failure is a vague *it*: "When Lizzie smacked her arm into the glass window, she broke it" leaves readers unsure whether the arm or the window broke. The fix is to name the noun: "Lizzie broke the window when she smacked her arm into it." The same problem appears with vague *they* ("They should not show violence on TV" — who are *they*?) and with *this* or *that* used without a clear target.

A practical revision habit: read each pronoun and ask what noun it points to; if two answers are possible, repeat the noun or rebuild the sentence. One usage note: *they* and *their* are now widely used as singular, gender-neutral pronouns, a practice recognized by major style guides. Choosing that form is a matter of context and audience, not a rule violation.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Pronoun:** A word that stands in for a noun or noun phrase, such as *she*, *them*, or *this*.
- **Antecedent:** The noun or noun phrase that a pronoun refers back to in the sentence or nearby text.
- **Subject pronoun:** A personal pronoun form used as the subject of a clause, such as *I*, *she*, *we*, or *they*.
- **Object pronoun:** A personal pronoun form used as the object of a verb or preposition, such as *me*, *him*, *us*, or *them*.
- **Possessive pronoun:** A form such as *mine*, *yours*, or *theirs* that shows ownership and stands alone without a following noun.
- **Possessive determiner:** A form such as *my*, *your*, or *their* that shows ownership and appears directly before a noun.
- **Reflexive pronoun:** A pronoun ending in *-self* or *-selves*, used when the subject and the object of a verb are the same person or thing.
- **Demonstrative pronoun:** A pronoun that points to a specific item: *this*, *that*, *these*, or *those*.
- **Indefinite pronoun:** A pronoun that refers to a person or thing that is not specific or named, such as *someone*, *everything*, or *none*.
- **Relative pronoun:** A pronoun such as *who*, *which*, or *that* that introduces a clause connecting back to a noun.

## Eli-10

Pronouns are short words that take the place of names and nouns. Instead of saying "Priya picked up Priya's coat and put Priya's coat over Priya's arm," you say "Priya picked up her coat and put it over her arm." Pronouns come in families: *I* and *me* talk about the speaker; *you* talks to someone; *he*, *she*, and *it* talk about other people and things; *we* and *they* talk about groups. Some pronouns show ownership (*mine*, *yours*), some point at things (*this*, *these*), and some talk about people or things without naming them (*someone*, *everything*). The important habit is to make sure each pronoun clearly points back to its noun, because a reader can only understand *it* or *they* by finding the noun it stands for.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a pronoun as a ticket stub. The stub is not the person — it is a small piece of paper that stands for the person and lets you find them again. When you write *it* or *they*, you are handing the reader a stub; the reader checks which noun it belongs to. If the stub matches a clear name, everyone knows who is meant.

The analogy has limits. A ticket stub always belongs to exactly one ticket, but one pronoun can point to several nouns, and readers sometimes have to look back a sentence or two. Pronouns are also not blank placeholders: they carry grammar, such as whether the word is a subject (*she*) or an object (*her*), and a stub does no such work.

## Worked Example

Consider this draft: "When the bus arrived at the station, it was late, so the driver opened the doors and told the passengers they could wait inside." A reader can probably work out that *it* means the bus and *they* means the passengers, but the sentence is forcing a lot of guesswork. The revision repeats the key noun once: "When the bus arrived at the station, the bus was late, so the driver opened the doors and told the passengers they could wait inside." Repeating *bus* removes the ambiguity around *it*. The pronouns that remain — *they* — point clearly to the passengers, and the subject forms in the sentence match their positions. The lesson: if a pronoun could reasonably be two different nouns, name the noun. Clear reference beats elegant compression every time.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using an object pronoun in subject position:** "Him and Sara won the prize." Use subject forms for subjects: "Sara and he won the prize." Save *him*, *her*, and *me* for objects.
- **Adding a reflexive pronoun where a plain object pronoun belongs:** "If you have questions, contact Priya or myself." The subject (*you*) is not the object, so no reflexive is needed: "Contact Priya or me." Reflexive forms are for when the subject and object are the same.
- **Leaving a vague *it* or *they*:** "Mina put the cake beside the candle, and then it melted." The pronoun could point to either noun. Name the noun: "Mina put the cake beside the candle, and then the candle melted."
- **Confusing possessive pronouns with possessive determiners:** "That umbrella is my." *My* needs a noun after it ("my umbrella"); when the pronoun stands alone, use *mine*: "That umbrella is mine."
- **Mismatching a plural antecedent with a singular pronoun:** "The students brought his lunch." A plural noun takes a plural pronoun: "The students brought their lunches." Match the pronoun's number to the noun it replaces.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Possessive pronoun (*mine*) | Possessive determiner (*my*) | *Mine* stands alone and replaces the noun ("That coat is mine"), while *my* must be followed by a noun ("my coat"). |
| Subject pronoun (*she*) | Object pronoun (*her*) | *She* fills the subject position ("She called"), while *her* fills the object position ("I called her"). |
| Demonstrative pronoun (*this*) | Demonstrative determiner (*this bus*) | As a pronoun, *this* stands alone and replaces the noun ("This is my bus"); as a determiner it modifies a following noun ("this bus"). |

## Key Takeaway

Pronouns stand in for nouns and spare us endless repetition, but they work only when each one clearly matches its antecedent; choose the right form for the job and name the noun whenever the reference gets fuzzy.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which list contains only subject forms of personal pronouns?**
   - A. me, him, us, them
   - B. I, she, we, they
   - C. my, his, our, their
   - D. mine, yours, hers, ours
   - **Answer:** B. *I*, *she*, *we*, and *they* are subject forms used in subject position; the other lists contain object, possessive-determiner, or possessive-pronoun forms.

2. **In "The engineers tested the bridge, and they approved the design," what is the antecedent of *they*?**
   - A. the bridge
   - B. the design
   - C. the engineers
   - D. the test
   - **Answer:** C. *They* replaces *the engineers*, the noun that performs the approving; the bridge and the design are the things the engineers acted on.

3. **Which sentence uses the correct object pronoun?**
   - A. The professor praised Amara and me.
   - B. The professor praised Amara and I.
   - C. The professor praised Amara and myself.
   - D. The professor praised Amara and we.
   - **Answer:** A. *Praised* takes an object, so the object form *me* is correct; *I* and *we* are subject forms, and *myself* is a reflexive pronoun that is not needed when the subject and the object are different.

4. **Which sentence uses a reflexive pronoun incorrectly?**
   - A. Sam taught himself to play the guitar.
   - B. The children dressed themselves before breakfast.
   - C. If you have questions, contact Priya or myself.
   - D. I reminded myself to bring the charger.
   - **Answer:** C. In the incorrect sentence the subject is *you* and the object is Priya or the speaker, so the plain object pronoun *me* belongs; reflexive forms are reserved for when the subject and the object are the same person or thing.

5. **In "These are the best cookies," what kind of pronoun is *these*?**
   - A. a personal pronoun
   - B. an indefinite pronoun
   - C. a relative pronoun
   - D. a demonstrative pronoun
   - **Answer:** D. *These* points directly at the cookies, so it is a demonstrative pronoun; personal, indefinite, and relative pronouns do different jobs, and indefinite pronouns such as *someone* or *none* do not point at a specific item.
