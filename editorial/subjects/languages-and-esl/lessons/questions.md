# Questions

## In 30 Seconds

English builds questions by moving an auxiliary verb, or the verb *be*, in front of the subject: "Is Priya ready?" When a statement has no auxiliary, the helper *do* appears: "She works" becomes "Does she work?" Wh- questions open with a question word (what, where, when, who, why, how) and keep the auxiliary after it: "Where does the bus stop?" If the question word is the subject, nothing moves: "Who called you?" Question tags ("isn't it?", "do you?") and polite frames ("Could you tell me...?") complete the system.

## Why This Matters

Questions are how we find things out, and in college they do real work: clarifying an assignment in office hours, running an interview, or emailing a professor. A well-formed question gets a precise answer; a muddled one makes the listener guess. Knowing the difference between yes/no and wh- questions lets you choose the right tool, because a yes/no question confirms a fact while a wh- question invites detail and explanation. The same machinery — auxiliaries, inversion, and do-support — reappears in question tags and polite forms, so learning it once pays off across speaking and writing. And since question patterns differ across languages, seeing the English system explicitly makes it easier to use rather than assuming the rules you already know.

## Learning Objectives

- Define yes/no questions and wh- questions and distinguish them by the kind of answer each expects.
- Explain how auxiliary verbs and the verb *be* move before the subject to form questions, and describe when do-support supplies an auxiliary.
- Distinguish subject questions from object questions by the grammatical role of the question word.
- Apply the polarity rule to build question tags for affirmative and negative statements.
- Form polite indirect questions with frames such as "Could you tell me...?" and statement word order in the embedded clause.
- Analyze common question-formation errors, including missing do-support and missing inversion, and correct them.

## The College Version

### Questions as a sentence type

English sentences come in four main types: declarative sentences make statements, imperative sentences give commands or requests, interrogative sentences ask questions, and exclamatory sentences express strong emotion. The interrogative is the type this lesson is about. The clearest clue is often the question mark at the end, but the real test is word order: a question moves part of the verb in front of the subject. Compare "The bus stops here" with "Does the bus stop here?" Both describe the same situation, but the second puts the auxiliary first and turns the sentence into a request for information. Noticing that difference, a reader or listener can hear a question coming before it even ends.

### Yes/no questions: inversion and do-support

A yes/no question expects an answer of yes or no. English forms it by inversion: the auxiliary verb, or the verb *be* itself, moves in front of the subject. "Priya is ready" becomes "Is Priya ready?"; "They have finished" becomes "Have they finished?"; "Nina can swim" becomes "Can Nina swim?" Only the first auxiliary moves, so "The garden has been watered" becomes "Has the garden been watered?"

Many statements have no auxiliary at all: "She works at the clinic." English never moves the main verb, so "Works she at the clinic?" is not a question in English. Instead, the helper *do* supplies the auxiliary: "Does she work at the clinic?" The main verb returns to its base form, so the *-s* that marked the statement disappears. In the past, *did* plays the same role: "You usually walked to work" becomes "Did you usually walk to work?" The verb *be* is the exception: it moves by itself and never borrows *do*, so we say "Are you ready?" and never "Do you be ready?"

### Wh- questions: question word first

Wh- questions open with a question word, such as what, where, when, who, why, or how, and they ask for information rather than a yes or no answer. The question word goes first, and the auxiliary stays before the subject: "Where does the bus stop?" "When is the library open?" "Why did the train stop?" "How much does the course cost?"

There is one important exception. When the question word is itself the subject of the clause, no auxiliary is added and no inversion happens. "Who called you?" works because *who* is the subject and the word order is exactly that of a statement. But "Who did you call?" needs do-support, because here *you* is the subject and *who* is the object. The same contrast appears with *what*: "What happened next?" treats *what* as the subject, while "What did you buy?" treats it as the object.

### Question tags and polite questions

A question tag is a short ending that turns a statement into a question seeking confirmation: "The bus has left, hasn't it?" The tag reuses the auxiliary or *be* of the statement plus a pronoun, and it follows the polarity rule: an affirmative statement takes a negative tag, and a negative statement takes an affirmative tag. "The bus hasn't left, has it?" A statement with no auxiliary takes *do* in the tag: "You like green tea, don't you?"

English also softens direct questions in polite situations, often by embedding the question inside a frame such as "Could you tell me...?" or "Do you know...?" "Where is the station?" becomes "Could you tell me where the station is?" The embedded clause keeps statement word order, with no inversion and no *do*, even though the whole sentence is a question.

### Common errors to watch for

The most frequent question-formation errors follow from the two rules above. Missing do-support: "What means this word?" should be "What does this word mean?" Missing inversion: "Why she left early?" should be "Why did she leave early?" Adding *do* to *be*: "Are you agree?" should be "Do you agree?", because *be* never borrows *do*. And after *do*, *does*, or *did*, the main verb always returns to its base form: "Does she works here?" is wrong, while "Does she work here?" is right. When you catch yourself producing one of these patterns, the fix is usually a single move: add the missing auxiliary, or move the one that is already there.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Interrogative sentence:** A sentence that asks a question, such as "Is Priya ready?", and is one of the four main English sentence types.
- **Yes/no question:** A question that expects yes or no as its answer, formed by placing an auxiliary verb or the verb *be* before the subject.
- **Wh- question:** A question that opens with a question word (what, where, when, who, why, how) and asks for information rather than a yes or no answer.
- **Inversion:** The word-order change that puts the auxiliary verb or the verb *be* before the subject in a question.
- **Do-support:** The use of *do*, *does*, or *did* as the auxiliary in a question when the statement has no other auxiliary verb.
- **Question word:** A word such as what, when, where, who, why, or how that opens a wh- question and names the kind of information requested.
- **Subject question:** A question in which the question word is the subject of the clause, so no auxiliary is added, as in "Who called you?"
- **Object question:** A question in which the question word is the object of the clause, so an auxiliary is used, as in "Who did you call?"
- **Question tag:** A short auxiliary-or-*be* plus pronoun ending added to a statement, such as "isn't it?" or "do you?", following the polarity rule.
- **Indirect question:** A question embedded in a frame such as "Could you tell me...?" in which the embedded clause keeps statement word order.

## Eli-10

Questions are easy to build once you know one trick: find the verb. If the sentence already has a helper verb like *is*, *have*, or *can*, move that helper in front of the subject and you have a question: "The bus has left" becomes "Has the bus left?" If there is no helper, add the helper *do*: "She works" becomes "Does she work?" Then, if you need specific information, put a question word at the front: "Where does she work?" But if the question word is the person or thing doing the action, leave everything where it is: "Who works there?" The same helper system runs the tags at the end of questions, "The bus has left, hasn't it?", and the polite forms, "Could you tell me where the station is?"

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a question as a doorbell. The sentence is the door, and the auxiliary is the button: you press it, by moving it in front of the subject, and the sentence opens into a question. When there is no button on the door, no auxiliary, you install one: *do*. The question word is the label on the door telling you what kind of room is behind it: *where* for places, *when* for times, *who* for people, *why* for reasons.

The analogy has limits. A doorbell rings the same way every time, but a sentence can contain several auxiliaries and only the first one moves: "Has the garden been watered?" The label idea also breaks down, because question words do not just name the answer, they change the grammar of the sentence, as when *who* becomes the subject and no auxiliary appears at all. And unlike a doorbell, a question invites an answer rather than ending the exchange.

## Worked Example

A student is preparing a group presentation and needs three facts from her professor. She wants to know whether slides are required. The statement "The presentation needs slides" has no auxiliary, so she asks with do-support: "Does the presentation need slides?" Next she needs the deadline: the question word *when* goes first, then the auxiliary: "When does the draft have to be submitted?" Then she remembers the outline review. The person who will review is unknown, so the question word is the subject and nothing moves: "Who will review the outline?" As she leaves she checks one more thing with a tag: "You've seen our outline, haven't you?" And when the professor's assistant appears, she softens a request: "Could you tell me where the sign-up sheet is?" Every one of these questions uses the same machinery: an auxiliary before the subject, *do* when there is none, and a question word out front when specific information is needed.

## Common Mistakes

- **Leaving out do-support, as in "What means this word?"** Add *do* with the base form of the main verb: "What does this word mean?"
- **Keeping statement word order in a wh- question, as in "Why she left early?"** Invert: put the auxiliary before the subject, so "Why did she leave early?"
- **Using *do* with *be*, as in "Are you agree?" or "Do you be ready?"** *Be* moves by itself without *do*: "Do you agree?" and "Are you ready?"
- **Keeping the *-s* after *do*, as in "Does she works here?"** After *do*, *does*, or *did*, the main verb returns to its base form: "Does she work here?"
- **Forgetting the polarity rule in tags, as in "She hasn't arrived, hasn't she?"** A negative statement takes an affirmative tag: "She hasn't arrived, has she?"

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yes/no question | Wh- question | A yes/no question expects yes or no and forms by inversion alone; a wh- question opens with a question word, expects information, and keeps the auxiliary after the question word. |
| Subject question | Object question | In a subject question the question word is the subject, so no auxiliary appears ("Who called you?"); in an object question it is the object, so do-support appears ("Who did you call?"). |
| Direct question | Indirect question | A direct question inverts ("Where is the station?"); an indirect question embeds the question in a frame and keeps statement word order ("Could you tell me where the station is?"). |

## Key Takeaway

English questions run on one engine: put an auxiliary or the verb *be* before the subject, add *do* when there is none, and let a question word lead whenever you need specific information.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which question asks for a yes or no answer?**
   - A. When does the library open?
   - B. Who locked the lab door?
   - C. Is the library open yet?
   - D. Where did you park the van?
   - **Answer:** C. *Is* before the subject *library* is inversion with *be*, and the question expects yes or no as its answer. The other three options open with question words (when, who, where) and ask for information instead.

2. **Which sentence correctly turns "She works at the clinic" into a question?**
   - A. Works she at the clinic?
   - B. Does she works at the clinic?
   - C. Is she work at the clinic?
   - D. Does she work at the clinic?
   - **Answer:** D. The statement has no auxiliary, so the question needs do-support: *does* carries the present-tense form and *work* returns to its base form. Inverting the main verb, doubling the *-s*, and borrowing *be* are all patterns English does not use.

3. **In the question "Who did you call?", what is the grammatical role of *who*?**
   - A. It is the object of the verb *call*.
   - B. It is the subject of the verb *call*.
   - C. It is the auxiliary verb of the question.
   - D. It is the main verb of the question.
   - **Answer:** A. *You called someone*, so *who* stands in for the object, and because the question word is the object the auxiliary *did* is needed. If *who* were the subject, the question would be "Who called you?" with no *do*; *did* is the auxiliary and *call* the main verb.

4. **Which question correctly asks where the meeting will take place?**
   - A. Where the meeting will take place?
   - B. Where will the meeting take place?
   - C. Where the meeting will takes place?
   - D. Will where the meeting take place?
   - **Answer:** B. Wh- questions put the question word first, then the auxiliary *will*, then the subject and the main verb. Options A and C keep statement order without inversion, and option D buries the question word after the auxiliary.

5. **Which question tag completes "The bus hasn't arrived yet, ___?"**
   - A. hasn't it?
   - B. is it?
   - C. has it?
   - D. doesn't it?
   - **Answer:** C. The statement is negative, so by the polarity rule the tag must be affirmative and reuse the same auxiliary: *hasn't arrived* takes *has it*. *Hasn't it* repeats the negative, *is it* uses the wrong auxiliary, and *doesn't it* both changes the auxiliary and ignores the negative statement.
