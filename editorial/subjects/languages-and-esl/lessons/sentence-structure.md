# Sentence Structure: Clauses, Sentence Types, and Sentence Problems

## In 30 Seconds

English sentences are built from clauses: groups of words with a subject and a predicate. An independent clause can stand alone; a dependent clause cannot. Four sentence types come from combining clauses: simple (one independent clause), compound (two or more), complex (one independent plus a dependent clause), and compound-complex (both patterns together). English usually follows subject-verb-object order, and conjunctions join clauses. Knowing these patterns helps you fix fragments, run-ons, and comma splices, and vary your writing.

## Why This Matters

Every English sentence, from a text message to a research paper, is a clause or a combination of clauses. Understanding how clauses fit together lets you read long academic sentences without losing the main idea, and write sentences that say exactly what you mean. When you know the four sentence types, you can fix the problems that make writing hard to follow: fragments that leave thoughts unfinished and run-ons that cram several ideas into one breathless stretch. Sentence variety also keeps your writing readable, because a page of identical short sentences reads like a list. These patterns are tools for clear communication, not judgments about any speaker's English.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a clause and distinguish independent clauses, which can stand alone, from dependent clauses, which cannot.
- Identify the four sentence types (simple, compound, complex, compound-complex) by the number and kind of clauses they contain.
- Explain basic English word order as subject-verb-object by default, with modifiers and objects arranged around that core.
- Apply coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS) to join independent clauses, and subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns to attach dependent clauses.
- Diagnose fragments, run-ons, and comma splices and revise them using the standard fixes.
- Analyze a paragraph for sentence variety and revise repetitive structures.

## The College Version

### Clauses: the building blocks

A clause is a group of words built around two parts: a subject and a predicate. The subject is the person or thing the clause is about, and the predicate is what is said about that person or thing, usually through a verb. An independent clause can stand alone as a complete sentence: "The bakery opens at six o'clock." A dependent clause also has a subject and a predicate, but it begins with a word that ties it to the rest of the sentence, so it cannot stand alone: "because the power failed" leaves the reader waiting for more. Dependent clauses usually start with a subordinating conjunction such as after, as, before, once, since, until, or while, or with a relative pronoun such as who, which, or that. The key skill for sentence structure is spotting which clauses are independent; the four sentence types are simply the possible ways of combining them.

### Four sentence types

A simple sentence has one independent clause: "The ferry leaves at noon." A compound sentence has two or more independent clauses joined by a comma and a coordinating conjunction, or by a semicolon: "The ferry leaves at noon, but the ticket booth opens earlier." A complex sentence has one independent clause and at least one dependent clause: "Because the forecast warned of fog, the ferry left an hour late." A compound-complex sentence has two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause: "After the fog cleared, the ferry departed, and the passengers cheered." Judge sentence type by counting clauses, not words: a long sentence can still be simple if it contains just one clause, and a short sentence can be compound if it packs two independent clauses together.

### Word order and joining clauses

English declarative sentences follow subject-verb-object order by default: the subject (the ferry), the verb (crosses), and the object (the bay). Modifiers and phrases attach around that core, which is why "Every morning the ferry crosses the wide bay" still has subject-verb-object at its center. Joining clauses follows two patterns. Coordinating conjunctions connect equal, independent clauses, and in formal writing a comma precedes the conjunction. Subordinating conjunctions and relative pronouns attach dependent clauses to independent ones; the dependent clause can come first, usually followed by a comma, or last, usually without one. Choosing between a compound and a complex sentence is a choice about emphasis: coordination gives two ideas equal weight, while subordination makes one idea the background for the other.

### Fragments, run-ons, and comma splices

A sentence fragment is an incomplete sentence: a piece missing a subject, a predicate, or both, often a dependent clause left to stand alone. "Because the bus was late" is a fragment; attach it to a nearby sentence: "We missed the first act because the bus was late." A run-on, also called a fused sentence, joins two independent clauses with no punctuation at all: "The rain stopped the match continued." A comma splice joins them with only a comma: "The rain stopped, the match continued." The standard fixes are the same for both problems: split the clauses into separate sentences, add a comma plus a coordinating conjunction, or join them with a semicolon. Which fix you choose depends on how closely you want the ideas connected.

### Sentence variety

Readability depends on variety as much as correctness. A paragraph of nothing but simple sentences feels choppy, while a paragraph of long compounds feels relentless. Skilled writers mix short simple sentences, which carry emphasis, with compound and complex sentences, which show how ideas relate. A useful revision habit: after drafting, read the paragraph aloud, notice where every sentence starts the same way or runs the same length, and rebuild one or two of them. Sentence variety is a matter of rhythm and emphasis rather than a fixed rule, so there is no single correct mix; the goal is prose that a reader can follow easily from one sentence to the next.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Clause:** A group of words containing a subject and a predicate.
- **Independent clause:** A clause that can stand alone as a complete sentence, such as "The bakery opens at six."
- **Dependent clause:** A clause that has a subject and a predicate but cannot stand alone because it begins with a subordinating word, such as "because the power failed."
- **Simple sentence:** A sentence made of exactly one independent clause.
- **Compound sentence:** A sentence with two or more independent clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or a semicolon.
- **Complex sentence:** A sentence with one independent clause and at least one dependent clause.
- **Compound-complex sentence:** A sentence with two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.
- **Coordinating conjunction:** A joining word (and, but, for, or, nor, so, yet) that connects equal elements, including independent clauses.
- **Subordinating conjunction:** A word such as after, because, or until that begins a dependent clause and ties it to an independent clause.
- **Sentence fragment:** An incomplete sentence, such as a dependent clause left to stand alone or a group of words missing a subject or a predicate.

## Eli-10

Think of a sentence as a structure you build from clauses. A clause is a group of words with a doer and an action, like "the ferry crosses the bay." Some clauses can stand by themselves, and those are independent. Others need a partner: "because the ferry crosses the bay" leaves you waiting for more. English has four basic sentence shapes. One clause alone is a simple sentence. Two independent clauses joined by and, but, or so make a compound sentence. One independent clause with a dependent clause attached makes a complex sentence, and both patterns together make a compound-complex sentence. Most English sentences put the doer first, then the action, then the thing acted on: subject, verb, object. If a group of words is missing a piece, like "because the bus was late," or crams two complete sentences together without punctuation, your ear will usually tell you something is off.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of independent clauses as adults and dependent clauses as children holding a parent's hand. Adults can walk anywhere alone. Children are fine too, but only while holding on: "because the bus was late" cannot cross the street by itself. FANBOYS conjunctions are like hand-holding between two adults; and, but, and so let two independent clauses walk together as a compound sentence.

The analogy has limits. Dependent clauses are not less important than independent ones; they often carry the most interesting information, like the reason something happened. Sentences are also not limited to two people: a complex sentence can hold several dependent clauses, and word order is more flexible than a line of walkers suggests.

## Worked Example

Here is a first draft: "The bus arrived late. The driver apologized. Passengers missed the morning ferry. Because the weather was terrible." Identify the problems. The first three sentences are simple sentences in a row; they are grammatically fine but choppy. The last group of words is a fragment: it begins with because, so it is a dependent clause with nothing to attach to. One revision: "The bus arrived late, but the driver apologized. Passengers missed the morning ferry because the weather was terrible." The first sentence is now compound, since two independent clauses are joined by but, and the second is complex, since one independent clause carries a dependent clause starting with because. The same facts, with clearer relationships.

## Common Mistakes

- **Letting a dependent clause stand alone, as in "Because the bus was late."** Attach it to an independent clause: "We missed the ferry because the bus was late."
- **Joining two independent clauses with only a comma, as in "The rain stopped, the match continued."** Add a coordinating conjunction after the comma: "The rain stopped, so the match continued."
- **Writing a fused run-on with no punctuation at all, as in "The rain stopped the match continued."** Split the clauses into two sentences, or join them with a comma and a conjunction or a semicolon.
- **Forgetting the comma before a coordinating conjunction that joins two independent clauses, as in "The ferry left at noon and the crew secured the deck."** Use the comma in formal writing: "The ferry left at noon, and the crew secured the deck."
- **Judging sentence type by length instead of clauses, calling any long sentence compound.** Count the clauses: a long sentence with one independent clause is still simple, and a short sentence with two independent clauses is compound.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent clause | Dependent clause | An independent clause can stand alone as a complete sentence; a dependent clause begins with a subordinating word and must attach to an independent clause. |
| Compound sentence | Complex sentence | A compound sentence joins two or more independent clauses of equal weight; a complex sentence combines one independent clause with a dependent clause that cannot stand alone. |
| Fragment | Run-on | A fragment is an incomplete sentence missing a subject or a predicate; a run-on crams two complete sentences together without proper punctuation. |

## Key Takeaway

English sentences are built from clauses, and the four sentence types are the possible combinations of independent and dependent clauses; master that pattern and you can fix fragments, run-ons, and comma splices and write with variety and control.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which group of words is an independent clause?**
   - A. after the train left the station
   - B. the bakery opens at six o'clock
   - C. because the power failed
   - D. which surprised everyone
   - **Answer:** B. An independent clause has a subject and a predicate and can stand alone, which "the bakery opens at six o'clock" does. The other options begin with a subordinating conjunction (after, because) or a relative pronoun (which), so they are dependent clauses that cannot stand alone.

2. **Which sentence type has exactly one independent clause and at least one dependent clause?**
   - A. simple
   - B. compound
   - C. complex
   - D. compound-complex
   - **Answer:** C. A complex sentence contains one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause. A simple sentence has one independent clause only, a compound sentence has two or more independent clauses, and a compound-complex sentence has two or more independent clauses plus a dependent clause.

3. **Which revision correctly fixes the comma splice in "The rain stopped, the match continued"?**
   - A. The rain stopped, so the match continued.
   - B. The rain stopped the match continued.
   - C. The rain stopped, the match continued.
   - D. The rain stopped because the match continued.
   - **Answer:** A. A comma alone cannot join two independent clauses. Adding the coordinating conjunction so after the comma fixes the splice. The second option is a fused run-on with no punctuation, the third repeats the splice, and the fourth changes the meaning by making the match a result of the rain.

4. **Which word is a coordinating conjunction (one of the FANBOYS)?**
   - A. although
   - B. because
   - C. whenever
   - D. yet
   - **Answer:** D. Yet is one of the seven coordinating conjunctions (for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) that join equal elements such as independent clauses. Although, because, and whenever are subordinating conjunctions, which introduce dependent clauses instead.

5. **What sentence type is "After the alarm rang, Sam packed his bag, and the taxi arrived"?**
   - A. simple
   - B. compound-complex
   - C. complex
   - D. compound
   - **Answer:** B. The sentence contains the dependent clause "After the alarm rang" plus two independent clauses, "Sam packed his bag" and "the taxi arrived," joined by and. Two or more independent clauses plus at least one dependent clause is the compound-complex pattern.
