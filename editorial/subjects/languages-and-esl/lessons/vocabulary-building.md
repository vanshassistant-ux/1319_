# Vocabulary Building

## In 30 Seconds

Vocabulary is the set of words you know and use, and it is key to reading comprehension: you cannot understand a text without knowing what most of its words mean. You can grow it deliberately. Meet new words while reading and listening, understand them with dictionaries and word parts, use them in your own sentences, and review them from a word journal. Learn words in chunks and word families, not in isolation. A few words used well beat a long list memorized once.

## Why This Matters

Every sentence you read, hear, write, or speak is built from words, so the size and precision of your word knowledge shapes how well you understand others and how clearly you express yourself. Reading specialists describe vocabulary as key to comprehension — readers cannot understand a text without knowing what most of its words mean. Because most words are learned indirectly through wide reading and listening, everyday input already grows your vocabulary; deliberate habits — noticing new words, learning their parts, recording them, and reviewing them — make that growth faster and more reliable. These habits carry over to lectures, textbooks, workplaces, and conversations, and they prepare you for later topics such as academic vocabulary and reading comprehension.

## The College Version

### Why vocabulary deserves deliberate attention

Vocabulary knowledge is central to reading comprehension. Reading specialists put it plainly: readers cannot understand what they are reading without knowing what most of the words mean. The same logic extends to listening, writing, and speaking — every act of communication runs on word knowledge. Research on vocabulary instruction shows that most words are learned indirectly, through daily encounters with spoken and written language, while some words must be taught directly. Wide reading and listening therefore do most of the work; deliberate attention does the rest, and it matters most for exactly the words that do not show up often enough to teach themselves.

### The word-learning cycle

A practical framework for deliberate growth has four steps: encounter, understand, use, and review. You encounter a new word in something you read or hear; understanding comes when you check its meaning — in a dictionary, from its word parts, or from the surrounding text; use means putting the word into your own speaking and writing, which turns passive recognition into active command; review means coming back to the word later, because a single meeting rarely sticks. The steps feed one another: each use and review strengthens the next encounter. The cycle also matches how reading research describes learning — most vocabulary grows through repeated encounters with language, and recording words and revisiting them supports memory.

### Words in chunks: collocations and phrases

Words rarely travel alone. A collocation is a combination of words that are often used together in a way that sounds natural: speakers say *make a decision*, not *do a decision*; *heavy traffic*, not *weighty traffic*; *catch a cold*, not *receive a cold*. Collocations range from fixed, where there is little choice about the partner word, to freer, where more options exist. Learning a word together with its usual partners is more useful than learning it alone, because that is the shape in which the word actually appears. Teachers recommend recording collocations in a notebook — the word, its partners, and an example sentence — so that each entry shows the word at work.

### Word families and word parts

English words are built from parts. The base word, or root, carries the core meaning; prefixes attach to the front and change the meaning — *un-* reverses or negates (*unkind*, *unlock*), *re-* means again (*rebuild*, *retell*), *dis-* reverses (*disconnect*, *disagree*), and *inter-* means between (*intercity*, *international*). Suffixes attach to the end and usually change the meaning or the part of speech: *-tion* often turns verbs into nouns (*celebrate* becomes *celebration*, *inform* becomes *information*), and *-ful* often turns words into adjectives (*care* becomes *careful*, *colour* becomes *colourful*). Words built from the same base form a family: *view*, *viewer*, *review*, and *preview* all carry the idea of seeing. Recognizing parts multiplies the words you can figure out, because morphology — how words are built from roots, prefixes, and suffixes — shows how words relate to one another.

### High-frequency, academic, and technical words

Vocabulary is often described in layers. High-frequency words — the everyday core such as *book*, *eat*, and *happy* — do most of the work in ordinary conversation and general reading. Academic words appear across many school and workplace subjects: the Academic Word List, developed by Averil Coxhead, contains 570 word families that sit outside the most frequent 2,000 words of English and were chosen as the words learners most need for tertiary study. Technical words belong to one field, such as *photosynthesis* in biology or *leverage* in finance, and word lists are also built for specific purposes, such as English for particular professions. The layers guide where to spend effort: the everyday core comes first, academic words repay attention as your study deepens — a topic of its own lesson — and technical words matter most inside their own fields.

### Tools and habits: dictionaries, word journals, and realistic review

Two tools make the cycle practical. A good learner's dictionary gives the meaning, the part of speech, pronunciation, and example sentences, and it records which words travel together. A word journal is your personal record: for each new word, note the meaning in your own words, a sentence that shows the word at work, its usual partners, and its word-family relatives. Realistic goals beat heroic ones — a small number of words used and reviewed beats a long list skimmed once. Review is the step learners skip most often, yet it is where memory is won: revisiting words after a few days, then again later, a habit often called spaced repetition, supports remembering far better than a single cramming session. Build the habit into your week, and let reading supply the encounters.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Vocabulary:** The words a person knows and uses, including words recognized when reading or listening and words produced when speaking or writing.
- **Word-learning cycle:** A four-step loop for learning a word deliberately: encounter it, understand its meaning, use it yourself, and review it later.
- **Collocation:** A combination of words that are often used together so that the pairing sounds natural, such as *make a decision* or *heavy traffic*.
- **Word family:** A group of words built from the same base word with different prefixes and suffixes, such as *view*, *viewer*, *review*, and *preview*.
- **Base word (root):** The core part of a word to which prefixes and suffixes attach, such as *view* in *review* or *act* in *react*.
- **Prefix:** An affix attached to the beginning of a base word that changes its meaning, such as *re-* in *rebuild* or *un-* in *unkind*.
- **Suffix:** An affix attached to the end of a base word that usually changes its meaning or part of speech, such as *-tion* in *celebration* or *-ful* in *careful*.
- **Morphology:** The study of how words are built from roots, prefixes, and suffixes, and of how words relate to one another.
- **Academic vocabulary:** Words that appear across many school and workplace subjects, distinct from everyday high-frequency words and from field-specific technical terms.
- **Word journal:** A personal notebook in which a learner records new words with meanings, example sentences, usual partners, and related forms.

## Eli-10

Your vocabulary is the set of words you know, and it does a huge job: you cannot understand a text without knowing what most of its words mean. The good news is that you are already learning words all the time — mostly without trying, through everything you read and hear. To grow on purpose, run a simple loop: meet a new word, figure out what it means, use it in a sentence of your own, and come back to it later. Learn words with their friends: *make a decision* and *heavy traffic* stick better than *decision* and *traffic* alone. Look inside the word too — *un-* flips meaning (*unkind*), *re-* means again (*rebuild*), *-tion* makes nouns (*celebration*), *-ful* makes adjectives (*careful*) — and whole families follow from one base (*view*, *viewer*, *review*). Keep a small journal, review it after a few days, and let reading supply new words. A few words you actually use beat a long list you met once.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of your vocabulary as a toolbox you carry everywhere. Reading and listening drop new tools into it for free; the cycle — encounter, understand, use, review — is you taking each tool out, checking what it is for, using it, and putting it back in a spot you will remember. Words learned in chunks are the multi-tools: one entry, many uses.

The analogy has limits. A tool does not change meaning depending on the job, but a word can shift meaning with context, and word parts do not always add up neatly. Unlike a tool you can grab once, a word needs repeated use and review before it is truly yours.

## Worked Example

Meet the word *sustainable* in a news article: "The report argues that the current farming methods are not sustainable." **Encounter:** you have seen the word before but could not define it. **Understand:** the dictionary says able to continue without being exhausted or damaged, and the word parts agree — *sustain*, meaning to keep going or support, plus the suffix *-able*, meaning able to. **Use:** you write in your journal, "sustainable — able to keep going; our library's plan to buy used furniture is sustainable," and you note the family: *sustain*, *sustained*, *sustainable*, *sustainability*. **Review:** three days later you reread the entry, and that weekend you use the word in a conversation about the cost of textbooks. Encounter, understand, use, review — the same word has moved from unknown to usable.

## Common Mistakes

- **Learning words from long alphabetical lists.** Words memorized without context rarely stick or transfer. Learn words in chunks and sentences instead, and let reading and listening supply most encounters.
- **Studying words one at a time, in isolation.** A word's partners and relatives carry much of its use. Record collocations such as *make a decision* and family members such as *decide*, *decision*, and *decisive* alongside the word.
- **Looking a word up and never meeting it again.** A single dictionary check is only the understand step. Use the word in a sentence and review it from your journal, or the encounter fades.
- **Ignoring word parts.** Words like *unkind*, *rebuild*, and *celebration* look harder than they are. Recognizing *un-*, *re-*, *dis-*, *-tion*, and *-ful* lets one known base unlock many words.
- **Setting impossible daily quotas.** Aiming for fifty words a day usually produces fifty forgotten words. A few words used and reviewed over several days outweigh a long list met once.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High-frequency vocabulary | Academic vocabulary | High-frequency words are the everyday core (*book*, *eat*, *happy*) that carries most ordinary communication; academic words such as *analyse* and *methodology* appear across many school and workplace subjects and are covered in their own lesson. |
| Academic vocabulary | Technical vocabulary | Academic words show up across many fields — *methodology* appears in science, business, and history papers alike — while technical words belong to one field, such as *photosynthesis* in biology or *leverage* in finance. |
| A single word | A collocation | A single word learned alone, such as *decision*, gives you a label; the collocation *make a decision* gives you the word in the shape it actually appears in, which is how speakers expect to meet it. |
| Encounter | Review | Encountering a word means meeting it in reading or listening; reviewing means returning to it later. Encounter supplies the raw material, and review is what turns a meeting into a memory. |

## Key Takeaway

Vocabulary is key to comprehension, and it grows fastest when you make it deliberate: learn words in chunks and families, and move each new word through the cycle of encounter, understand, use, and review.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What is a collocation?**
   - A. A combination of words that are often used together in a way that sounds natural
   - B. A word formed by joining a prefix and a suffix to a root
   - C. A sentence that defines an unfamiliar word directly
   - D. A group of words built from the same base word
   - **Answer:** A. A collocation is a habitual pairing such as *make a decision* or *heavy traffic*, which is how the dictionary defines the term. Forming words from parts, defining words in sentences, and grouping words by a shared base are related but different ideas.

2. **Which group of words forms a word family built from the same base?**
   - A. *fast*, *faster*, *fastest*
   - B. *hot*, *hotter*, *hottest*
   - C. *view*, *viewer*, *review*, *preview*
   - D. *apple*, *banana*, *orange*, *mango*
   - **Answer:** C. *View* is the shared base: *viewer* adds the suffix *-er*, *review* adds the prefix *re-*, and *preview* adds *pre-*. The other options are comparative forms of one adjective, a list of fruits, or forms of a single verb — not a family built from one base with affixes.

3. **If you know that the prefix *re-* means "again," which word most likely means "to build again"?**
   - A. *unbuild*
   - B. *rebuild*
   - C. *prebuild*
   - D. *subbuild*
   - **Answer:** B. *Re-* attaches to the front of the base *build* and signals doing it again, so *rebuild* means to build again. *Un-* reverses or negates, *pre-* means before, and *sub-* means under or below — none of which fits doing the action again.

4. **A learner reads the word "sustainable" in a report, checks its meaning in a dictionary, and writes a sentence of her own using it. Which step of the word-learning cycle is still missing?**
   - A. Encounter
   - B. Understand
   - C. Use
   - D. Review
   - **Answer:** D. Reading the word is the encounter, the dictionary check is understanding, and her own sentence is use. The cycle is incomplete until she returns to the word later, because review is what turns a single meeting into a lasting memory.

5. **Which word belongs to the high-frequency core of English rather than to academic or technical vocabulary?**
   - A. *photosynthesis*
   - B. *methodology*
   - C. *kitchen*
   - D. *analyse*
   - **Answer:** C. *Kitchen* is part of the everyday core that carries ordinary conversation and general reading. *Photosynthesis* is a field-specific science term, while *methodology* and *analyse* are academic words of the kind the Academic Word List collects because they appear across school and workplace subjects.
