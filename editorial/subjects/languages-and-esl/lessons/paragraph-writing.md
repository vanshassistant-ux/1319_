# Paragraph Writing

## In 30 Seconds

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that develops one main idea. It usually opens with a topic sentence that states that idea, continues with supporting sentences that add evidence, examples, and explanation, and ends with a concluding sentence that wraps up or links forward. Every sentence must serve the topic — that is unity. Clear ordering and transition words such as *first*, *next*, *however*, and *therefore* give the paragraph coherence.

## Why This Matters

Paragraphs are the basic building blocks of almost all academic and professional writing. Emails, discussion posts, reports, and essays are paragraphs arranged for a reader, so writing one well means being understood instead of being skimmed past. The paragraph also trains two skills at once: thinking, because you must decide what the single main idea is, and communicating, because you must order and connect sentences so a reader can follow. Every later writing task — the essay, the research paper, the work report — assumes you can handle a paragraph. Master this unit and the larger forms become a matter of arrangement rather than struggle.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a paragraph and identify its three main parts: the topic sentence, supporting sentences, and the concluding sentence.
- Explain what makes an effective topic sentence, including the main idea and the controlling idea.
- Apply the unity test to judge whether every sentence in a paragraph supports its topic.
- Use transition words such as *first*, *next*, *however*, and *therefore* to make a paragraph coherent.
- Distinguish a fully developed paragraph from an underdeveloped one.
- Apply a simple paragraph-writing process: brainstorm, write a topic sentence, add support, and review.

## The College Version

### What a paragraph is

A paragraph is a group of related sentences that develops one main idea. The basic rule of paragraphing is to keep one idea to one paragraph: when you begin a new idea, you start a new paragraph. Within that limit there is room to work: a paragraph can carry a single idea supported by several details, or several related points that all serve the same overall topic. What it cannot do is wander. Paragraph breaks also help the reader by dividing writing into units with a clear job.

### The topic sentence

The topic sentence states the main idea of the paragraph. It usually comes first, and in most academic writing that is where readers expect it: the opening sentence tells them what the paragraph will do. An effective topic sentence does two jobs. It names the main idea — the subject the paragraph discusses — and it adds a controlling idea, the writer's specific stance on that subject. Consider "Learning to cook a few simple meals saves money, improves health, and builds confidence." The main idea is learning to cook; the controlling idea is the claim that it saves money, improves health, and builds confidence. That claim is what the rest of the paragraph must support. A topic sentence without a controlling idea, such as "This paragraph is about cooking," tells the reader almost nothing.

### Supporting sentences

The body of the paragraph is made of supporting sentences. Their job is to develop the topic sentence by giving evidence, examples, and explanation. Evidence brings facts and data: "A family that cooks at home three nights a week spends about half as much on food as one that eats takeout." Examples make the idea concrete: "A pot of dried beans costs about a dollar and feeds four people." Explanation gives the reasons: "Home cooking saves money because raw ingredients cost less than the labor built into restaurant prices." Supporting sentences can also use short stories, definitions, and what other people say. The point is that each supporting sentence does work for the topic sentence; if it does not, it belongs somewhere else. When readers no longer ask "Why?" or "For example?", the paragraph is adequately developed.

### The concluding sentence

The concluding sentence closes the paragraph. Its job is to remind the reader of the point, usually by restating the topic sentence in different words. Where the topic sentence said "Learning to cook a few simple meals saves money, improves health, and builds confidence," the concluding sentence might say "Between the money saved and the skills gained, an hour in the kitchen each week pays for itself many times over." A concluding sentence can also link forward, preparing the reader for what comes next. It should never introduce a brand-new idea; a new idea is a signal that the paragraph should have ended earlier.

### Unity: the key test

Unity means the entire paragraph concerns itself with a single focus. The test is simple to run: read each sentence and ask what it does for the main idea. If a sentence supports the topic, it stays; if it is merely interesting, it goes or moves to a paragraph where it belongs. Consider a paragraph whose topic sentence promises that the library's evening hours matter to working parents. A sentence about the library's brick exterior, however charming, breaks that promise. The reader notices the break and loses trust in the organization. Writers catch most unity problems during review, which is why the final pass over a draft is where the unity test actually happens.

### Coherence and transitions

Coherence is what makes a paragraph easy to follow. It is built from two things: order and connection. Order the ideas so each sentence leads naturally into the next — chronological order for a process, claim-to-evidence order for an argument. Then connect sentences with transition words, which tell the reader how ideas relate. Sequence: "First, gather the ingredients; next, chop the vegetables." Contrast: "The recipe looks long; however, most of the time is simmering, not work." Result: "Dried beans need no refrigeration; therefore, they suit a student pantry perfectly." Repeating key words, using synonyms, and using pronouns that point back to earlier nouns also tie sentences together. Transitions are not decoration: a paragraph that jumps between ideas without them forces the reader to do the connecting work alone.

### Fully developed or underdeveloped

A fully developed paragraph answers the reader's questions: a claim, evidence or examples, explanation, and a wrap-up. An underdeveloped paragraph makes its claim and stops — often two or three sentences that leave the reader asking "Why?" or "So what?". Length is not the real goal. A long paragraph that keeps introducing new ideas is usually two paragraphs wearing one paragraph's clothes, and it should be split where the new idea begins. The real measure of development is whether the support is enough to make the topic sentence believable.

### A simple paragraph-writing process

Writing a paragraph is a process, not a single act. First, brainstorm: generate ideas by freewriting, listing, or asking questions, then choose the one main idea and the stance you will take. Second, write the topic sentence: state the main idea and controlling idea in one clear sentence. Third, add support: draft the supporting sentences, giving evidence, examples, and explanation, and check each one against the topic. Fourth, review: reread for unity (every sentence on topic), coherence (clear order and transitions), development (enough support), and a concluding sentence that wraps up. The four steps are a habit; with practice they run together.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Paragraph:** A group of related sentences that develops a single main idea.
- **Topic sentence:** The sentence, usually at the beginning, that states the main idea a paragraph will develop.
- **Main idea:** The subject a paragraph discusses, stated in its topic sentence.
- **Controlling idea:** The writer's specific stance on the main idea, expressed in the topic sentence.
- **Supporting sentences:** The sentences in the body of a paragraph that explain, prove, or enhance its topic sentence with evidence, examples, and explanation.
- **Concluding sentence:** The final sentence of a paragraph, which restates the main point in different words or links to what comes next.
- **Unity:** The quality of a paragraph in which every sentence supports the single focus stated in the topic sentence.
- **Coherence:** The quality that makes a paragraph easy to follow, created by clear ordering and connecting words.
- **Transition:** A word or phrase, such as *first*, *next*, *however*, or *therefore*, that links ideas and shows how they relate.
- **Adequate development:** A paragraph has adequate development when its evidence, examples, and explanation fully support the topic sentence.

## Eli-10

A paragraph is a small team of sentences with one job: to explain one main idea. The topic sentence is the leader. It announces the idea in one sentence, and a good one adds your opinion about it, not just the subject. The supporting sentences do the work. They give evidence, examples, and explanation so the idea is believable. The concluding sentence closes the team's report: it reminds the reader of the point, usually in different words. Two qualities decide whether the paragraph works. Unity means every sentence pulls in the same direction — if a sentence is not working for the main idea, it does not belong. Coherence means a reader can follow the order, helped by transition words: *first* and *next* for sequence, *however* for contrast, *therefore* for a result. There is no magic length; a paragraph is developed when it answers the reader's questions.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a paragraph as a small team working on one task. The topic sentence sets the goal for the team. The supporting sentences do the work, gathering evidence, giving examples, and explaining. The concluding sentence reports what the team accomplished. A team that stops to chat about yesterday's game is not working on the task, just as a paragraph that wanders off topic has lost its unity.

The analogy has limits. A team can change tasks mid-project or reorganize itself, but a paragraph's sentences keep one fixed order and one fixed job, chosen by the writer in advance. And a team can negotiate and improvise; a paragraph cannot — every connection between its sentences has to be built in at the writing stage.

## Worked Example

Task: write a paragraph explaining why the neighborhood library matters. Brainstorm: free books, computers, quiet space, events, tutoring. Topic sentence: "The neighborhood library matters because it gives residents free access to books, computers, and community events." Support: "Students without internet at home use the library's computers for homework and job applications." (example) "Last year the library's summer reading program served more than three hundred children." (evidence) "Because the space is free and quiet, it also hosts study groups and small community meetings." (explanation) Concluding sentence: "For a service that costs residents nothing, the library returns a great deal to the neighborhood." Review: every sentence supports the topic sentence, the transitions (*because*, *also*) connect the ideas, and the conclusion restates the main point in different words.

## Common Mistakes

- **Writing a topic sentence with no controlling idea**, such as "This paragraph is about exercise." Add a specific claim the paragraph can support: "Thirty minutes of daily exercise improves sleep quality within a few weeks."
- **Keeping an off-topic sentence because it is interesting**, such as a fact about the library's building in a paragraph about library programs. Apply the unity test: if a sentence does not support the topic sentence, cut it or move it to a paragraph where it belongs.
- **Using a transition word whose meaning does not match the relationship**, as in "The trail was steep; therefore, the view was worth the climb." Match the word to the logic: *however* for contrast, *therefore* for a result, *first* and *next* for sequence. The steep trail and the fine view contrast, so the sentence needs *however*.
- **Ending the paragraph by introducing a brand-new idea** in the concluding sentence. Restate the main point in different words, or link forward to the next paragraph — do not start a new topic.
- **Stopping after two or three sentences**, so the paragraph states a claim but never develops it. Add evidence, examples, or explanation until the topic sentence is adequately supported; a very short paragraph is usually an underdeveloped one.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic sentence | Concluding sentence | The topic sentence opens the paragraph by stating the main idea; the concluding sentence closes it by restating that idea in different words or linking forward. |
| Paragraph | Essay | A paragraph develops one main idea in a group of sentences; an essay develops a thesis across several related paragraphs — the essay-writing topic covers that larger structure. |
| Unified paragraph | Wandering paragraph | In a unified paragraph every sentence supports the topic; a wandering paragraph includes off-topic material that should be cut or moved. |
| Fully developed paragraph | Underdeveloped paragraph | A fully developed paragraph answers the reader's questions with evidence, examples, and explanation; an underdeveloped one makes its claim and stops. |

## Key Takeaway

A strong paragraph develops one main idea: the topic sentence states it, supporting sentences prove it, and the concluding sentence closes it, held together by unity and coherence.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best describes a paragraph?**
   - A. A group of related sentences that develops one main idea.
   - B. Any collection of sentences of any length.
   - C. A single sentence that states a complete thought.
   - D. A group of sentences about several different topics.
   - **Answer:** A. A paragraph is a group of related sentences dealing with a single topic — one main idea per paragraph. The other options miss the two defining features: the sentences are related, and they develop one idea.

2. **Which sentence is the strongest topic sentence for a paragraph about the benefits of walking?**
   - A. Walking is a popular activity.
   - B. Many people enjoy walking in parks.
   - C. Walking thirty minutes a day lowers stress and strengthens the heart.
   - D. I bought comfortable shoes for walking.
   - **Answer:** C. The strongest topic sentence names the main idea (walking) and adds a controlling idea — the specific claim that it lowers stress and strengthens the heart. The other options state vague subjects or minor details without a claim the paragraph can support.

3. **Read this paragraph: [1] The city should extend the library's evening hours. [2] Working parents would finally have time to borrow books after dinner. [3] The library's brick exterior was repainted in 2019. [4] Students with day jobs could use the quiet study room at night. Which sentence breaks the paragraph's unity?**
   - A. Sentence 1
   - B. Sentence 3
   - C. Sentence 4
   - D. Sentence 2
   - **Answer:** B. Sentence 3 is about the building's exterior, which does not support the topic — extending evening hours to serve working parents and students. Sentences 1, 2, and 4 all serve that topic, so sentence 3 fails the unity test and should be cut or moved.

4. **Which sentence uses a transition word correctly to show contrast?**
   - A. The trail was steep; therefore, the view was worth the climb.
   - B. The trail was steep; first, the view was worth the climb.
   - C. The trail was steep; however, the view was worth the climb.
   - D. The trail was steep; next, the climb took two hours.
   - **Answer:** C. *However* signals contrast, and the sentence contrasts a difficult climb with a worthwhile view. *Therefore* signals a result, while *first* and *next* signal sequence, so those options do not match the contrast between steepness and reward.

5. **A student's draft paragraph is about library evening programs, but one sentence describes the history of the library building. Applying the unity test, what should the student do?**
   - A. Rewrite the topic sentence so the paragraph covers both subjects.
   - B. Add more historical details so the paragraph feels balanced.
   - C. Delete the topic sentence and let readers identify the main idea.
   - D. Move the history sentence into a paragraph where it supports the topic.
   - **Answer:** D. The unity test says every sentence must support the paragraph's one main idea. The history sentence is off-topic here, so it should move to a paragraph about the building — broadening the topic sentence would split the paragraph into two ideas, and deleting the topic sentence would leave readers without a guide.
