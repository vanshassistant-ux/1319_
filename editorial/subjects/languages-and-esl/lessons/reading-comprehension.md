# Reading Comprehension

## In 30 Seconds

Reading comprehension is building meaning from a written text, and it works best when you read actively: predict what is coming, ask questions, and connect the text to what you already know. Match your approach to your purpose — skim for the gist, scan for one specific fact, read closely when details matter. Preview headings, annotate key ideas, summarize paragraphs, and reread what is unclear. Notice how the text is built: the topic sentence carries the main idea, and transitions show how ideas connect.

## Why This Matters

Almost everything a college student learns arrives through reading — textbook chapters, research articles, lab instructions, assignment prompts. Readers who match their approach to their purpose finish more in less time and remember more of what they read. Beyond college, the same habits carry into work: contracts, reports, and instructions all demand that you find the key point, notice the supporting evidence, and catch what changes the meaning. Reading comprehension is not a fixed talent; it is a set of moves — predicting, questioning, connecting, rereading — that anyone can practice. This lesson builds the framework; context clues and vocabulary building handle individual words, and paragraph writing shows how the structures described here are produced.

## Learning Objectives

- Define reading comprehension as an active process of building meaning from a written text.
- Distinguish skimming, scanning, and close reading, and match each approach to a reading purpose.
- Identify topic sentences, main ideas, supporting details, and transitions in a paragraph.
- Apply previewing, annotating, summarizing, and rereading to a reading task.
- Analyze common comprehension problems and choose practical fixes for them.

## The College Version

### Reading is an active process

Reading comprehension is the process of building meaning from a written text. The word *building* matters: meaning is not inside the text, waiting to be picked up. Readers construct it by combining what the words and sentences say with what they already know. Reading instruction keeps returning to the same advice: read actively. An active reader engages with the text like a conversation: posing questions, seeking answers, and clarifying confusing elements rather than letting the eyes move over words.

The practical moves are easy to name. **Predict** what a text will be about before and while you read, using titles, headings, and what you already know about the topic. **Question** as you go: turn headings into questions, ask what the writer's main point is, and ask what the writer may be leaving out. **Connect** what you read to earlier parts of the text, to the course, and to your own experience. These moves turn reading into thinking with a text — which is why active reading takes longer than a casual pass and produces so much more understanding.

### Reading for a purpose: skimming, scanning, and close reading

Not all reading is the same, and the first decision a good reader makes is why they are reading. Do you need the details that determine cause and effect, or only a general familiarity with the topic? The answer sets the approach.

**Skimming** is moving quickly through a text to take in its major points — the gist — using the guides writers leave behind: headings, definitions, pull quotes, tables, and context clues, plus introductory and concluding paragraphs. Skimming is not a rushed substitute for reading; it is a first pass that tells you whether the text deserves your time. **Scanning** is searching for one specific piece of information — a date, a name, a figure, a word — while ignoring the rest. A reader who needs the year a policy changed scans for the number rather than reading the report. **Close reading** is the careful end of the scale: attending to details, language, and structure and interpreting what they do. It moves beyond literal summary, notices patterns, and supports an interpretation with textual evidence. The three approaches are not rivals; they are different gears for different purposes, and one session may shift among them.

### How texts are built: topic sentences, main ideas, and transitions

Texts are easier to understand when you see how they are organized. The paragraph is the basic unit, and most paragraphs are built around a main idea. The **topic sentence** states, in a general way, what idea the paragraph is going to deal with. It often appears near the beginning, though it can appear elsewhere; what matters is that the paragraph develops it. Everything else in the paragraph is **supporting detail**: examples, data, testimony, reasons, and explanations that develop the main idea.

An effective paragraph also has unity — a single focus — and coherence: sentences that connect. Coherence is largely built with **transitions** — words and phrases such as *however*, *for example*, and *in addition* — and signposts that tell readers where the text has been and where it is going. Find the topic sentence and you have the main idea; see how the details support it and the transitions connect it, and you can follow an argument paragraph by paragraph. The paragraph-writing lesson shows how these structures are produced.

### Strategies that keep comprehension on track

Beyond matching your approach to your purpose, several strategies protect comprehension.

- **Preview** before you read: check the title, headings, summaries, and captions, and form expectations — the survey step of SQ3R (survey, question, read, recite, review).
- **Annotate** while you read: mark selectively, writing questions in the margins, noting key ideas, and flagging what you do not understand. Annotation works when it captures your thinking in the moment; it fails when it becomes aimless highlighting.
- **Summarize** as you go: after a paragraph or a section, restate the main points in your own words. A summary is a condensed version of a longer text that reviews its main ideas, and writing one forces you to check that you hold them.
- **Reread** when the thread slips: reading for comprehension is recursive, and going back over a confusing passage is the normal move — college reading is rereading, with the intention of improving comprehension and taking notes.
- **Use headings and visuals**: they often name main ideas and give you a map of the text as you read.

### Common comprehension problems and practical fixes

Three habits cause most avoidable comprehension problems.

The first is **reading every word at the same slow speed**. The belief that you must read every word — and that reading faster means understanding less — is exactly what skimming-and-scanning training was designed to correct. The fix: decide your purpose first, and let it set your pace.

The second is **stopping cold at unfamiliar vocabulary** or translating word by word into another language. Stopping breaks the sentence, and word-by-word translation slows reading to a crawl; second-language reading research shows rates differ sharply by task. The fix: keep moving, form a working sense of the word from context clues, and check a dictionary when the word matters (the context-clues lesson covers that skill).

The third is **reading a text exactly once**, from top to bottom, and expecting full understanding. The fix: reread strategically — once for the shape of the text, again for the parts that matter — and summarize in your own words to confirm what you understood. None of these fixes is exotic; they are the active-reading moves above, applied deliberately.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Reading comprehension:** The process of building meaning from a written text by connecting its words, sentences, and structure to what the reader already knows.
- **Active reading:** Reading with deliberate engagement — predicting, asking questions, noting reactions, and checking understanding as you go.
- **Skimming:** Moving quickly through a text to take in its major points, using headings, first and last paragraphs, and key sentences.
- **Scanning:** Searching a text for one specific piece of information, such as a date, name, or figure, without reading everything.
- **Close reading:** Reading carefully and deliberately to notice details, language, and structure and to interpret what they mean.
- **Topic sentence:** The sentence that states, in a general way, what idea the paragraph is going to deal with.
- **Main idea:** The central point of a paragraph or passage, which the supporting details develop.
- **Supporting detail:** A fact, example, reason, or explanation that develops the main idea of a paragraph.
- **Transition:** A word or phrase such as "however" or "for example" that links ideas between sentences or paragraphs.
- **Annotate:** To mark a text with notes, questions, and highlights that capture your thinking while reading.

## Eli-10

Reading in English is not about decoding every word in order. Think of a text as a building you are walking through. Before you enter, you can look at the outside — the title, the headings, the pictures — and predict what is inside. That is previewing. Once inside, you move differently depending on why you came. If you need to know whether the building is worth touring, you walk the main corridors and glance at the room names: that is skimming. If you came for one specific thing, like the library's opening hours, you head straight for the sign that says it: that is scanning. If you came to study the architecture, you stop in each room, look at the materials, and take notes: that is close reading. Most reading days mix all three. The reader decides which to use, and the decision comes from the purpose. When a passage confuses you, go back and walk it again — rereading is not a failure; it is how meaning gets built.

## Eli's Analogy

Reading is like walking through a building you have never visited. You glance at the front to know what it is, walk the main hall to get the feel of the place, step into one room when you need something specific, and linger in the rooms you came to study. You would not photograph every wall on every visit; you choose your pace by your errand.

The analogy has limits. A building is fixed, but a text needs the reader to build its meaning — the understanding does not exist until someone actively constructs it. And unlike a hallway you have already left, a text can always be revisited: rereading and jumping between sections are normal moves, not detours.

## Worked Example

A student must answer three questions about a two-page article titled "How Farmers Are Adapting to Warmer Winters." First she previews: the headings mention new crop varieties, earlier planting, and water storage, so she predicts the article is about practical adjustments. To answer "What is the article's main point?" she skims — the first paragraph says farmers are treating the changing climate as a planning problem, not a catastrophe. To find "In what year did the pilot orchard program begin?" she scans for a year and lands on "2019" in the third section. To answer "Explain one risk the author identifies," she reads that section closely and underlines the sentence about frost damaging early blossoms. She finishes by writing a one-sentence summary of each paragraph, then checks her answers against her notes. One reading session, three different speeds — each chosen by the question.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading every word at the same slow speed, as if understanding required one pace.** Set your pace by your purpose. Skim for the gist, scan for a specific fact, and read slowly only where the details matter.
- **Stopping to translate every unfamiliar word into your first language.** Keep moving. Use context clues for a working sense of the word, reread the sentence, and check a dictionary only when the word matters or repeats.
- **Highlighting or underlining huge stretches of the text.** Mark selectively: questions, key terms, and reactions. Bare highlighting without a purpose is not remembered, and notes in your own words do the real work.
- **Reading a text once, top to bottom, and expecting full understanding.** Reading for comprehension is recursive. Reread the parts that matter and summarize each section in your own words to confirm what you actually understood.
- **Ignoring headings, visuals, and the paragraph structure.** Use them. Headings and visuals often name main ideas, topic sentences carry the point of each paragraph, and transitions show how the ideas connect.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Skimming vs. Scanning:** Skimming moves through a text to take in its major points — the gist; scanning searches for one specific piece of information, such as a date or figure, and ignores the rest. One gives the shape of the whole; the other finds a single detail.
- **Main idea vs. Supporting details:** The main idea is the central point a paragraph deals with, usually announced by the topic sentence; supporting details are the examples, facts, and explanations that develop it. Understanding a paragraph means holding both at once.
- **Summarizing vs. Paraphrasing:** A summary condenses a text's main ideas into a shorter form in your own words; a paraphrase restates a passage at roughly the same length to clarify its meaning. Summarizing checks whether you hold the whole; paraphrasing checks whether you hold one part.

## Key Takeaway

Read actively and purposefully: preview before you read, match your speed to your goal, notice how the text is organized, and reread what matters — comprehension is built, not absorbed.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. Which reading approach is the best match when you need only the general sense of a long article before deciding whether to read it fully?**

A. Close reading of every paragraph in order
B. Skimming the headings, the first and last paragraphs, and key sentences
C. Scanning the article for one specific number
D. Reading the article aloud at a steady pace

**Correct answer: B.** Skimming takes in the major points of a passage using headings, first and last paragraphs, and key sentences, which is exactly what this task needs. Close reading, scanning for a single number, and reading aloud serve different purposes: detailed study, a specific fact, and oral practice.

**2. A student must find the exact year a program began by reading a five-page government report. Which approach fits that purpose?**

A. Skim the report's introduction for its overall argument
B. Read the whole report closely and take notes on every section
C. Scan the report for the year, moving quickly until the date appears
D. Recite each paragraph aloud before continuing

**Correct answer: C.** Scanning means searching a text for one specific piece of information — a date, name, or figure — without reading everything, which is the efficient move here. Skimming gives a general sense, close reading studies detail, and reciting is a memory aid, not a search method.

**3. Read this paragraph: "Public libraries do more than lend books. Many branches run job-skills workshops, host citizenship classes, and offer free internet access." Which sentence states the main idea?**

A. The first sentence, because it states in a general way what the paragraph deals with
B. The second sentence, because it lists the library's services
C. Both sentences, because every sentence in a paragraph states the main idea
D. Neither sentence, because paragraphs state main ideas only in their final sentence

**Correct answer: A.** A topic sentence indicates in a general way what idea the paragraph deals with, and here the first sentence does that; the rest of the paragraph develops it with examples. The second sentence supplies supporting details, and the other options misunderstand where and how often topic sentences appear.

**4. A student highlights three full pages of a chapter, then later cannot remember why any of it was marked. What went wrong, and what is the better habit?**

A. She read too slowly; the better habit is skimming the entire chapter
B. Highlighting is never useful; the better habit is never marking a text
C. She chose the wrong chapter; the better habit is reading a different source
D. She marked without a purpose; the better habit is annotating selectively — questions, key ideas, and reactions

**Correct answer: D.** Over-marking is the trap: marks and notes are a way to organize and make sense of information, and aimless highlighting is not remembered later. Selective annotation with questions and key ideas supports comprehension, while skimming everything, never marking, or changing sources does not fix the problem.

**5. Halfway through a difficult passage, a reader realizes she has lost the thread of the argument. Which move most directly restores comprehension?**

A. Going back to reread the confusing part, then restating its point in one sentence
B. Increasing her reading speed so the whole passage is finished sooner
C. Underlining every sentence she does not yet understand
D. Moving to a different article and returning to this one next week

**Correct answer: A.** Reading for comprehension is recursive: readers go back and reread passages to determine meaning, and restating the point in your own words checks understanding. Speeding up, marking everything, or abandoning the text does not rebuild the thread.
