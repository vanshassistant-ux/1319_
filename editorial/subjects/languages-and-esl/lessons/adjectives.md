# Adjectives

## In 30 Seconds

Adjectives describe nouns and pronouns. They can stand before the noun they modify — a wooden bench — or after a linking verb, as in the bench feels cold. Adjectives also compare: cheaper, cheapest; more careful, most careful; and irregular pairs like good, better, best. When several adjectives precede a noun, English usually follows a conventional order of opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, and material. Pairs such as interested in are fixed combinations, and an adjective must never be used where an adverb belongs.

## Why This Matters

Adjectives carry the detail that makes language precise: the difference between a report and a twelve-page report is a single adjective. In academic writing, adjectives describe evidence, scope, and results accurately, and choosing the right comparative or superlative shows whether you are comparing two things or describing a whole group. In everyday conversation, knowing which preposition follows an adjective — interested in, not interested on — keeps your meaning clear. The same skills prevent the most common modifier errors in learner English, such as placing an adjective where an adverb belongs. You will use adjectives constantly, from lab reports and job interviews to messages to colleagues, so the small choices covered here repay attention.

## Learning Objectives

- Define an adjective as a word that describes or gives information about a noun or pronoun.
- Distinguish attributive adjectives, which appear before the noun, from predicative adjectives, which follow a linking verb.
- Explain how regular adjectives form comparatives and superlatives and name the common irregular forms such as good, better, best.
- Apply the conventional order of multiple adjectives — opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material — in original sentences.
- Recognize that adjectives pair with specific prepositions and that an adjective is used only where the word it modifies is a noun or pronoun, not a verb.

## The College Version

### What adjectives do and where they sit

An adjective is a describing word whose target is always a noun or pronoun. It answers questions like which one, what kind, what color, and what shape: the twelve-page report, the wooden bench, a quiet lake. Adjectives reach their noun from two positions. In the attributive position they stand directly before the noun: a fragile vase, an old bridge. In the predicative position they follow a linking verb — is, seems, feels, looks, tastes, sounds, appears — and describe the subject: the vase is fragile, the bridge seems old. The linking verb matters because it changes what the describing word is doing: in "the dog seems calm," calm describes the dog, but in "the dog sniffed calmly," the describing word has become an adverb telling how the dog sniffed. Some words that behave like adjectives are classified separately in some grammar traditions — articles such as a and the are one example; that family belongs to the articles lesson, and this lesson only names it. The practical test for an adjective is always the same: find the noun or pronoun and ask which word is giving information about it.

### Comparing: comparatives and superlatives

Adjectives change form to express degree. The comparative form compares two people or things, and the superlative form compares one person or thing with a whole group. For most one-syllable adjectives, add -er for the comparative and -est for the superlative: cheap, cheaper, cheapest; tall, taller, tallest. Two-syllable adjectives that end in -y change the y to i: busy, busier, busiest; happy, happier, happiest. Longer adjectives of three or more syllables use more and most instead of endings: more interesting, the most interesting, with less and least for the negative direction. A few common adjectives are irregular and simply change words: good, better, best and bad, worse, worst, along with far, farther, farthest. Two cautions follow from the pattern. Never stack the two systems: more cheaper is redundant because cheaper already carries the comparison. And reserve the superlative for groups of three or more: "of the two routes, the coast road is shorter" compares two, while "of the three routes, the coast road is the shortest" compares the coast road with the group. The superlative normally appears with the: the tallest, the most expensive.

### Stacking adjectives: the conventional order

When several adjectives precede the same noun, English usually arranges them in a conventional sequence rather than a random one. The common guideline runs: opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material. Opinion comes first because it is the most subjective judgment, and the sequence then moves outward to the object's fixed facts: a delightful small antique round green Greek marble table. The order is a guideline, not a rigid law — full reference lists add categories such as physical quality, type, and purpose, and a speaker may deliberately break the pattern for emphasis. Two practical habits keep stacked adjectives natural. Adjectives of the same kind are joined with and, as in a black and white photo. And when the adjectives feel equally important, the familiar sequence matters most, because that is when a reader's ear expects it: a lovely red balloon sounds natural, while a red lovely balloon sounds scrambled even though both words describe the same noun.

### Fixed pairs and the adjective-adverb boundary

Some adjectives travel with a specific preposition, and there is no general rule for which one. You are interested in something, good at something, afraid of something, and worried about something. The combination is a fixed pair, so the reliable strategy is to learn the adjective together with its preposition and a short phrase, and to check a dictionary when unsure. The preposition in such pairs is itself followed by a noun or a gerund: interested in marine biology, good at fixing bicycles. The second boundary is the adjective-adverb line. Because adjectives describe nouns and pronouns, while adverbs describe verbs, adjectives, and other adverbs, a sentence that needs a manner word after an action verb must use the adverb: the choir sang beautifully, not the choir sang beautiful. Choosing an adjective where an adverb belongs is one of the most common learner errors in English, and the full rules for adverb forms belong to the adverbs lesson; here it is enough to recognize that the choice always depends on what the word is describing.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Adjective:** A word that gives information about a noun or pronoun, describing a quality such as size, age, color, or origin.
- **Modifier:** A word, phrase, or clause that adds detail to, limits, or clarifies the meaning of another word or group of words.
- **Attributive adjective:** An adjective placed directly before the noun it modifies, as in a wooden bench.
- **Predicative adjective:** An adjective that follows a linking verb and describes the subject, as in the bench feels cold.
- **Linking verb:** A verb such as be, seem, or feel that connects the subject to a word describing it.
- **Comparative form:** The form of an adjective used to compare two people or things, such as cheaper or more careful.
- **Superlative form:** The form of an adjective used to compare one person or thing with a group, such as cheapest or most careful.
- **Irregular adjective:** An adjective whose comparative and superlative forms do not follow the regular patterns, such as good, better, best.
- **Adjective order:** The conventional sequence — opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material — in which English usually places several adjectives before a noun.
- **Collocation:** A combination of words that speakers of a language regularly use together, such as the adjective and preposition pair interested in.

## Eli-10

Adjectives are describing words. Their one job is to add information to a noun or pronoun: which one, what kind, what color, what shape. They usually stand right before the noun — a warm coat — or after a linking verb like is, feels, or looks — the coat is warm. When you compare, most adjectives take -er and -est (tall, taller, tallest) or more and most (helpful, more helpful, most helpful), and a few well-known ones change completely: good, better, best and bad, worse, worst. If you put several adjectives in front of a noun, English expects a rough order: opinion first, then size, age, shape, color, origin, and material. Finally, some adjectives always travel with a specific preposition — you are interested in something and good at something — so learn them as a pair.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of an adjective as a spotlight on a stage. The noun is the actor, and the adjective decides which detail the light falls on. Point the same spotlight from different angles — tall, Italian, helpful — and each adjective shows a different feature. When several spotlights shine at once, the audience expects them in a familiar order, so the picture is easy to read.

The spotlight picture has limits. A spotlight can be moved freely, but an adjective cannot: English fixes its position before the noun or after a linking verb, and many adjectives change form when you compare. Spotlights also never pair with particular prepositions or verbs, while adjectives often do — interested in, good at — which is a constraint the analogy cannot show.

## Worked Example

You are editing a travel note: "We found a lovely small old round blue Mexican ceramic bowl in the market, and it was more cheaper than the one we saw in the city." Three fixes are needed. First, stack the adjectives in the conventional order: lovely (opinion), small (size), old (age), round (shape), blue (color), Mexican (origin), ceramic (material). Second, cheaper already carries the comparison, so more is redundant: it was cheaper. Third, decide the comparison's direction: if the bowl is compared with one other bowl, cheaper is right; if it beats every bowl in the market, use the superlative, the cheapest. The revised sentence reads: "We found a lovely small old round blue Mexican ceramic bowl in the market, and it was cheaper than the one we saw in the city."

## Common Mistakes

- **Using an adjective where an adverb belongs:** "The choir sang beautiful."
  - *Correction:* Beautiful describes nouns; to describe how the choir sang, use the adverb: "The choir sang beautifully." The adverbs lesson covers this distinction in full.
- **Stacking comparative markers:** "This route is more shorter than the other."
  - *Correction:* Shorter already carries the comparison, so more adds nothing: "This route is shorter than the other." Never combine more or most with an -er or -est form.
- **Putting several adjectives in random order:** "a red lovely balloon."
  - *Correction:* Follow the conventional order — opinion, size, age, shape, color, origin, material — so the phrase becomes "a lovely red balloon."
- **Guessing the preposition:** "She is interested on marine biology."
  - *Correction:* Each adjective has its own prepositions, and there is no rule to derive them. The fixed pair is interested in: "She is interested in marine biology."
- **Using the superlative for a comparison of two:** "Of the two apartments, this one is the cheapest."
  - *Correction:* Grammar guides traditionally reserve the superlative for groups of three or more. With two, use the comparative: "Of the two apartments, this one is the cheaper."

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Attributive adjective (the wooden bench) | Predicative adjective (the bench is wooden) | An attributive adjective stands directly before the noun it modifies, while a predicative adjective follows a linking verb and describes the subject. |
| Comparative (cheaper, more careful) | Superlative (cheapest, most careful) | The comparative compares two people or things; the superlative compares one person or thing with a group of three or more. |
| Adjective (a quiet library) | Adverb (she read quietly) | An adjective describes a noun or pronoun; an adverb describes a verb, adjective, or another adverb. Using one where the other belongs is a common learner error, and the adverbs lesson treats the full rules. |

## Key Takeaway

Adjectives describe nouns and pronouns: they sit before the noun or after a linking verb, compare with -er/-est or more/most, and follow a conventional order when several appear together.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which words do adjectives modify?**
   - A. verbs and other adjectives
   - B. nouns and pronouns
   - C. prepositions and conjunctions
   - D. whole clauses and sentences
   - **Answer:** B. Adjectives modify nouns and pronouns, adding detail such as size, color, or origin; verbs and other adjectives are modified by adverbs, and prepositions connect words rather than being described.

2. **In the sentence "The wooden bench felt cold in the shade," which word is a predicative adjective?**
   - A. wooden
   - B. bench
   - C. cold
   - D. shade
   - **Answer:** C. Cold follows the linking verb felt and describes the subject bench, so it is a predicative adjective; wooden is an attributive adjective because it stands directly before bench.

3. **Which sentence uses the correct comparative form?**
   - A. This trail is better than the other one.
   - B. This trail is gooder than the other one.
   - C. This trail is more good than the other one.
   - D. This trail is best than the other one.
   - **Answer:** A. Good is an irregular adjective: its comparative is better, not gooder or more good, and best is a superlative that compares with a group rather than with a single other trail.

4. **A shopkeeper's table is lovely, large, round, and wooden. Which sentence places the adjectives in the conventional English order?**
   - A. a round lovely large wooden table
   - B. a large lovely round wooden table
   - C. a wooden lovely large round table
   - D. a lovely large round wooden table
   - **Answer:** D. The conventional order runs opinion, size, shape, then material, so lovely (opinion) comes first, followed by large (size), round (shape), and wooden (material); the other arrangements reverse that sequence.

5. **Which sentence uses the correct adjective and preposition combination?**
   - A. Leila is interested in marine biology.
   - B. Leila is interested at marine biology.
   - C. Leila is interested for marine biology.
   - D. Leila is interested on marine biology.
   - **Answer:** A. Interested pairs with in, not at, for, or on; combinations like interested in and good at have no general rule, so they are learned as fixed pairs.
