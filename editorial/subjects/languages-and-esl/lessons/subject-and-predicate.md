# Subject and Predicate

## In 30 Seconds

Every sentence makes a claim about something. The subject names who or what the sentence is about, and the predicate says something about that subject, built around a verb. In “The ferry crosses the bay,” the subject is the ferry and the predicate is crosses the bay. Subjects and predicates can be simple or compound, and commands carry an unstated subject. Learning to spot these two parts makes your own sentences easier to check and revise.

## Why This Matters

When you can name the subject and predicate of a sentence, editing stops being guesswork. Unclear or broken sentences, such as fragments that leave out a piece or verbs that do not match their subjects, usually trace back to these two parts. Once you can find the subject, you can check whether the verb agrees with it, whether the sentence is complete, and whether the reader can tell who did what. The same habit transfers to questions, where the subject hides in an unusual position, and to commands, where it is not written at all. Writers who see sentences as two working parts revise faster and explain their changes with precision.

## Learning Objectives

- Define subject and predicate as the two parts of every clause.
- Distinguish the simple subject from the complete subject.
- Identify compound subjects and compound predicates in original sentences.
- Explain how the subject of a command is understood even when it is not stated.
- Apply the who-or-what test to locate the subject of a question.

## The College Version

### Two parts in every clause

Every clause rests on a two-part structure: a subject and a predicate. The subject names who or what the clause is about, and the predicate says something about that subject. The predicate is built around a verb: an action word such as crossed, a linking verb such as tastes, or a helping verb paired with a main verb such as has practiced. In “The ferry crosses the bay,” the subject is the ferry and the predicate is crosses the bay. In “The soup tastes salty,” the predicate is tastes salty, because the verb links the subject to a description instead of showing an action.

Identifying the two parts does not require labeling every word first. It requires asking two questions: Who or what is this sentence about? And what is said about that person or thing? The answer to the first question is the subject, and everything in the clause that says something about that subject is the predicate. The same skeleton appears in every independent clause. “Birds sing” has a one-word subject and a one-word predicate. “Every spring, the young birds in the courtyard sing at first light” keeps the same two-part core beneath far more detail. Notice that the verb group can itself contain two words, as in has practiced, and the entire group belongs to the predicate.

### Simple, complete, and compound

The subject of a clause may be a single word or a long phrase. The simple subject is the essential noun or pronoun, the word the verb connects to. The complete subject includes that word plus everything attached to it. In “The old brick warehouse on Harbor Street caught fire,” the simple subject is warehouse. The complete subject is the old brick warehouse on Harbor Street. The modifiers and the prepositional phrase describe the warehouse, but they do not perform the burning. To find the simple subject, strip away descriptive words and phrases and ask what the verb connects to.

Sometimes a clause has more than one subject. A compound subject is two or more nouns or pronouns joined by a conjunction such as and, or, or nor, and it shares a single predicate. In “Amir and Priya designed the poster,” both Amir and Priya designed, so the subject is compound. Just as a subject can be compound, a predicate can hold two or more verbs joined by a conjunction and sharing one subject. In “The gardener watered the herbs and weeded the beds,” watered and weeded both describe what the gardener did, so the predicate is compound. These patterns matter because a clause is defined by one subject-predicate pair at its core. Adding a second subject or a second verb attaches to that core; it does not automatically create a new clause.

### Commands, questions, and what the distinction is for

Two common sentence types complicate the simple picture. Commands, which are imperative sentences, make a request, give a command, or offer an instruction, and their subject is not written. In “Return your books by Friday,” the sentence addresses the listener directly, and grammar references describe the unstated subject as understood you. You can prove this by turning the command into a statement: “You return your books by Friday.” Questions, which are interrogative sentences, rearrange the usual order. In “Has the team practiced today?” the subject team sits between the helping verb has and the main verb practiced. The reliable move is still the who-or-what test: restate the question as a statement, “The team has practiced today,” and the subject steps back into its familiar place.

Why does this distinction earn so much attention? Sentence-level repair starts here. A fragment is an incomplete sentence, often one missing a subject or a predicate, so seeing the two parts lets you spot the gap. Agreement, the matching of a singular or plural verb to its subject, also begins with locating the true subject, because phrases between subject and verb can mislead. The rules of agreement belong to their own topic; the habit of finding the subject first is this lesson's contribution. Once the subject is found, a writer can check the verb against it and the reader can follow who did what.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Subject:** The person, place, or thing that a clause is about; the answer to who or what the sentence concerns.
- **Predicate:** The part of a clause that says something about the subject and is built around a verb.
- **Simple subject:** The essential noun or pronoun in a subject, the word the verb connects to.
- **Complete subject:** The simple subject together with all the words attached to it in the clause.
- **Compound subject:** Two or more nouns or pronouns joined by a conjunction and sharing one predicate.
- **Compound predicate:** Two or more verbs joined by a conjunction and sharing one subject.
- **Imperative sentence:** A sentence that gives a request, command, or instruction; its subject is understood rather than stated.
- **Interrogative sentence:** A sentence that asks a question, often placing an auxiliary verb before the subject.

## Eli-10

A sentence is built from two pieces that always work together. The first piece is the subject: the person, place, or thing the sentence is about. The second piece is the predicate: what the sentence says about that person or thing, with a verb at its center. In “The ferry crosses the bay,” the ferry is the subject and crosses the bay is the predicate. You can find the subject by asking who or what the sentence is about; everything that answers what happens next belongs to the predicate. Some sentences have more than one subject, and some have more than one verb doing the work. Commands leave the subject out on purpose, and questions move their parts around. If you can find these two pieces, you can explain most sentence problems clearly.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a sentence as a photograph of one event. The subject is the main figure the photograph centers on; the predicate is everything the picture shows that figure doing. Change the figure, or change the action, and you have a different photograph, just as changing the subject or the predicate makes a different sentence.

The analogy has limits. A photograph can show several figures and actions without structure, but a sentence must organize its subjects and predicates around verbs, and those two parts can be rearranged, hidden, or joined in ways a picture never is.

## Worked Example

Read this sentence: “Before the storm arrived, the crew on the night shift sealed the windows and moved the supplies upstairs.” The independent clause is the crew on the night shift sealed the windows and moved the supplies upstairs. Ask who or what the clause is about: the crew. The simple subject is crew; the complete subject is the crew on the night shift. The predicate is everything said about the crew: sealed the windows and moved the supplies upstairs. It is a compound predicate because sealed and moved are two verbs joined by and, both sharing the same subject. Before the storm arrived is a dependent clause with its own subject, storm, and predicate, arrived. The structure is sound, so no repair is needed; a writer could keep the sentence as it is.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the first noun in the sentence as the subject.** Apply the who-or-what test to the verb: the subject is what the sentence is actually about, even when it appears after an introductory phrase such as after dinner or in the corner.
- **Confusing the object with the subject.** The object receives the action; it does not perform it. In “The chef sliced the onions,” chef is the subject and onions are the object.
- **Calling a sentence with two verbs two sentences.** Two verbs joined by a conjunction and sharing one subject form a single clause with a compound predicate, as in “The gardener watered and weeded.”
- **Hunting for a written subject in every command.** Commands address the listener directly; their subject is the understood you, so do not expect one on the page.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Simple subject | Complete subject | The simple subject is the core noun or pronoun the verb connects to; the complete subject includes it plus every word attached to it. |
| Compound subject | Compound predicate | A compound subject joins two or more subjects that share one predicate; a compound predicate joins two or more verbs that share one subject. |
| Declarative sentence | Imperative sentence | A declarative sentence states a fact with an expressed subject; an imperative sentence gives a command whose subject is understood as you. |

## Key Takeaway

Every clause names something, its subject, and says something about it, its predicate. Locate the subject first, and questions about agreement, completeness, and clarity become answerable.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In the sentence “The old oak tree swayed in the wind,” which word is the simple subject?**
   - A. tree
   - B. old
   - C. oak
   - D. wind
   - **Answer:** A. The simple subject is the core noun the verb connects to: the thing that swayed is the tree, while old and oak only describe it. Wind belongs to the prepositional phrase in the wind, so it is not the subject.

2. **Which sentence has a compound subject?**
   - A. The bus arrived late and left again.
   - B. Maria and Omar planned the mural together.
   - C. The recipe calls for fresh basil and ripe tomatoes.
   - D. The ferry crosses the bay every morning.
   - **Answer:** B. A compound subject is two or more subjects joined by a conjunction and sharing one predicate. Maria and Omar are both doing the planning. In the recipe sentence, basil and tomatoes are objects, not subjects.

3. **A library sign reads, “Return your books by Friday.” What is the subject of this sentence?**
   - A. books
   - B. Return
   - C. you, understood
   - D. Friday
   - **Answer:** C. Commands address the listener directly, and their subject is not stated; grammar references describe it as the understood you. Books is the object of the verb, and Friday belongs to the time phrase by Friday.

4. **In the question “Did the committee approve the budget?”, where is the subject, and how can you confirm it?**
   - A. approve, because it is the main verb
   - B. budget, because the question asks about it
   - C. did, because it begins the question
   - D. committee, because restating the question as “The committee did approve the budget” shows what the sentence is about
   - **Answer:** D. In questions the subject can sit after the helping verb. Turning the question into a statement reveals committee as the subject; approve is the main verb, and budget is the object that receives the approval.

5. **Which sentence has a compound predicate?**
   - A. The gardener watered the herbs and weeded the beds.
   - B. The gardener and the neighbor traded tools.
   - C. The gardener filled the watering can slowly.
   - D. The gardener carried the hose to the far bed.
   - **Answer:** A. A compound predicate has two verbs that share one subject: watered and weeded both describe what the gardener did. In the traded-tools sentence the compound element is the subject, not the predicate.
