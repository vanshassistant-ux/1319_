# Email Writing

## In 30 Seconds

A clear email follows a simple structure: a subject line that says what the message is about, a greeting, a body that states the purpose first, a closing, and a signature. The right level of formality depends on the reader: *Dear Dr. Chen* and *Best regards* for formal messages, *Hi Sam* and *Thanks* for casual ones. Requests sound softer as questions, such as *Could you...?*, and a quick reread catches tone that can come across harsher than intended.

## Why This Matters

Email is one of the most common ways that students, teachers, colleagues, and organizations communicate. A well-structured message gets read, understood, and answered; a disorganized or abrupt one gets ignored, misunderstood, or answered with irritation. Because email carries no voice or face, small choices — the subject line, the greeting, the wording of a request — do the work of showing respect and clarity. Learning the basic structure and the register range pays off across college, work, and daily life, and it prepares you for the paragraph-writing and conversation-skills topics.

## Learning Objectives

- Identify the five standard parts of an email — subject line, greeting, body, closing, and signature — in a model message.
- Distinguish formal, semi-formal, and informal greetings and closings, and explain how audience and purpose set the register.
- Apply the front-loading principle by stating the purpose first, then the details, then the ask.
- Evaluate subject lines for specificity, brevity, and action-orientation.
- Use polite request forms such as *Could you...?*, *Would you mind...?*, and *I was wondering if...* to soften direct commands.
- Explain why email can read harsher than intended and revise abrupt phrasing before sending.

## The College Version

### The anatomy of an email

A clear email has five recognizable parts: the subject line, the greeting, the body, the closing, and the signature. Each part has a job. The subject line tells the reader what the message is about before they open it. The greeting names the reader and sets the tone of the exchange. The body carries the message itself. The closing is a short sign-off such as "Best regards," or "Thanks,". The signature identifies you and gives the reader a way to reply — at minimum your name, usually with contact details. Here is a complete example:

Subject: Question about Monday's reading assignment

Hi Sam,

I have one quick question about the reading for Monday's class. You mentioned that we should look at the first two chapters, but I wanted to check whether the practice questions at the end are also required. If you get a chance, could you let me know by Thursday?

Thanks,

Maya

The subject line is "Question about Monday's reading assignment." The greeting is "Hi Sam,". The body is the two sentences in the middle. The closing is "Thanks," and the signature is "Maya." Every part works: the subject line names the topic, the greeting fits a familiar classmate, the body leads with the question, and the closing and signature end the message cleanly.

### Subject lines that work

A good subject line is specific, brief, and action-oriented: it tells the reader what the email is about and what, if anything, it asks. Vague subject lines — "Hello," "Question," "Meeting" — make the reader guess, and busy readers may open them last or not at all; a blank subject line is worse, because the message cannot be identified in an inbox or found again in a search. Compare two versions of the same message: "Meeting" tells the reader nothing, while "Rescheduling Thursday's team meeting" names the topic and the action in one glance. A subject line of a few specific words is usually enough; put the details — times, places, reasons — in the body. Keeping each email to one topic keeps subject lines honest and messages easy to find later.

### Greetings and closings: the register spectrum

Greetings and closings sit on a spectrum from formal to informal, and the choice signals how you see the relationship and the situation. For a first email to someone you do not know, a professor, or any official contact, formal forms fit: "Dear Dr. Chen," with a closing such as "Best regards," or "Sincerely,". For ongoing contact where you have already exchanged messages, semi-formal forms work: "Hello Ms. Park," with "Best," or "Kind regards,". For friends and close colleagues, informal forms are natural: "Hi Sam," with "Thanks," or "Cheers,". The register is set by audience and purpose, not by a rulebook: the same person receives a formal email from you on first contact and an informal one once you know each other. If you are unsure, start formal and follow the other person's lead. In fast back-and-forth threads, greetings often shorten or disappear entirely.

### The body: purpose first, details, then the ask

The most useful habit in email is front-loading: state the purpose in the first sentence, then give the details, then make the ask clear. Readers skim, and a busy reader who has to hunt for the request may never find it. Compare two openings for the same message. Weak: "I hope you had a nice weekend. I was looking at the course site, and I noticed there are two files in the resources folder, and the announcement about the deadline caught my eye, and actually I have a question about that…" Strong: "I'm writing to ask whether the lab report is due this Friday or next Monday." The strong version states the purpose in the first sentence, and the details and the ask follow naturally: "I noticed the syllabus lists both dates. If you get a chance, could you let me know by Wednesday?" Keep paragraphs short, stay on one topic, and end by telling the reader what you need and when.

### Politeness and tone

Email carries no voice or face, so short phrasing can land harder than the writer intended. A direct command such as "Send me the report" can read as rude even when no rudeness is meant; question forms soften it: "Could you send me the report?", "Would you mind sending me the report?", "I was wondering if you could send me the report." In general, the more indirect the form, the softer the request — so choose the form to fit the relationship and the size of the favor. Modal verbs carry much of this politeness: "can" and "may" mark informal and formal requests, while "would" marks polite requests, so request word choice matters. Tone rules of thumb: avoid ALL CAPS, which reads as shouting; avoid text abbreviations such as "u" for "you"; and be careful with jokes, which often fall flat or sound sharp in writing. Reading a message aloud before sending is a good test: if it would sound rude said to the person's face, rephrase it. Expectations also vary by context and culture — workplaces, schools, and countries differ in how formal their email is — so observing how the people around you write is part of learning the skill. Finally, email is not private: it travels unencrypted by default, so avoid sending passwords or account details in it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Subject line:** The line at the top of an email that summarizes what the message is about; it is what the recipient sees in the inbox before opening the message.
- **Greeting:** The opening line that names the reader, such as *Dear Dr. Chen,* or *Hi Sam,*.
- **Body:** The main part of the email that carries the message itself.
- **Closing:** The short sign-off phrase at the end of an email, such as *Best regards,* or *Thanks,*.
- **Signature:** The sender's name, and usually contact information, written after the closing.
- **Register:** The level of formality of language, chosen to fit the audience, purpose, and situation.
- **Formal language:** Language used in serious or first-contact situations, such as *Dear Dr. Chen,* and *Best regards,*.
- **Informal language:** Relaxed language used with people you know well, such as *Hi Sam,* and *Thanks,*.
- **Front-loading:** Arranging a message so the main purpose appears first, followed by the details and the ask.
- **Polite request:** A question form used to ask for something without commanding, such as *Could you...?* or *Would you mind...?*.

## Eli-10

An email is a letter that travels over the internet, and clear emails follow the same small pattern: a subject line that says what the message is about, a greeting, a body that starts with the main point, a closing, and your name at the end. Match how formal you are to who you are writing: "Dear Dr. Chen" and "Best regards" for formal messages, "Hi Sam" and "Thanks" for friends. Ask for things as questions — "Could you...?" — because "Send me the notes" sounds harsher in writing than it would in speech. Read your email over before sending, and fix anything that sounds sharp.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of an email like a message left on a colleague's desk. Because you are not there to smile, explain, or soften your words, the note has to do all the work alone: a clear title on the envelope, a polite opening, the point up front, and your name so they know who to reply to.

The analogy breaks down because a desk note is read by one person in one place, while an email can be forwarded, stored, searched, and read years later by anyone with access to the account — so email calls for more care with privacy and tone than a quick note ever would.

## Worked Example

A student needs her professor to sign a registration form. Her first draft reads: "Dr. Chen. I need you to sign my form. It is due Friday." The revision fixes five things. First, a subject line: "Request: signature on registration form." Second, a full greeting: "Dear Dr. Chen,". Third, the body leads with the purpose: "I am writing to ask if you could sign my registration form for the study-abroad program." Fourth, the details and the ask are clear: "The form is attached, and the office needs it by Friday. If you have any questions, please let me know." Fifth, the closing and signature match the formal register: "Best regards," and the writer's full name with course code. Every change makes the message easier to read and answer — and the polite request form "could you" turns a command into a favor.

## Common Mistakes

- **Leaving the subject line blank or vague,** as in "Hello" or nothing at all. Readers cannot tell what the message is about and may skip it or mistake it for spam. Fix: summarize the topic in a few specific words, such as "Question about Monday's reading assignment."
- **Burying the ask under background,** as in "I hope you're well. I was looking at the syllabus, and I noticed… and actually, could you…?" The request arrives last, if at all. Fix: state the purpose in the first sentence, then the details, then the ask.
- **Overly abrupt requests,** such as "Send me the notes." Direct commands can sound harsh in writing. Fix: use a question form: "Could you send me the notes?" or "I was wondering if you could send me the notes."
- **Over-formality in casual contexts,** such as "Dear Mr. Sam, … Sincerely," to a classmate you talk to every day. Fix: match the register to the relationship; when unsure, start more formal and follow the other person's lead.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Formal email ("Dear Dr. Chen," / "Best regards,") | Informal email ("Hi Sam," / "Thanks,") | Both follow the same five-part structure; they differ in register, chosen to match the audience and purpose rather than by any fixed rule. |
| Direct request ("Send me the report.") | Polite request ("Could you send me the report?") | The request is the same; the question form softens it so it reads as a favor rather than a command. |
| Email | Conversation | Email is written and asynchronous, so tone must be carried by words alone; conversation adds voice, face, and instant repair — the territory of the conversation-skills topic. |

## Key Takeaway

A clear email is a small structured message — a specific subject line, a fitting greeting, a body that leads with the purpose, and a closing and signature matched to the register — written for a reader who is skimming.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which list names the five standard parts of an email in the order they appear?**
   - A. Subject line, greeting, body, closing, signature
   - B. Greeting, subject line, body, signature, closing
   - C. Subject line, body, greeting, closing, signature
   - D. Greeting, body, subject line, signature, closing
   - **Answer:** A. A standard email opens with the subject line, then the greeting, then the body, and ends with the closing (the sign-off) and the signature; the other orders place the greeting or subject line in the wrong position.

2. **Which subject line would help a busy reader the most?**
   - A. Hello
   - B. Important!!!
   - C. Question about Monday's reading assignment
   - D. Assignment
   - **Answer:** C. "Question about Monday's reading assignment" is specific, brief, and names both the topic and the action expected of the reader; the vague or shouty alternatives tell the reader nothing useful and could even be mistaken for spam.

3. **A student is writing a first email to a professor she has never met. Which greeting and closing pair fits best?**
   - A. "Hey Dr. Chen," … "Later!"
   - B. "Dear Dr. Chen," … "Best regards,"
   - C. "Hi Dr. Chen," … "Cheers,"
   - D. "Hi Dr. Chen," … "Sincerely,"
   - **Answer:** B. First contact with a professor calls for the formal register: "Dear Dr. Chen," with a formal closing such as "Best regards,"; "Hey" and "Later!" are too casual, and the last option mixes a casual greeting with a formal closing.

4. **Which opening sentence for an email to a classmate states the purpose first?**
   - A. "I hope your weekend went well and that you got some rest."
   - B. "I was just looking through my notes and thought of you."
   - C. "It has been a while since we last talked about the project."
   - D. "I'm writing to ask whether you still have Tuesday's lecture notes."
   - **Answer:** D. Front-loading means stating the purpose in the first sentence, and only the last option does that; the others delay the point with pleasantries and background that make the reader search for the real message.

5. **Which request is the softest way to ask a colleague for a document?**
   - A. "Send me the report."
   - B. "I was wondering if you could send me the report."
   - C. "Could you send me the report?"
   - D. "Would you mind sending me the report?"
   - **Answer:** B. Requests grow softer as they become more indirect: the command is the hardest, the modal questions soften it, and the longest, most indirect form — "I was wondering if you could…" — is the softest of the four.
