# Photo Editing

## In 30 Seconds

Photo editing is everything you do to a photograph after the shutter closes. You correct exposure, contrast, and color across the whole frame using tools like levels and curves read against a histogram; you make local fixes with masks, dodge and burn, and healing brushes; you crop and straighten; and you export the right file format. Working non-destructively keeps the original recoverable. The one rule that changes everything is context: the same edit can be routine in an ad and dishonest in a news photo.

## Why This Matters

Almost no professional photograph reaches an audience straight from the camera. Editing is where a flat, cool, slightly crooked capture becomes a finished image, so the skill sits at the center of graphic design, marketing, journalism, and product work. Learning it well means understanding the histogram instead of dragging sliders blindly, protecting your original file so mistakes are reversible, and exporting a format that survives its destination. It also means knowing where correction ends and deception begins, a judgment that protects your credibility. As generative tools make alteration effortless, the editor who can explain what they changed and why is the one people trust.

## Learning Objectives

- Define photo editing as post-capture adjustment and distinguish it from taking the photograph.
- Explain how the histogram guides global tonal and color adjustments through levels and curves.
- Distinguish local adjustments and retouching (masks, dodge/burn, healing, cloning) from global edits.
- Distinguish non-destructive editing from destructive editing and explain why RAW widens editing latitude.
- Apply an ethical test to decide whether a given edit is acceptable correction or misleading manipulation.

## The College Version

### What editing does, and where it starts

Photo editing is the stage that begins once capture ends. The camera records a frame; editing decides how that frame finally looks and where it can be used. Capture choices such as exposure, aperture, and framing belong to photography itself and are fixed the moment the shutter fires. Editing works with what the sensor recorded, and its reach depends heavily on how much information that recording preserved. Editors group their work into two broad kinds. Global adjustments change the whole image at once: making it brighter or darker, adding or reducing contrast, warming or cooling the color, boosting or muting saturation. Local adjustments change only a chosen part: darkening a bright sky, brightening a face, or erasing a stray object. Most finished photographs pass through both. A typical workflow moves from the largest, most global corrections toward the smallest, most local ones, ending with cropping and export, so that early broad moves set the stage for precise final touches.

### Reading tone and color with the histogram

The single most useful instrument in editing is the histogram, a graph of how an image's pixels are distributed by brightness, with the darkest shadows on the left and the brightest highlights on the right, and a count of pixels stacked at each level. Reading the histogram is purely informational; it changes nothing by itself. You act on what it shows with tonal tools. The Levels control lets you set the black point, the white point, and the midtones, which is the fastest way to fix an image that looks flat or washed out. The Curves control does the same job with far more precision: instead of three anchor points it lets you reshape brightness across any part of the tonal range, so you can lift shadows while holding highlights in place. Color has its own controls. White balance neutralizes a color cast so that whites read as white rather than orange under indoor light or blue in shade. Saturation raises the intensity of all colors uniformly, while vibrance is a gentler version that protects already-strong colors and skin tones from becoming garish. Learning to watch the histogram while you move these controls is what separates deliberate editing from guesswork.

### Local adjustments and retouching

When only part of an image needs work, editors reach for local tools. A mask restricts an adjustment to a selected region, so a curve or a color shift applies to the sky but not the ground. Dodge and burn, terms inherited from darkroom printing, lighten or darken small areas by hand to guide the eye or restore detail. Retouching repairs flaws in the image content. The clone tool copies pixels directly from one place to another. The healing tool is its smarter relative: rather than copying pixels verbatim, it samples the area around the destination and blends the source into the surrounding texture, lighting, and color, which makes it ideal for removing blemishes, dust spots, or wrinkles without leaving a visible patch. Cropping and straightening also count as edits: cropping recomposes the frame after the fact and straightening corrects a tilted horizon. Each of these tools is powerful precisely because it changes what the photograph appears to show, which is why the same techniques sit at the heart of the ethics discussion below.

### Non-destructive editing and RAW

How you edit matters as much as what you change. Destructive editing alters the original pixels directly, so painting over them or flattening the file discards the underlying data permanently. Non-destructive editing keeps the original intact and records your changes separately, as instructions layered on top. Adjustment layers hold a tonal or color change that can be re-tuned or removed at any time; masks confine those layers; smart objects let a placed image be transformed repeatedly without degrading; and editing a RAW file is inherently non-destructive because the edits are stored as parameters applied to an untouched original. RAW matters here because a RAW file holds the data the camera's sensor captured with only very modest processing before output, unlike a JPEG that the camera has already sharpened, color-corrected, and compressed. That larger reserve of information gives the editor real latitude to reset white balance, recover a blown sky, or open deep shadows without the image falling apart. The practical payoff of working non-destructively is simple: every choice stays reversible, and the original is always recoverable.

### Exporting, and the ethics of alteration

Editing ends in an export, and the format encodes a trade-off. JPEG compresses aggressively and discards data to make small files, which suits photographs for the web but loses quality each time it is re-saved and cannot store transparency. PNG compresses without losing data and supports full transparency, which suits graphics, screenshots, and images with sharp edges. TIFF is typically lossless and is favored for print and archival masters. Resize and sharpen for the destination, then export a copy so the editable original survives. Finally, every edit carries an ethical weight that depends entirely on context. In advertising, fashion, and fine art, heavy retouching and compositing are accepted creative norms. In journalism the standard is strict: professional codes hold that editing must preserve a photograph's content and context, that altering documentary content to deceive the public is wrong, and that acceptable work is limited to basic tonal correction and cropping, never adding, removing, moving, or staging what the frame shows. The reliable test is not whether a tool was used but whether the result still tells the truth about what happened.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Histogram** — A graph showing how many pixels in an image fall at each brightness level, arranged from shadows on the left to highlights on the right.
- **Levels** — A tonal control that sets an image's black point, white point, and midtone balance.
- **Curves** — A tonal control that remaps brightness across any part of the tonal range for fine adjustment of contrast and color.
- **White balance** — An adjustment that neutralizes color casts so neutral tones appear neutral regardless of the light the photo was taken in.
- **Vibrance** — A saturation adjustment that raises the intensity of muted colors while protecting already-saturated colors and skin tones.
- **Mask** — A control that limits an adjustment or edit to a selected part of the image.
- **Dodge and burn** — Localized lightening (dodge) and darkening (burn) of chosen areas, named after a darkroom printing technique.
- **Healing tool** — A retouching tool that blends sampled pixels into the surrounding texture, lighting, and color, unlike the clone tool, which copies pixels directly.
- **Non-destructive editing** — Editing that leaves the original image data untouched and stores changes separately so they stay reversible.
- **RAW file** — A file holding the camera sensor's minimally processed data, giving wider latitude for tonal and color adjustment than a processed JPEG.

## Eli-10

Taking a photo is like catching light in a jar. Editing is what you do afterward to make what you caught look the way you meant it to. Some changes touch the whole picture, like turning up the brightness or fixing a color that came out too orange. Other changes touch just one spot, like darkening a bright window or erasing a smudge on someone's shirt. Smart editors keep the original safe and make their changes on a see-through layer on top, so nothing they do is ever permanent. And there is one big rule: fixing how a true picture looks is fine, but changing it to fool people about what really happened is not.

## Eli's Analogy

Editing a photo is like adjusting a recording of a song you already played. You can raise the volume, brighten the tone, or quiet one loud note, but if you splice in a chorus that was never sung, you have stopped mixing and started faking.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy fits tonal and volume-style fixes, but it understates the visual side: a photo can be altered by adding or removing objects, which has no clean audio equivalent, and unlike a private song mix, a news photo is judged against what actually happened in front of the camera.

## Worked Example

A student shoots an indoor portrait in RAW. The histogram shows pixels bunched on the left, so the image is underexposed; she raises the exposure and nudges the Levels white point until the graph fills the range. The skin looks orange under the room's lamps, so she corrects white balance until a white collar reads white. A slight tilt is fixed by straightening, and she crops to tighten the composition. On a duplicate adjustment layer she brightens only the face with a mask, then uses the healing tool to remove a single blemish, blending it into the surrounding skin. Because every step lives on layers over an untouched RAW original, she can revisit any choice. She exports a JPEG for the web and keeps a TIFF master, having changed how the photo looks without changing who or what it shows.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the histogram as something you "fix" by making it look a certain shape.** The histogram is a readout, not a target; a correct exposure for a dark scene will have a legitimately left-weighted histogram. Read it to inform tonal choices, don't force it into an ideal curve.
- **Editing directly on the original file and saving over it.** Work non-destructively on layers or adjust a RAW file, and export copies, so the original stays recoverable and every change remains reversible.
- **Assuming the clone tool and the healing tool do the same thing.** The clone tool copies pixels exactly; the healing tool blends sampled pixels into the surrounding texture and color, which usually gives an invisible repair on skin and smooth surfaces.
- **Believing any edit is acceptable as long as it looks good.** Acceptability depends on context. Retouching that is normal in a fashion ad becomes dishonest in a news photo, where adding, removing, or moving content violates professional ethics.
- **Exporting everything as JPEG.** JPEG is lossy and re-compresses on every save and holds no transparency; use PNG for graphics and transparency and TIFF for lossless print or archival masters.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Global adjustment vs. Local adjustment** — A global adjustment changes the entire image at once (overall exposure, contrast, white balance); a local adjustment, usually via a mask, changes only a selected region.
- **Levels vs. Curves** — Levels sets three anchor points (black, white, midtone); Curves reshapes brightness across any part of the tonal range, giving finer control over contrast and color.
- **Clone tool vs. Healing tool** — The clone tool copies source pixels verbatim; the healing tool blends sampled pixels into the destination's surrounding texture, lighting, and color for a seamless repair.
- **Non-destructive editing vs. Destructive editing** — Non-destructive editing stores changes as separate, reversible instructions over an untouched original; destructive editing rewrites the original pixels permanently.
- **Acceptable correction vs. Misleading manipulation** — Correction (tone, color, cropping) preserves what the photo shows; manipulation adds, removes, moves, or stages content so the image misrepresents what happened.

## Key Takeaway

Photo editing is post-capture adjustment: read tone and color with the histogram, correct globally then locally, work non-destructively so the original survives, export the right format, and never let an edit cross from honest correction into deception.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What does an image histogram show?**
   - A. The number of edits applied to a file
   - B. The distribution of pixels across brightness levels, from shadows to highlights
   - C. The physical dimensions of the image in pixels
   - D. The compression ratio used when the file is exported

   **Answer: B.** A histogram graphs how many pixels fall at each brightness level, shadows on the left and highlights on the right. It reports nothing about edit count, pixel dimensions, or compression; those are separate properties of a file.

2. **A photographer wants to keep the option of undoing any tonal change days later and always returning to the untouched capture. Which approach best achieves this?**
   - A. Painting corrections directly onto the background pixels
   - B. Flattening the image after each adjustment to save space
   - C. Overwriting the original JPEG after every edit
   - D. Editing a RAW file and using adjustment layers and masks

   **Answer: D.** Non-destructive editing stores changes as separate instructions over an untouched original, and RAW editing plus adjustment layers is exactly that. Painting on the background, flattening, and overwriting the original all permanently discard the data needed to undo the change.

3. **How does the healing tool differ from the clone tool?**
   - A. It blends sampled pixels into the surrounding texture and color instead of copying them verbatim
   - B. It works only on RAW files, while cloning works only on JPEGs
   - C. It changes the whole image at once rather than a local area
   - D. It permanently deletes pixels rather than replacing them

   **Answer: A.** The healing tool samples the area around the destination and blends the source into its texture, lighting, and color, whereas the clone tool copies pixels directly. Both are local tools that work on any raster image; neither is file-format specific, global, or a deletion tool.

4. **A news photographer submits an image for publication. Under professional photojournalism ethics, which edit is NOT acceptable?**
   - A. Adjusting overall contrast to match what the eye saw
   - B. Correcting white balance to remove an indoor color cast
   - C. Cropping to tighten the composition
   - D. Cloning out a bystander to simplify the background

   **Answer: D.** Photojournalism ethics permit basic tonal and color correction and cropping because they preserve the photo's content and context. Removing a person from the scene alters the content to misrepresent what happened, which the standard prohibits.

5. **A designer needs to export an edited photo that includes a transparent background for use over other layouts. Which format is the best fit?**
   - A. PNG, because it is lossless and supports full transparency
   - B. JPEG, because it is lossy and supports transparency
   - C. TIFF, because it is the only format that stores color
   - D. JPEG, because re-saving it improves the alpha channel

   **Answer: A.** PNG uses lossless compression and supports full alpha transparency, making it right for images that must sit over other content. JPEG is lossy and has no alpha channel, and it degrades on re-saving; TIFF stores color but is not the only format that does and is aimed at print/archival use.
