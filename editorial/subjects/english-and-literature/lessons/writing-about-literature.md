# Writing About Literature

## In 30 Seconds

Writing about literature is a craft with a process: you plan around a working thesis, draft with evidence, revise the big picture, and edit the details. You quote sparingly and with purpose—introduced, integrated, and explained—and you attribute every borrowed idea through MLA's author-page citations and a Works Cited list, the common U.S. convention for literary essays. Careful revision checks the thesis, cuts summary, and strengthens transitions, so readers always know what the text says and why it matters.

## Why This Matters

College literature courses in the United States routinely expect essays that quote and cite the texts they discuss, and most ask for MLA style. Learning the craft makes that work predictable rather than mysterious: once you know how to introduce and explain quotations, build a paragraph from claim, evidence, and analysis, and cite accurately, you can spend your energy on the thinking. The same habits transfer beyond the classroom—reports, reviews, and professional writing reward evidence handled honestly and attributed clearly. Careful revision and consistent citation also keep your writing honest, showing readers exactly where your ideas came from.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the stages of the writing process for a literary essay: planning, drafting, revising, and editing.
- Distinguish quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing, and explain when each is appropriate.

## The College Version

### The writing process: plan, draft, revise, edit

Writing about literature is not a single act of inspiration; it is a process with recognizable stages. In planning, you reread the text with your essay question in mind, annotate as you go, and note quotations together with their page numbers so evidence is easy to find later. You also sketch a working thesis: a provisional claim about the text that is subject to revision, because it is nearly impossible to craft a finished thesis before analyzing some of the text. In drafting, you follow that working thesis, using brief summary only as orientation for readers and placing evidence where it supports each claim. In revising, you work on the big picture first—whether the thesis still holds, whether every paragraph supports it, whether summary is crowding out analysis, and whether transitions connect your ideas. In editing, you turn to the details: sentence-level clarity, grammar, punctuation, and the accuracy of your citations. Handling the stages in order keeps the work manageable and prevents sentence-polish from disguising an argument that has not been thought through.

### Quoting, paraphrasing, and summarizing

When you bring a text into your essay, you have three moves, and they differ in how close your words are to the original. A quotation reproduces the source word for word, inside quotation marks (or in block format for long passages), and must be attributed. A paraphrase restates a specific passage in your own words and must also be attributed; it is usually shorter than the original. A summary condenses the main ideas of a longer passage or whole work into your own words, is much shorter, and is attributed too. The three serve different purposes: quote directly to feature the author's diction—a striking phrase or a distinctive way of saying something; paraphrase to convey a specific idea efficiently; summarize to give readers the gist of a larger whole. Quotations deserve your judgment: quote sparingly, and only when you have a good reason. And a quotation never speaks for itself. Introduce it with a signal phrase that names who is speaking, integrate it grammatically into your own sentence, and follow it with analysis that explains what the quoted words show and why they matter.

### MLA conventions: in-text citations and the Works Cited page

MLA style, published by the Modern Language Association, is the common U.S. academic convention for essays about literature. Its in-text citations use the author-page method: the author's last name and the page number from which the quotation or paraphrase is taken appear with the material, and the page number always sits in parentheses. The author's name may appear in your sentence—Okafor writes that the keeper "wound the clock each evening" (4)—or in the parenthetical with the page: the keeper "wound the clock each evening" (Okafor 4). Every in-text citation must lead to a full entry on the Works Cited page, a separate page at the end of the essay. Entries are alphabetized by the first element, usually the author's last name, are double-spaced with a hanging indent, and correspond to the sources actually cited in the text. MLA is a convention, not a law of nature: other disciplines use other styles, and your instructor's requirement governs. Long quotations have their own format: a passage of more than four typed lines of prose is set as a free-standing block, indented from the left margin, without quotation marks, with the parenthetical citation placed after the closing punctuation.

### Building paragraphs: topic sentence, evidence, analysis

The paragraph is the working unit of a literary essay, and its shape is simple: a topic sentence, evidence, and analysis. The topic sentence states the paragraph's claim and connects it to the thesis, telling readers what this paragraph will show. The evidence is textual—a quotation, a paraphrase, or a specific detail—with a citation that says where it comes from. The analysis is the explanation of how that evidence supports the claim; it is the part that does the essay's intellectual work. A paragraph that states a claim, quotes a line, and stops leaves the reader to guess why the quotation matters. A paragraph that quotes three passages in a row without comment is a pile of exhibits with no testimony. The tested pattern is: make the claim, present the evidence in a properly introduced and cited form, then reason about it—what the quoted words do, how they fit the pattern you are tracing, and why they matter for your argument. Readers should be able to find, after every piece of evidence, a sentence that says what it shows.

### Revision strategies and avoiding plagiarism

Revision is where an essay becomes an argument. Three strategies pay the highest returns. Check the thesis: does the essay still argue what the introduction claims, and does every paragraph support it? If a paragraph has drifted, revise the paragraph or revise the thesis—whichever is actually wrong. Cut summary: retell only what readers need to follow your analysis, and replace retelling with explanation of how the text works. Strengthen transitions: connect each paragraph to the one before it so the argument reads as a single line of thought rather than a list of observations. Plagiarism is the other side of the same discipline: using someone else's words or ideas without giving credit. It can be unintentional—forgetting to include a source—or intentional, and it carries serious academic consequences. You prevent it by attribution: a signal phrase naming the source, an in-text citation, and a Works Cited entry, so every borrowed word or idea is traceable. Common knowledge—information documented in many credible sources—generally needs no citation. The practical rule from writing handbooks is simple: when in doubt, cite.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Direct quotation:** The exact words of a source reproduced word for word, enclosed in quotation marks (or set as a block for long passages) and attributed to the author.
- **Paraphrase:** A specific passage restated in your own words, usually somewhat shorter than the original, and still attributed to the source.
- **Summary:** The main ideas of a longer passage or whole work condensed into your own words and attributed to the source.
- **Signal phrase:** Words that introduce a quotation or paraphrase and name its source, such as "Okafor writes that" or "According to the narrator."
- **In-text citation:** A parenthetical reference within the essay—in MLA, the author's last name and page number—that points readers to the full entry on the Works Cited page.
- **Works Cited:** The alphabetical list at the end of the essay containing full publication information for every source cited in the text.
- **Block quotation:** A long quotation set off from the surrounding text as an indented block without quotation marks; in MLA, more than four typed lines of prose.
- **Hanging indent:** Works Cited formatting in which the first line of an entry is flush with the left margin and subsequent lines are indented.
- **Plagiarism:** Using someone else's words or ideas without giving them proper credit, whether unintentionally or deliberately.
- **Common knowledge:** Information documented in many credible sources that is generally assumed to be widely known and therefore needs no citation.

## Eli-10

Writing about literature means making your reading visible and keeping it honest. You begin with a working thesis—what you want to show about the text—and gather quotations that could support it. You draft, then you revise: check that every paragraph still backs the thesis, cut places where you are only retelling the story, and connect paragraphs with transitions. When you use the text's words, introduce them, quote them exactly, and explain them. When you use someone else's idea—the author's or a critic's—say where it came from, in the sentence and in a Works Cited list. Citation is not punishment; it is how readers can check your work and build on it.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of integrating a quotation like hosting a guest at a small dinner party. You do not open the door and walk away. You introduce the guest (a signal phrase: "as Okafor writes"), let them speak (the quotation itself), and then make sure everyone understood why they were invited (your analysis). The citation is the name tag: it tells everyone where this guest came from and how to find them again.

The analogy has limits. A dinner guest can answer follow-up questions; a quotation cannot—it stays fixed on the page, so the writer must supply the interpretation. And a name tag does not make an uninvited guest welcome: citation records where words came from, but it does not replace actually integrating and explaining them.

## Worked Example

A student argues that the clock in the invented short story "The Keeper of the Harbor Clock" by Maya Okafor represents stubborn hope. One body paragraph reads: "Tomas's winding is not routine but refusal. Okafor writes that he 'wound the clock each evening, though the town had stopped asking why,' and that detail separates the act from any demand (Okafor 4). The town has stopped asking; the clock is no longer needed; only Tomas's hands keep the harbor's time alive." The quotation is introduced with a signal phrase, integrated into the paragraph, explained in the analysis, and cited with the MLA author-page format. The matching Works Cited entry is: Okafor, Maya. "The Keeper of the Harbor Clock." Stories of the Salt Coast, edited by Priya Raman, Harbor Press, 2023, pp. 41-58.

## Common Mistakes

- **Dropping a quotation with no introduction or explanation.** Introduce it with a signal phrase, integrate it into your own sentence, and follow it with a sentence of analysis explaining what the quoted words show.
- **Quoting too much.** Quote sparingly and with a reason; paraphrase specific ideas and summarize larger ones, reserving direct quotation mainly for passages where the author's wording itself matters.
- **Revising only sentences and commas.** Revise globally first—thesis, evidence, organization, transitions—then edit sentences, grammar, and citation details.
- **Citing inaccurately or inconsistently.** Give the author and page for every quotation or paraphrase, and make sure every in-text citation has a matching entry on the Works Cited page.
- **Assuming a paraphrase needs no citation.** The idea still belongs to its source; attribute it with a signal phrase and citation even when the wording is your own.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Quotation / Paraphrase:** A quotation reproduces the source's exact words with quotation marks and attribution; a paraphrase restates a specific passage in your own words with attribution. Quote to feature diction; paraphrase to convey an idea efficiently.
- **Paraphrase / Summary:** A paraphrase restates one specific passage in similar detail, while a summary condenses the main points of a larger passage or whole work; both use your own words and both require attribution.
- **Revising / Editing:** Revising changes the big picture—thesis, evidence, organization, transitions—while editing polishes sentences, grammar, punctuation, and citation accuracy.
- **In-text citation / Works Cited entry:** The in-text citation tells readers, at the point of use, where material comes from (author and page in MLA); the Works Cited entry gives full publication information at the end of the essay. They work as a pair.

## Key Takeaway

Writing about literature is a craft of process and honesty: plan, draft, revise, and edit; quote with purpose and explain every quotation; and attribute every borrowed word or idea so readers can follow your evidence.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In MLA style, an in-text citation for a quotation from a print source normally includes which elements?**
   - A. The author's first name and the year of publication.
   - B. The author's last name and the page number.
   - C. The full title of the work and the publisher.
   - D. The URL of the source and the date of access.
   - **Answer:** B. MLA uses the author-page method: the author's last name and the page number appear with the quotation or paraphrase, with the page number always in parentheses, while full publication details go on the Works Cited page. The year, full title, and URL belong to other citation styles or other parts of the entry.

2. **A student is quoting six typed lines of prose from an invented short story. Which format follows MLA convention for a quotation of this length?**
   - A. Keep the passage in quotation marks inside the paragraph.
   - B. Indent the whole passage but keep quotation marks around it.
   - C. Set the passage as an indented block without quotation marks, with the parenthetical citation after the closing punctuation.
   - D. Place the parenthetical citation before the quoted passage and omit the author's name.
   - **Answer:** C. MLA sets quotations of more than four typed lines of prose as a free-standing indented block without quotation marks, and the parenthetical citation follows the closing punctuation. Short quotations stay in quotation marks inside the paragraph; the other options combine formats incorrectly.

3. **Which statement best describes paraphrasing?**
   - A. Restating a specific passage in your own words while still attributing it to the source.
   - B. Reproducing the source's exact words inside quotation marks.
   - C. Changing a few words of the source so it no longer needs a citation.
   - D. Condensing the main points of an entire work into a few sentences.
   - **Answer:** A. A paraphrase puts a specific passage into your own words and must still be attributed to the source. Reproducing exact words is quoting, condensing main points is summarizing, and light rewording without citation is plagiarism, not paraphrase.

4. **A first draft of a literary essay devotes one body paragraph mostly to retelling the story's events. Which revision best serves the essay?**
   - A. Expanding the retelling so readers know every plot detail.
   - B. Deleting the paragraph entirely.
   - C. Replacing the plot details with a long quotation and no explanation.
   - D. Cutting the summary to the few details readers need and adding analysis of how the quoted evidence supports the claim.
   - **Answer:** D. Revision should trim summary and expand analysis: the paragraph exists to support the thesis with evidence and reasoning, not to retell the story. Expanding the retelling, deleting the paragraph, or quoting without explanation each leaves the paragraph without an argued point.

5. **A student paraphrases a critic's argument about an invented novel. Which step is required to avoid plagiarism?**
   - A. Nothing, because paraphrases are always original work.
   - B. Changing the critic's words so thoroughly that the source no longer matters.
   - C. Adding quotation marks around the paraphrase.
   - D. Attributing the idea to the critic with an in-text citation and a matching Works Cited entry.
   - **Answer:** D. A paraphrase is your wording, but the idea still belongs to the source, so it must be attributed with a signal phrase, an in-text citation, and a matching Works Cited entry. Without attribution it is plagiarism; quotation marks are for direct quotes, not paraphrases.
