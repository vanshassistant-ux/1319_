# Rhetorical Analysis

## In 30 Seconds

Rhetorical analysis is the examination of how a text persuades. You look at the rhetorical situation — the author, audience, purpose, context, message, and medium — and then identify the appeals and strategies doing the persuasive work: ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion), logos (reason), and moves such as repetition, parallel structure, word choice, anecdote, counterargument, and rhetorical questions. Analysis describes how persuasion works; judging whether it works well is a separate step.

## Why This Matters

Rhetoric surrounds you: every post, ad, speech, and policy memo tries to shape what you believe or do. Rhetorical analysis gives you a vocabulary for noticing how that persuasion works, which is the first defense against being moved without knowing why. In college, it is the backbone of critical reading and persuasive writing across disciplines. Later, in any field, the same skills help you read proposals critically and craft messages that reach their intended audience honestly.

## Learning Objectives

- Define rhetorical analysis as the examination of how a text persuades.
- Identify the elements of the rhetorical situation: author, audience, purpose, context, message, and medium.
- Distinguish ethos, pathos, and logos as appeals built on credibility, emotion, and reason.
- Identify common rhetorical strategies — repetition, parallel structure, word choice, anecdote, counterargument, and rhetorical questions — in a text.
- Explain the difference between analyzing how a text persuades and evaluating whether it persuades effectively or ethically.
- Apply a claim–evidence–effect structure to write a short rhetorical analysis.

## The College Version

### What rhetorical analysis is

Rhetorical analysis is the examination of how a text persuades. Instead of asking only what a text says, it asks how the text works: which choices the writer made, why those choices matter, and what they accomplish with the audience. The Greek term rhetoric originally referred to speaking, and it came to mean the craft of persuading through writing or speaking; rhetorical analysis is the practice of breaking that craft into its parts and understanding how they operate. The aim is not to agree with the writer or to praise the text, although an analysis may end with a judgment about effectiveness. The core task is descriptive: identify the writer's tools and explain what they accomplish. An audience member who can name the moves a speech is making — “she is telling a story to make me care,” “he keeps repeating that phrase” — is already doing a version of rhetorical analysis. The method applies to any communication: a campaign speech, an advertisement, a letter to the editor, a social-media post, or a school board proposal. Summary tells you what a text says; rhetorical analysis tells you how it tries to move you and why that attempt matters.

### The rhetorical situation

Every act of persuasion happens inside a rhetorical situation: the set of circumstances surrounding the communication. The main elements are the author (the person or people originating the message), the audience (the people it addresses, whether real or anticipated), the purpose (what the author wants the audience to do, feel, or believe), the context (the social, political, or personal circumstances in which the communication occurs), the message (what is actually communicated), and the medium (the genre or channel, from a speech to a text message to a poster). The same request can be delivered as a quick text to a neighbor, a printed flyer, or a formal speech to a city council, and each version changes what the message can do: a text assumes a close relationship and little time; a speech must earn the attention of strangers and answer their concerns. The situation also includes timing: an appeal made at the right moment can succeed where the same appeal earlier or later would fall flat. When you begin an analysis, name these elements before you judge any strategy. An appeal is not persuasive in the abstract; it is persuasive for a particular audience, in a particular context, through a particular medium. This is why two texts can argue the same position and one can work while the other fails.

### The appeals and strategies: an analytic vocabulary

The three appeals are the classic vocabulary for describing how persuasion works. Ethos is the appeal based on the perceived character, credibility, or reliability of the writer or speaker: a surgeon writing about hospital safety, a neighbor who has lived on the street for twenty years, or a candidate who discloses her own experience can all build trust through ethos. Pathos is the appeal to the audience's emotions, needs, and values: fear, hope, pride, loyalty, a sense of fairness, or a wish to protect loved ones. Logos is the appeal to reason: facts, examples, statistics, and reasoning that moves from evidence toward a conclusion. These three are an analytic vocabulary, not a formula. A persuasive text rarely uses exactly one appeal in a neat sequence, and there is no rule that every analysis paragraph must find all three. A single anecdote can carry pathos and build ethos at the same time; a statistic can serve logos and, in the right context, stir fear or hope as well. The analyst's job is to name what is actually happening in the text. Writers also use smaller, identifiable strategies: repetition of a word or phrase to build emphasis; parallel structure, which repeats a pattern of words to show ideas as equally important; word choice and connotation, where one word rather than another carries the persuasive weight; the anecdote, a short story that gives an argument a human face; the counterargument, which acknowledges and answers opposing views; the rhetorical question, asked for effect rather than for an answer; and shifts in the text's projected attitude, which can build toward a climax or make criticism sound reasonable. Some persuasive moves are questionable — bandwagon pressure, personal attacks, exaggeration — and analysis can name those too, describing how they try to push past reasoned judgment.

### From analysis to evaluation, and how to structure it

Analyzing rhetoric and evaluating it are different moves. Analysis describes how a text works: it identifies the strategy, points to the evidence, and explains the effect on the audience. Evaluation applies standards: it judges whether the persuasion is effective, honest, or fair, and it must say which standards it is using. “The speech repeats the warning three times to keep the danger in the audience's mind” is analysis. “The speech is manipulative” or “the speech is effective” is evaluation. A rhetorical analysis essay may conclude with an evaluation of effectiveness, but the description has to come first; a verdict without analysis is just an opinion. To write a rhetorical analysis, use a claim–evidence–effect structure. Each paragraph opens with a claim about a strategy — for example, that the writer builds ethos by disclosing personal experience. Then present the specific evidence: the sentence or passage that shows the move. Then explain the effect on the audience: why this move, for these readers, in this situation, makes the persuasion work or fail. Move through the text's appeals and strategies one at a time, and note whether the writer addresses opposing views and how. The claims that arguments make, the reasoning that supports them, and the tone of a text are each studied more fully in their own topics; this lesson keeps the focus on how a text persuades.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Rhetoric:** The craft or study of using language to persuade, inform, or motivate an audience.
- **Rhetorical analysis:** The examination of a text that identifies the strategies a writer or speaker uses to persuade and explains how they work.
- **Rhetorical situation:** The circumstances of a communication: the author, audience, purpose, context, message, and medium.
- **Ethos:** An appeal based on the perceived character, credibility, or reliability of the writer or speaker.
- **Pathos:** An appeal to an audience's emotions, needs, and values.
- **Logos:** An appeal to reason through facts, examples, evidence, and clear reasoning.
- **Repetition:** Repeating a word or group of words to build emphasis and make an idea memorable.
- **Parallel structure:** Repeating the same pattern of words or clauses to show that ideas are equally important.
- **Rhetorical question:** A question posed for dramatic effect rather than for an expected answer.
- **Counterargument:** A strategy that acknowledges and addresses opposing views to show the writer has considered them.

## Eli-10

Rhetorical analysis is looking at how a message tries to persuade you, not just what it says. When someone asks for something — say, a later bus on your route — they might sound fair and experienced (ethos), tell a story that makes you care (pathos), or give you facts and reasons (logos). Those are the three big appeals, and writers also use smaller moves: repeating a key phrase, matching sentence patterns, choosing words with strong feelings attached, telling a short story, answering the other side's objection, or asking a question they do not expect you to answer. Your job as an analyst is to notice the move and say what it does for the audience. You do not have to decide yet whether the message is honest or good; describing how it works comes first, and judging comes later — or not at all, if that is not the assignment.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a sports commentator explaining why a play worked: the commentator does not just say “great goal”; they point to the pass, the run, the angle, and how the defense was drawn out of position. Rhetorical analysis does that with words: it names the moves and shows what they accomplish.

The analogy has limits. Unlike a game, a text's effect is not fixed. The same strategy can persuade one audience and annoy another, and analysts can reasonably disagree about what a move accomplishes, so analysis must stay tied to evidence and context.

## Worked Example

Maya's speech to the city council asks for a crosswalk at Maple Avenue and Fourth Street. It opens with her own story: “Last October, my daughter was almost hit by a car at Maple and Fourth.” That anecdote appeals to pathos, putting a face on the traffic statistics. She then cites the city's own study — three hundred students cross that corner every school day — which is logos, and she mentions her eight years as a crossing guard, which builds ethos. She repeats the phrase “a safe way across” three times and closes with a rhetorical question: “How many close calls do we need before we act?” A sample analysis paragraph: “Maya's repeated phrase 'a safe way across' builds emphasis and keeps the audience focused on the solution rather than the cost, making the request feel urgent and reasonable.”

## Common Mistakes

- **Listing strategies without explaining their effect.** After naming a strategy, state what it does for this audience in this text.
- **Reviewing the text instead of analyzing it.** Describe how the persuasion works before judging it; save evaluation for a clearly marked step with named standards.
- **Forcing every appeal into the analysis.** Treat ethos, pathos, and logos as vocabulary, not a checklist; many texts rely on one or two appeals or combine them.
- **Summarizing content instead of examining method.** Move from what the text says to how the language, structure, and situation carry the persuasion.
- **Assuming one strategy has one guaranteed effect.** Check the audience, context, and surrounding language, and qualify the claim.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rhetorical analysis | Summary | Summary reports what a text says; rhetorical analysis examines how it persuades and why those choices matter. |
| Analysis | Evaluation | Analysis describes how persuasion works; evaluation applies standards to judge effectiveness, fairness, or ethics. |
| Ethos | Logos | Ethos works through the speaker's perceived character and credibility; logos works through facts, reasons, and evidence. |

## Key Takeaway

Rhetorical analysis examines how a text persuades: name the situation, identify the appeals and strategies with evidence, and explain their effect on the audience — then, only if the task calls for it, judge.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What does rhetorical analysis primarily examine?**
   - A. How a text persuades its audience through identifiable strategies.
   - B. How many pages a text contains.
   - C. Whether a text is grammatically perfect.
   - D. How much a reader personally likes a text.
   - **Answer:** A. Rhetorical analysis looks at how a text persuades — the appeals, strategies, and situation — not its length, grammar, or the reader's taste.

2. **A speaker says, “We planned, we built, we opened the doors.” Which strategy is this sentence using?**
   - A. Anecdote
   - B. Parallel structure
   - C. Counterargument
   - D. Rhetorical question
   - **Answer:** B. The three clauses repeat the same pattern (we + verb), which is parallel structure; it presents the actions as equally significant and makes the line memorable.

3. **An analyst writes: “When the mayor mentions her years as a teacher, she builds credibility with parents before introducing the budget plan.” Which feature of a rhetorical analysis does this sentence show?**
   - A. It summarizes the speech's main events.
   - B. It announces the analyst's personal opinion of the mayor.
   - C. It links a claim about a strategy to its intended effect on the audience.
   - D. It lists every strategy the speech contains without comment.
   - **Answer:** C. The sentence claims a specific strategy (credibility, or ethos), points to the evidence (the teaching years), and explains its effect (trust with parents) — the core claim–evidence–effect structure.

4. **Which statement is an act of rhetorical analysis rather than an evaluation?**
   - A. That speech was the best of the three candidates' speeches.
   - B. The advertisement is dishonest and should be banned.
   - C. The essay would be stronger with more statistics.
   - D. The writer repeats the warning to keep the danger in the audience's mind.
   - **Answer:** D. It describes how a strategy works for the audience; the other options judge quality, honesty, or strength, which is evaluation rather than analysis.

5. **Which list names the elements of the rhetorical situation?**
   - A. Author, audience, purpose, context, message, and medium.
   - B. Chapter, page number, cover, and index.
   - C. Noun, verb, adjective, and adverb.
   - D. Introduction, body, conclusion, and bibliography.
   - **Answer:** A. The rhetorical situation is the set of circumstances around a communication: who writes, who reads, why, in what context, with what message, and through what medium.
