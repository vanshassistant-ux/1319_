# Poetry

## In 30 Seconds

Poetry is a literary form organized in lines rather than sentences. Those lines compress meaning, so poets lean on sound and rhythm: meter, rhyme, alliteration, assonance, consonance, and onomatopoeia. The line and the stanza are the basic units; scansion marks a poem's stressed and unstressed syllables; iambic pentameter, five iambs per line, is the most common English meter. The speaker is a voice the poet constructs, not necessarily the author. Free verse and formal verse are both deliberate, and poems reward rereading.

## Why This Matters

Reading poetry trains attention to how language works at the level of a single syllable. The skills it builds, hearing rhythm, noticing sound patterns, and tracking where lines break, transfer to any writing, from song lyrics to speeches to advertising. In college, poetry appears across literature courses, and the vocabulary of meter, rhyme, and stanza lets you discuss form precisely instead of saying a poem "sounds nice." Learning to reread a short text and find new structure on each pass also strengthens close reading and analytical writing, which demand the same patient attention to evidence.

## Learning Objectives

- Define poetry as a literary form organized through lineation, compression, sound, and rhythm.
- Distinguish the poetic line and the stanza from the sentence and paragraph of prose.
- Explain meter and rhythm, including iambic pentameter, and apply scansion to mark stressed and unstressed syllables.
- Identify end rhyme, internal rhyme, rhyme scheme, alliteration, assonance, consonance, and onomatopoeia in a poem.
- Distinguish the poem's speaker from the poet who wrote it.
- Compare free verse and formal verse and explain how line breaks shape a poem's meaning.

## The College Version

### Poetry as a form: lines, compression, sound, and rhythm

Poetry is one of the major literary forms, alongside fiction and drama, and its defining unit is the line rather than the sentence. A line in a poem is a set of words that ends for a specific reason, whether rhythm, meaning, syllable count, pacing, or rhyme, and the poet chooses where to break it. This practice, lineation, is what visibly separates poetry from prose, which runs sentences and paragraphs to the margin. Because lines are the scaffolding, poetry tends toward compression: a poem often packs into a few lines what a prose paragraph would stretch across many sentences. That density is why sound and rhythm carry so much weight. With fewer words to spare, poets use the music of language to organize meaning, and readers are expected to hear the poem as well as read it. OpenStax's writing guide lists "structure of lines and stanzas" and "sound (meter, rhyme, rhythm)" among the primary things to examine in a poem, and the Poetry Foundation's glossary defines the line as words that end for a specific reason chosen by the poet.

### Meter, rhythm, and scansion

English speech naturally stresses some syllables more than others, and poetry organizes those stresses into rhythm. When the rhythm follows a regular pattern, that pattern is called meter. The basic unit of meter is the foot, one stressed syllable together with its surrounding unstressed syllables. The most common foot in English is the iamb: an unaccented syllable followed by an accented one, as in "unite" or "provide." A line of five iambs is iambic pentameter, penta- meaning five, and it is the most common metrical line in English, sitting close to the rhythms of ordinary speech. Shakespeare's line "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?" (Sonnet 18) scans as five clean iambs. Scansion is the tool readers use to see this: marking each syllable stressed (/) or unstressed (x), grouping syllables into feet, and noting pauses. Scansion is descriptive rather than a verdict; lines rarely obey a pattern perfectly, and poets deliberately vary the meter to shift emphasis or speed.

### Rhyme and the sound devices

Rhyme is the repetition of sounds, typically at the end of a verse line. End rhyme, the most common kind, rhymes the final syllables of lines. Internal rhyme happens within a single line, when a word in the middle rhymes with a word at the end. A rhyme scheme is the pattern of end rhymes across a stanza, labeled with letters: a quatrain rhyming ABAB has its first and third lines sharing one rhyme and its second and fourth sharing another. Beyond rhyme, poets use other sound patterns. Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds in nearby words, as in "the quick clock clicks"; assonance repeats vowel sounds without repeating the consonants, as in "the low road home"; consonance repeats consonant sounds while the vowels differ, as in "bed" and "bad"; onomatopoeia uses words that imitate the sounds they name, such as "buzz," "clang," or "hiss." These devices are not decoration. They bind words together, create texture, and often underline meaning by linking the words that matter most.

### The speaker, imagery, and figurative language

Every poem has a speaker: the voice that delivers the words. The speaker is a persona the poet constructs, a character made of the poem's language, not the poet's biography. A poet may write in the voice of a child, a soldier, a ghost, or a stranger, and the "I" of a poem is not evidence about the author's life. Claims about a poem should therefore be anchored in the poem's own words rather than in what readers happen to know about the writer. Poems also compress meaning through imagery and figurative language, language that engages the senses or compares one thing to another. Imagery, metaphor, simile, symbolism, and allusion each deserve their own lessons; here it is enough to recognize that when a poem shows rather than states, those tools are likely at work, carried by the poem's lines, meter, rhyme, and sound.

### Free verse, formal verse, and the work of the line

Formal verse follows a set plan: a regular meter, a rhyme scheme, a fixed number of lines, or all three, as in a sonnet. Free verse does not follow a regular meter or rhyme scheme; its lines follow the natural rhythms of speech, though a pattern of sound or rhythm may still emerge. Free verse is not rule-free. The poet still chooses every line break, and those choices shape meaning. A line that stops mid-phrase and runs its sentence over into the next line is enjambed; a line that ends where a phrase or sentence ends is end-stopped. Compare "I watched the light / fade" with "I watched / the light fade": the same six words, two different emphases. Because poems compress so much, they reward rereading. A second or third pass can reveal a rhyme you missed, a meter that breaks at a crucial word, or a line break that changes the emphasis, and this is not a sign of difficulty. Rereading is how the form is built to be experienced.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Line:** A unit of verse that ends where the poet chooses; the break may be shaped by rhythm, meaning, syllable count, pacing, or rhyme.
- **Stanza:** A grouping of lines within a poem, usually separated from other groupings by a blank line or indentation.
- **Meter:** The rhythmical pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables in a line of verse.
- **Iamb:** A metrical foot made of one unaccented syllable followed by one accented syllable, as in the word "unite."
- **Iambic pentameter:** A line of verse made of five iambs, the most common metrical line in English poetry.
- **Scansion:** The analysis of a poem's metrical patterns by marking stressed and unstressed syllables and grouping them into feet.
- **Rhyme scheme:** The pattern of end rhymes in a stanza, labeled with letters of the alphabet such as ABAB.
- **Alliteration:** The repetition of initial consonant sounds in a series of nearby words within a phrase or line.
- **Speaker:** The voice that delivers a poem's words, a persona the poet constructs rather than the poet's own identity.
- **Free verse:** Poetry that does not follow a regular meter or rhyme scheme, instead following the natural rhythms of speech.

## Eli-10

Poetry is writing arranged in lines, and the poet decides where each line ends. That choice, plus the sounds of the words and the way stresses fall, does real work. A poem often says a lot in very few words, so it helps to read it more than once. On the second pass you may notice a rhyme you missed, or that a line breaks right at the word that matters. When you meet a poem, start by hearing it: read it aloud, notice which syllables are stressed, where sounds repeat, and where the lines end.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a poem as a photograph with a frame. The frame's edge decides what you see together, just as the line break decides what the eye groups together. And like a good photo, a poem leaves out a lot, so you keep looking.

The analogy has limits: a poem is not actually a still picture. It moves in time as you read, it can change meaning on a second reading, and its sounds and rhythms matter as much as its images. A photograph has no syllables to stress and no rhymes to hear.

## Worked Example

Consider this invented four-line poem:

> The plumber left a ladder by the door.
> The kitchen sink has quieted once more.
> The window holds a single strip of light;
> the hammer and the hush belong to night.

Scanned, the first line divides into five iambs, ten syllables alternating unstressed and stressed ("the PLUM-ber LEFT a LAD-der BY the DOOR"), so the poem opens in iambic pentameter. The lines rhyme in pairs, door/more and light/night, an AABB rhyme scheme. Now reread the final line: "the hammer and the hush belong to night" repeats the h sound in hammer and hush, a touch of alliteration, and "hush" quietly imitates silence. Try the third line enjambed as "The window holds / a single strip of light": the pause after "holds" makes the reader wait, shifting emphasis onto what is held. The speaker is invented; nothing in the poem reports the poet's life, only a voice describing a house settling into night.

## Common Mistakes

- **Assuming the speaker is the poet.** Treat the speaker as a voice the poem constructs, and use the poem's words, not the poet's biography, as evidence for what the speaker feels or believes.
- **Thinking poetry must rhyme or must follow a regular meter.** Much poetry, most free verse included, uses neither; line breaks, rhythm, and sound patterns still organize it.
- **Treating line breaks as accidental.** Poets choose where lines end; notice whether a line is end-stopped or enjambed and what the break emphasizes.
- **Reading free verse as rule-free writing.** Free verse avoids regular meter and rhyme, but the poet still makes deliberate choices of line, sound, and pacing.
- **Reading a poem once and calling it done.** Poems are compressed; reread to notice rhyme, metrical variation, and line breaks that a first pass misses.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Poetry / Prose:** Poetry organizes language in lines whose breaks are chosen for effect; prose organizes language in sentences and paragraphs that run to the margin.
- **End rhyme / Internal rhyme:** End rhyme pairs the final syllables of lines; internal rhyme pairs a word inside a line with the line's final word.
- **Free verse / Formal verse:** Free verse avoids regular meter and rhyme and follows natural speech rhythms; formal verse follows a set metrical or rhyming plan.
- **Speaker / Poet:** The speaker is the voice delivering the poem's words, a constructed persona; the poet is the writer, who may or may not resemble the speaker.
- **Enjambed line / End-stopped line:** An enjambed line runs its phrase over into the next line; an end-stopped line ends where a phrase, clause, or sentence ends.

## Key Takeaway

Poetry is writing whose lines, compression, sound, and rhythm all carry meaning: listen for meter and rhyme, separate the speaker from the poet, and reread to let the form speak.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What is an iamb?**
   - A. A line of five metrical feet
   - B. A foot of one accented syllable followed by one unaccented syllable
   - C. A metrical foot of one unaccented syllable followed by one accented syllable
   - D. A stanza of four lines that rhyme ABAB
   - **Answer:** C. An iamb is a foot of one unaccented syllable followed by one accented syllable, as in "unite." Five iambs make a pentameter line, an accented-then-unaccented foot is a trochee, and a four-line ABAB stanza is a quatrain, not an iamb.

2. **Which invented line contains an internal rhyme?**
   - A. The rain came down across the town.
   - B. A sudden silence filled the hall.
   - C. The kettle hums beside the open door.
   - D. She walked alone upon the quiet lane.
   - **Answer:** A. Internal rhyme pairs a word inside a line with the word at the line's end; "down" in the middle of the first line rhymes with "town" at its end. The other three lines contain no such pair.

3. **Why is the speaker of a poem not necessarily the poet?**
   - A. Because the speaker is always a fictional character in a story
   - B. Because poems are never about their authors
   - C. Because the speaker is the reader's own voice
   - D. Because the speaker is a voice the poet constructs, which may differ from the poet's identity
   - **Answer:** D. The speaker is the persona that delivers the poem's words, a voice the poet builds from language; it may resemble the poet or be entirely invented, so biography is not evidence for the speaker's identity.

4. **Which invented line is in iambic pentameter?**
   - A. The plumber left a ladder by the door.
   - B. The ladder leans against the wall.
   - C. Quiet now, the house is still.
   - D. Hush now, the house is quiet and still.
   - **Answer:** A. The first line has ten syllables in five iambs, unaccented followed by accented: "the PLUM-ber LEFT a LAD-der BY the DOOR." The second has eight syllables (tetrameter), the third has six, and the fourth begins with a heavy stress that breaks the iambic pattern.

5. **A poet writes the line "I watched the light fade" as two lines: "I watched / the light fade." What does this line break most directly do?**
   - A. It creates an end rhyme
   - B. It changes the poem's meter to iambic pentameter
   - C. It isolates "I watched," making the act of watching the first emphasis and the fading of the light the second
   - D. It proves that the poet is the speaker
   - **Answer:** C. The break divides one sentence into two emphases: the reader pauses after "I watched," so the watching stands alone before the light fades. Breaks can shift emphasis this way without creating rhyme, changing meter, or revealing the poet's identity.
