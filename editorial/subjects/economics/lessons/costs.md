# Costs of Production

## In 30 Seconds

Every firm faces costs, and economists sort them carefully. In the short run, fixed costs stay the same whatever you produce, while variable costs rise with output; together they are total cost. Divide costs by quantity and you get average costs; track the cost of one more unit and you get marginal cost. Marginal cost eventually rises, average total cost is usually U-shaped, and marginal cost cuts through average total cost at its lowest point. In the long run, every input can change, and scale itself becomes a decision.

## Why This Matters

Cost curves are the backbone of how firms decide what to produce and what to charge. Once you can read them, the later theories of competition, monopoly, and market supply stop feeling like new material and start looking like the same machinery applied again. The fixed-versus-variable split explains why a factory keeps running at a loss during a downturn, why the first units off a new production line are so expensive, and why a struggling business chases volume. Economies of scale shape which industries end up dominated by a few giant firms and which stay full of small ones, a pattern that drives real debates about market power.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish fixed costs from variable costs and combine them into total cost in the short run.
- Compute average fixed, average variable, and average total cost, and marginal cost, from a cost table.
- Explain why marginal cost eventually rises and why average total cost is typically U-shaped.
- Explain why the marginal cost curve intersects average total cost at its minimum point.
- Distinguish short-run from long-run costs and identify economies and diseconomies of scale on the long-run average cost curve.

## The College Version

### Fixed, variable, and total cost in the short run

Economists split a firm's costs by asking a single question: if output changed right now, would this cost change too? Costs that would not change are fixed costs. They are the price of inputs the firm is locked into for the current period, such as a factory lease, insurance, or specialized machinery. A bakery pays the same monthly rent whether it bakes one loaf or a thousand. Costs that rise and fall with output are variable costs, the price of inputs the firm can adjust immediately, such as flour, electricity, and hourly labor. Total cost is simply the two added together: TC = FC + VC. The distinction only makes sense over a defined time horizon. The short run is the period during which at least one input is fixed, so the firm can change output only by using more or fewer variable inputs. That single locked input is what gives fixed cost its meaning; stretch the horizon far enough and every cost becomes changeable.

### Four per-unit costs: AFC, AVC, ATC, and marginal cost

Totals tell you the firm's overall bill; per-unit costs tell you how efficiently it is producing, and they are what firms actually compare against price. Average fixed cost is fixed cost divided by quantity (AFC = FC/Q). Because the numerator never changes, AFC falls continuously as output grows, spreading a fixed bill over more and more units; managers call this "spreading the overhead." Average variable cost is variable cost divided by quantity (AVC = VC/Q), and average total cost is total cost divided by quantity (ATC = TC/Q). A useful identity ties them together: because TC = FC + VC, dividing everything by Q gives ATC = AFC + AVC. Marginal cost is different in kind. It is not an average but the cost of producing one more unit: MC = change in total cost divided by change in quantity (ΔTC/ΔQ). Since fixed cost does not change, marginal cost comes entirely from the extra variable inputs the next unit requires. Marginal cost is the number a firm watches most closely, because the decision to make one more unit turns on whether that unit adds more to revenue than to cost.

### Why the curves take their shapes

Plot these per-unit costs against output and clear patterns appear. Marginal cost typically falls at first and then rises. The rise is the money-side shadow of a physical fact from the production topic: diminishing marginal returns. When a fixed input such as factory space is crowded by ever more variable input such as workers, each additional worker adds less extra output, so each additional unit of output costs more to produce. That is the whole reason marginal cost eventually climbs. Average total cost is typically U-shaped. At low output it is high because fixed cost is spread thin over very few units, so AFC dominates. As output grows, AFC shrinks and pulls ATC down. Eventually, rising marginal cost overtakes the falling-AFC effect and ATC turns back up. The turning point is governed by a precise relationship: the marginal cost curve passes through both the average variable cost curve and the average total cost curve exactly at their minimum points. The logic is the same logic as a test average. If your next quiz score (the marginal value) is below your current average, it drags the average down; if it is above, it pushes the average up; the average bottoms out at the moment the marginal value crosses it. So while MC is below ATC, ATC falls; once MC rises above ATC, ATC rises; they meet at ATC's lowest point.

### From the short run to the long run: economies of scale

In the long run, no input is fixed. The firm can build a bigger factory, sign different leases, or install more machines, so every cost becomes variable and the firm gets to choose its scale. For each possible plant size there is a short-run ATC curve; the long-run average cost curve (LRAC) is the lower envelope of all of them, tracing the cheapest way to produce each quantity once the firm is free to pick the best-sized plant. The LRAC has its own shape. Where producing more lets per-unit cost fall, the firm enjoys economies of scale, often from specialization, bulk buying, or spreading large setup costs over more units. OpenStax illustrates this with an alarm-clock maker whose small plant produces each clock for about $12 while a larger plant produces each for about $4. A flat middle stretch, where scaling up leaves average cost roughly unchanged, is constant returns to scale. Push scale too far and diseconomies of scale set in: extra layers of management and communication problems make each unit more expensive, and LRAC turns upward. Note the sharp contrast with the short run. Rising short-run ATC comes from diminishing returns to one variable input against a fixed plant; rising long-run cost comes from a firm growing too large to coordinate even when it can change everything. How much scale matters differs by industry, which is one reason some markets are dominated by a few large firms while others stay full of small ones. Deciding how much of the next unit's cost should sway a production choice is the province of marginal thinking, and turning these cost curves into output and pricing decisions belongs to the market-structure topics.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Fixed cost (FC):** A cost that does not change with the level of output in the short run, such as rent on a leased building or insurance premiums.
- **Variable cost (VC):** A cost that rises or falls as output changes, such as spending on raw materials, hourly labor, or electricity used in production.
- **Total cost (TC):** The sum of fixed and variable costs at a given level of output: TC = FC + VC.
- **Marginal cost (MC):** The added cost of producing one more unit of output, calculated as the change in total cost divided by the change in quantity (ΔTC/ΔQ).
- **Average fixed cost (AFC):** Fixed cost divided by the quantity produced (FC/Q); it falls continuously as output rises because a fixed amount is spread over more units.
- **Average variable cost (AVC):** Variable cost divided by the quantity produced (VC/Q).
- **Average total cost (ATC):** Total cost divided by the quantity produced (TC/Q); it equals AFC plus AVC and is typically U-shaped.
- **Short run:** The time horizon over which at least one input is fixed, so output can change only by adjusting variable inputs.
- **Long-run average cost curve (LRAC):** The curve showing the lowest average cost of producing each quantity when the firm can vary every input, including its scale; it is the lower envelope of the short-run ATC curves.
- **Economies of scale:** A situation in which the per-unit cost of production falls as the firm increases its scale of output; the opposite case, where per-unit cost rises with scale, is diseconomies of scale.

## Eli-10

Imagine you run a lemonade stand. Some costs you pay no matter what, like renting the table for the day. Those are fixed. Other costs go up the more lemonade you make, like lemons and sugar. Those are variable. Add them and you get your total cost. Now ask two different questions. First, on average, what does each cup cost me? That is total cost split evenly across all the cups. Second, what did just the next cup cost me? That is marginal cost, and it is the number you really watch, because if the next cup costs less than what people pay, making it is worth it. Early on, making more cups is cheap and spreads your table rent over more cups, so the average cost per cup drops. But squeeze too much lemonade-making into one small stand and each extra cup gets harder and more expensive, so eventually the average creeps back up. That is why the average-cost picture looks like a valley: down, bottom, then up.

## Eli's Analogy

Marginal cost pulling average cost around works exactly like your grade point average. Each new grade is the "marginal" one. Score below your current average and it drags the average down; score above it and it lifts the average up. Your average is lowest right at the moment a rising new grade crosses it, which is exactly where marginal cost cuts through average total cost.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The grade analogy captures the pull between a marginal value and an average, but grades have a ceiling of 100 and you cannot "produce more" of a single grade, whereas a firm can keep producing units and its costs have no fixed maximum. The analogy also says nothing about fixed versus variable costs or about long-run scale, which have no natural counterpart in a GPA.

## Worked Example

Take a firm with fixed cost of $50 and this short-run schedule. At Q = 1 through 5, variable cost is $40, $70, $95, $130, $185, so total cost (FC + VC) is $90, $120, $145, $180, $235.

| Q | FC | VC | TC | MC | AFC | AVC | ATC |
|---|----|----|----|----|-----|-----|-----|
| 1 | 50 | 40 | 90 | 40 | 50.00 | 40.00 | 90.00 |
| 2 | 50 | 70 | 120 | 30 | 25.00 | 35.00 | 60.00 |
| 3 | 50 | 95 | 145 | 25 | 16.67 | 31.67 | 48.33 |
| 4 | 50 | 130 | 180 | 35 | 12.50 | 32.50 | 45.00 |
| 5 | 50 | 185 | 235 | 55 | 10.00 | 37.00 | 47.00 |

Average total cost (TC/Q) falls, reaches a minimum of $45.00 at Q = 4, then rises: a U-shape. Marginal cost, the jump in total cost for each extra unit, is $40, $30, $25, $35, $55 — it falls then rises. Watch the crossing: at Q = 4 the marginal cost of that unit ($35) is below ATC ($45), so ATC is still falling; at Q = 5 marginal cost ($55) is above ATC ($47), so ATC turns up. Marginal cost cuts through average total cost right at ATC's minimum near Q = 4, exactly as the theory predicts. (Arithmetic checked in code: AFC + AVC equals ATC at every row.)

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating average total cost and marginal cost as the same thing, or assuming MC always lies below ATC.** ATC is the cost per unit averaged over all units; MC is the cost of just the next unit. MC lies below ATC only while ATC is falling; it rises above ATC once ATC starts climbing, and the two are equal only at ATC's minimum.
- **Believing average fixed cost is U-shaped like average total cost.** AFC falls continuously toward zero as output rises, because a constant fixed cost is divided by an ever-larger quantity. The U-shape of ATC comes from AVC and rising marginal cost, not from AFC.
- **Saying fixed costs are costs the firm cannot avoid, ever.** Fixed costs are fixed only in the short run, while some input is locked in. In the long run every input can change, so no cost is fixed; fixed versus variable is defined relative to the time horizon, not to whether a cost feels unavoidable.
- **Confusing diminishing marginal returns (short run) with diseconomies of scale (long run).** Rising short-run marginal cost comes from adding a variable input to a fixed plant, so returns diminish. Diseconomies of scale are a long-run phenomenon in which a firm that can change every input still sees rising average cost because it has grown too large to coordinate.
- **Reading the long-run average cost curve as just another single short-run curve.** The LRAC is the lower envelope of many short-run ATC curves, one for each possible plant size. It shows the cheapest way to produce each quantity once the firm is free to choose its scale, not the cost for one fixed plant.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Fixed cost vs. Variable cost:** Fixed cost does not change with output in the short run (rent, insurance); variable cost rises and falls with output (materials, hourly labor). They sum to total cost.
- **Average total cost (ATC) vs. Marginal cost (MC):** ATC is total cost spread over all units produced; MC is the cost of the single next unit. MC drives the direction of ATC and crosses it at ATC's minimum.
- **Diminishing marginal returns (short run) vs. Diseconomies of scale (long run):** Diminishing returns raise short-run marginal cost when a variable input crowds a fixed input; diseconomies of scale raise long-run average cost when a firm free to vary all inputs grows too large to manage.

## Key Takeaway

Split costs into fixed and variable, turn totals into per-unit averages and a marginal cost, and the picture is consistent: marginal cost eventually rises, average total cost is U-shaped, and marginal cost passes through average total cost at its lowest point. In the long run the firm also chooses its scale, trading economies of scale against diseconomies of scale.

## Practice Question Bank

1. A bakery signs a 12-month lease on its shop and also buys flour fresh each day to bake with. In the short run, how are these two costs correctly classified?
   - A. Both the lease and the flour are variable costs
   - B. The lease is a fixed cost and the flour is a variable cost
   - C. The lease is a variable cost and the flour is a fixed cost
   - D. Both the lease and the flour are fixed costs

   **Answer: B.** In the short run the lease payment does not change with how much the bakery bakes, so it is a fixed cost. Flour is bought in proportion to output, rising and falling with production, so it is a variable cost.

2. A firm's total cost is $180 when it produces 4 units and $235 when it produces 5 units. What is the marginal cost of the 5th unit?
   - A. $47, the average total cost at 5 units
   - B. $37, the average variable cost at 5 units
   - C. $11, one quarter of the cost increase
   - D. $55, the increase in total cost from the 5th unit

   **Answer: D.** Marginal cost is the change in total cost divided by the change in quantity: ($235 − $180) / (5 − 4) = $55. Option A is ATC, option B is AVC once fixed cost is removed, and option C wrongly divides the increase by 5.

3. As a firm steadily increases output in the short run, what happens to its average fixed cost (AFC)?
   - A. It falls continuously, because a fixed total is spread over more and more units
   - B. It rises continuously, because fixed costs grow as the firm produces more
   - C. It is U-shaped, falling at first and then rising like average total cost
   - D. It stays exactly constant at every level of output

   **Answer: A.** AFC = FC/Q. The numerator is fixed, so dividing it by a larger and larger quantity makes AFC fall continuously toward zero. Fixed cost does not grow with output, AFC is not U-shaped (that describes ATC), and it is the total fixed cost, not the average, that stays constant.

4. On a standard cost diagram, the marginal cost curve crosses the average total cost curve exactly at the lowest point of ATC. What is the reason?
   - A. Marginal cost is always equal to average total cost at every output level
   - B. Fixed costs fall to zero at the quantity where the two curves meet
   - C. When MC is below ATC it pulls ATC down, and when MC is above ATC it pulls ATC up, so ATC bottoms out where MC crosses it
   - D. Average variable cost happens to equal average fixed cost at that quantity

   **Answer: C.** Marginal cost relates to the average the way a new grade relates to a GPA: a below-average next unit drags the average down and an above-average next unit lifts it up, so the average is at its minimum exactly where marginal cost passes through it.

5. An alarm-clock maker finds that a large plant can produce each clock for about $4, while a small plant produces each clock for about $12. Over this range, the firm is experiencing:
   - A. Diseconomies of scale, because larger operations are always harder to manage
   - B. Economies of scale, because per-unit cost falls as the firm produces at larger scale
   - C. Constant returns to scale, because the firm can change all of its inputs
   - D. Diminishing marginal returns, because it added variable labor to a fixed plant

   **Answer: B.** Per-unit cost falls from $12 to $4 as the firm moves to a larger scale, which is the definition of economies of scale. Diseconomies of scale would mean per-unit cost rising, constant returns would leave it roughly unchanged, and diminishing marginal returns is a short-run effect rather than a long-run choice of plant size.
