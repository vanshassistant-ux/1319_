# The Business Cycle

## In 30 Seconds

The business cycle is the short-run rise and fall of real GDP around its long-run growth trend. It runs through four phases: expansion, a peak, contraction (a recession), and a trough, before recovery begins. A recession is a broad, sustained decline in activity, not merely two down quarters of GDP. In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research officially dates recessions, weighing the depth, diffusion, and duration of the downturn across many indicators.

## Why This Matters

Whether the economy is expanding or contracting shapes almost everything people feel: jobs, wages, business profits, and the taxes and spending that governments plan around. Knowing which phase you are in, and what an official recession actually means, keeps you from mistaking one weak quarter for a genuine downturn or a temporary rebound for lasting growth. Leading indicators give an early read on where activity is heading, which matters to anyone making hiring, investment, or policy decisions. And separating short-run swings from the long-run growth trend is the first step in diagnosing whether a problem is cyclical and temporary or structural and lasting.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the business cycle and describe its four phases: expansion, peak, contraction (recession), and trough.
- Distinguish the popular two-consecutive-quarters rule from the NBER's official definition of a U.S. recession.
- Explain how the NBER dates recessions using the depth, diffusion, and duration of a broad decline across many indicators.
- Distinguish leading indicators from lagging indicators and give examples of each.
- Explain how short-run business-cycle fluctuations differ from long-run economic growth.

## The College Version

### The cycle and its four phases

Real output does not grow in a smooth line. Over decades the path of real GDP trends upward, but it is regularly interrupted by shorter-run rises and falls. That recurring pattern of expansion and contraction is the business cycle. Economists describe it with four phases. An expansion is a stretch in which real GDP rises: firms produce more, hiring increases, and unemployment tends to fall. Eventually the expansion loses momentum and reaches a peak, the high point where the economy turns from growing to shrinking. The contraction that follows is a period of falling activity, and a contraction serious enough to be widely felt is called a recession. The decline continues until it reaches a trough, the low point where the economy stops shrinking and begins to recover. From the trough a new expansion begins, and the cycle repeats. So a recession runs from peak to trough, and the following upswing runs from trough back to peak. The peak and the trough are turning points, not long phases; the expansion and the contraction are the stretches of time between them. Because these swings are irregular in length and depth, no two cycles are identical, and the word "cycle" should not be read as a fixed, predictable clock.

### What counts as a recession: rule of thumb versus official call

In casual use, a recession is often defined as two consecutive quarters of declining real GDP. That shortcut is easy to apply and roughly tracks the truth, but it is not the official standard in the United States. The recognized arbiter is the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), a private nonprofit whose Business Cycle Dating Committee assigns the official peak and trough dates for U.S. cycles. The NBER defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. Three ideas sit behind that sentence: depth (how sharply activity falls), diffusion (how widely the decline spreads across industries and regions), and duration (how long it lasts). The committee treats these criteria as somewhat interchangeable, so an unusually deep and broad decline can qualify even if it is short. The committee also does not rely on GDP alone. It weighs a range of monthly indicators, giving particular weight in recent decades to real personal income less transfer payments and to nonfarm payroll employment, alongside measures such as industrial production, real consumer spending, and manufacturing and trade sales. Crucially, the NBER dates cycles retrospectively: it waits until enough data have arrived to be confident, so a recession is usually announced well after it began. This is why the quarter-counting rule and the official call can diverge, and why the NBER, not a mechanical formula, has the final word in the United States.

### Reading the cycle: leading and lagging indicators

Because the official dating comes late, analysts watch statistics that hint at where the cycle is heading. Indicators are grouped by their timing relative to the overall economy. Leading indicators tend to turn before the economy as a whole. The Conference Board bundles ten of them into its Leading Economic Index, which aims to anticipate turning points by roughly seven months; its components include building permits for new housing, average weekly initial claims for unemployment insurance, new orders from manufacturers, the S&P 500 stock index, the interest-rate spread between long-term Treasuries and the federal funds rate, and consumer expectations. Coincident indicators move roughly in step with real GDP and describe the present: payroll employment, personal income less transfers, industrial production, and manufacturing and trade sales. Lagging indicators turn after the economy has already changed direction; a classic example is the average duration of unemployment, which keeps rising for a while even after a recovery has begun, because firms rehire cautiously. No single indicator is decisive, and leading indicators give false signals from time to time, so forecasters read them as a group rather than trusting any one number.

### The cycle versus long-run growth, with dated examples

It is worth separating two different phenomena that both involve real GDP. Long-run economic growth is the rise of the trend itself over decades, driven by productivity, capital, skills, and technology; that is the subject of the economic-growth topic. The business cycle is the shorter-run wobble of output around that trend. A recession is a dip below the recent path; a recovery is the climb back toward it. Two recent U.S. recessions, both dated by the NBER, show the range. The Great Recession ran from a peak in December 2007 to a trough in June 2009, an eighteen-month contraction and the longest since the 1930s. The COVID-19 recession, by contrast, ran from a peak in February 2020 to a trough in April 2020, a contraction of just two months and the shortest on NBER record. The committee still classified 2020 as a recession because the decline was so deep and so widely diffused across the economy that it qualified even though it was brief. The two episodes illustrate both ends of the definition: a long, grinding downturn and a short, violent one, each an interruption of the long-run growth path rather than a change in the path itself.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Business cycle** — The recurring short-run pattern of expansion and contraction in real economic activity, tracing the movement from peak to trough and from trough back to peak.
- **Expansion** — A phase in which real GDP rises over time, typically with growing production, more hiring, and falling unemployment.
- **Peak** — The turning point at which an expansion ends and the economy begins to contract; the high point of the cycle before a decline.
- **Contraction (recession)** — A phase of falling economic activity; a contraction broad and sustained enough to be widely felt is called a recession.
- **Trough** — The turning point at which a contraction ends and a recovery begins; the low point of the cycle.
- **Recovery** — The early part of a new expansion, when activity climbs back toward its pre-recession level after passing the trough.
- **National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER)** — The private nonprofit whose Business Cycle Dating Committee is the recognized U.S. arbiter of official peak and trough dates for recessions.
- **Depth, diffusion, and duration** — The three interrelated criteria the NBER weighs when judging a recession: how sharply activity falls, how widely the decline spreads, and how long it lasts.
- **Leading indicator** — A statistic that tends to change direction before the overall economy does, such as building permits or initial unemployment claims, used to anticipate turning points.
- **Lagging indicator** — A statistic that tends to change direction after the overall economy has already turned, such as the average duration of unemployment.

## Eli-10

Imagine the economy as someone walking up a long hill that never really ends. Overall they keep climbing, but they don't move at a steady pace. Sometimes they speed up and feel great (an expansion), then they get tired at the top of a rise (a peak), slow down and even step backward for a while (a recession), reach the bottom of a dip (a trough), and start climbing again (recovery). Those ups and downs around the general climb are the business cycle. When the backward step is big and affects almost everyone, people call it a recession. In the United States, a group called the NBER is the official scorekeeper that decides when a recession really started and ended, and it looks at lots of signs, not just one number.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a hiker climbing a mountain trail that keeps rising overall. Along the way there are small dips and climbs. A recession is one of the noticeable downhill stretches, and the peak and trough are the top of a rise and the bottom of a dip. Weather forecasts along the trail are like leading indicators: darkening clouds warn of a rough patch before it hits, while a soaked jacket that stays wet afterward is a lagging sign that the storm already passed.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The trail hides two things. First, the long uphill climb (long-run growth) is driven by completely different forces than the dips (the cycle) — better tools and know-how versus swings in spending and confidence. Second, a hiker feels every dip instantly, but a real recession is only confirmed later, once enough data arrive, so in real life nobody rings a bell the moment the downhill begins.

## Worked Example

Consider the 2020 downturn. Real GDP fell in both the first and second quarters of 2020, so the popular "two consecutive quarters of decline" rule would flag a recession. But the NBER's Business Cycle Dating Committee dated the episode from monthly data instead: it set the peak in February 2020 and the trough in April 2020, a contraction of just two months and the shortest on NBER record. The committee explained that the drop was so deep and so widely diffused across the economy that it counted as a recession even though it was brief. Compare the Great Recession, dated from a December 2007 peak to a June 2009 trough, an eighteen-month slide. The pair shows why the official call and the quarter-counting shortcut can diverge: the NBER weighs depth, diffusion, and duration across many monthly indicators and dates the turning points after the fact, rather than mechanically counting quarterly GDP.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the two-consecutive-quarters rule as the official definition of a recession.** That is a widely used rule of thumb, not the U.S. standard. The NBER's official definition is a significant decline in activity spread across the economy lasting more than a few months, judged by depth, diffusion, and duration — so the two can diverge.
- **Assuming a recession is declared in real time, the moment activity turns down.** The NBER dates cycles retrospectively, waiting until enough data arrive to be confident. Recessions are usually announced well after their start and end dates, not as they happen.
- **Thinking GDP is the only thing that determines a recession.** The NBER weighs many monthly indicators, giving particular weight to real personal income less transfers and to nonfarm payroll employment, alongside industrial production and sales. Quarterly GDP is one input, not the whole test.
- **Confusing the business cycle with long-run economic growth.** Growth is the rise of the trend over decades, driven by productivity and capital. The cycle is the short-run fluctuation of output around that trend. A recovery returns output toward the trend; it does not raise the trend itself.
- **Assuming all economic indicators move together.** Indicators are grouped by timing. Leading indicators turn before the economy, coincident indicators move with it, and lagging indicators turn after — which is why forecasters read them as a set rather than trusting one number.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Two-consecutive-quarters rule vs. NBER official definition** — The rule of thumb mechanically counts two quarters of falling real GDP; the NBER judges a broad, sustained decline by depth, diffusion, and duration across many monthly indicators and is the recognized U.S. arbiter, so the two can reach different verdicts.
- **Leading indicator vs. lagging indicator** — A leading indicator (such as building permits) tends to turn before the economy and helps anticipate the cycle; a lagging indicator (such as the average duration of unemployment) turns after the economy has already changed direction.
- **Business cycle (short-run fluctuation) vs. economic growth (long-run trend)** — The cycle is the short-run wobble of real GDP around its path, including recessions and recoveries; long-run growth is the sustained rise of the path itself over decades, driven by productivity, capital, skills, and technology.

## Key Takeaway

The business cycle is the short-run swing of real GDP around its long-run growth trend, moving through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. In the United States a recession is not simply two down quarters: the NBER officially dates recessions by weighing the depth, diffusion, and duration of a broad decline across many indicators, usually after the fact.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which sequence lists the phases of a business cycle in their correct order?**
   - A. Expansion, peak, contraction, trough
   - B. Peak, expansion, trough, contraction
   - C. Contraction, trough, peak, expansion
   - D. Trough, peak, contraction, expansion

   **Answer: A.** An expansion runs up to a peak, which is the turning point into a contraction (recession); the contraction falls to a trough, the turning point into the next expansion. The other options place the turning points and phases out of sequence — for example, a peak cannot precede the expansion that produces it, and a contraction does not lead directly into a peak.

2. **A country's real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters, yet the NBER does not classify the period as a recession. Which is the best explanation?**
   - A. This is impossible, because the NBER defines a recession solely as two down quarters of GDP
   - B. The NBER is required to wait exactly one year before it can make any determination
   - C. Real GDP plays no role at all in the NBER's decisions
   - D. The NBER judges depth, diffusion, and duration across many indicators, and a shallow or narrow decline may not meet that broader standard

   **Answer: D.** The NBER's official definition is broader than the two-quarter rule: it looks for a significant decline spread across the economy lasting more than a few months, weighed by depth, diffusion, and duration across many monthly indicators. A decline that is shallow or confined to a few sectors can fail that test even if GDP falls for two quarters. Option A restates the rule of thumb the NBER does not use; there is no fixed one-year waiting rule; and GDP is one of the inputs the committee considers, not irrelevant.

3. **An analyst wants an early signal of where the economy is likely to head over the next several months. Which of these is a leading indicator suited to that purpose?**
   - A. The average duration of unemployment
   - B. Building permits for new private housing
   - C. Industrial production for the current month
   - D. The number of quarters real GDP has already declined

   **Answer: B.** Building permits are a classic leading indicator: firms and households commit to future building before construction and spending actually occur, so permits tend to turn before the broader economy. The average duration of unemployment is a lagging indicator that keeps rising after a recovery begins; current industrial production is a coincident indicator that moves with real GDP; and counting quarters that have already declined looks backward rather than ahead.

4. **In the United States, which organization is generally regarded as the official arbiter that dates the start and end of recessions?**
   - A. The Bureau of Labor Statistics
   - B. The Federal Reserve Board of Governors
   - C. The National Bureau of Economic Research
   - D. The Congressional Budget Office

   **Answer: C.** The National Bureau of Economic Research, through its Business Cycle Dating Committee, assigns the official peak and trough dates for U.S. business cycles and is the recognized arbiter of when recessions begin and end. The Bureau of Labor Statistics produces employment and price data, the Federal Reserve conducts monetary policy, and the Congressional Budget Office analyzes budgets and the economy for Congress, but none of them officially date U.S. recessions.

5. **The NBER dated the 2020 U.S. recession from a peak in February 2020 to a trough in April 2020 — just two months. What does this dating best illustrate?**
   - A. A downturn must last at least a year before it can be called a recession
   - B. The trough marks the highest point of the business cycle
   - C. A recession is measured only from quarterly GDP figures
   - D. A very short downturn can still be classified as a recession when the decline is deep and widely diffused

   **Answer: D.** The committee explained that the 2020 drop was so deep and so widely spread across the economy that it qualified as a recession even though it lasted only two months, the shortest on NBER record. This reflects the interchangeable criteria of depth, diffusion, and duration. There is no minimum one-year requirement; the trough is the low point, not the high point; and the NBER weighs many monthly indicators, not quarterly GDP alone.
