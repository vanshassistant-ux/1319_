# Unemployment

## In 30 Seconds

Unemployment is not simply "not working." To be counted as unemployed, a person must have no job, be available for work, and have actively searched in the last four weeks. The unemployment rate is the unemployed divided by the labor force, where the labor force is the employed plus the unemployed. Economists sort unemployment into frictional, structural, and cyclical types, and the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics measures it monthly with a survey of about 60,000 households.

## Why This Matters

The unemployment rate is one of the most watched numbers in the economy, shaping how central banks set interest rates, how governments plan budgets, and how businesses read demand. Knowing exactly who the count includes and excludes lets you read a jobs report without being misled: a falling rate can mean more people found work, or it can mean discouraged workers stopped looking and left the count entirely. Understanding the frictional, structural, and cyclical pieces also tells you which unemployment a recession creates and which persists even in a healthy economy, so you can tell a temporary downturn from a lasting mismatch of skills to jobs.

## Learning Objectives

- Define who is counted as employed, unemployed, and not in the labor force under the BLS criteria.
- Calculate an unemployment rate and a labor-force participation rate from population figures.
- Distinguish frictional, structural, cyclical, and seasonal unemployment.
- Explain the natural rate of unemployment and what economists mean by full employment.
- Explain how the BLS measures unemployment, and how U-3 differs from the broader U-6 measure.

## The College Version

### Who counts as unemployed

Being out of work is not enough to be counted as unemployed. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) places every civilian aged 16 and older who is not in prison, a nursing home, or the military into one of three groups. The **employed** are people who did any paid work in the reference week, plus those temporarily absent from a job. The **unemployed** must satisfy three conditions at once: they had no job, they were available to work, and they had actively looked for work at some point in the prior four weeks. Everyone else — retirees, full-time students, people caring for family, and anyone not searching — is **not in the labor force**. That third category is why "not working" and "unemployed" are not the same thing. A retired 70-year-old and a discouraged worker who has given up searching are both jobless, but neither is counted as unemployed, because neither is actively looking. The active-search requirement is the hinge the whole measurement turns on, and it is the source of most misreadings of the jobs numbers.

### The rate and the participation rate

The **labor force** is the employed plus the unemployed — everyone who is either working or actively trying to. The **unemployment rate** is the share of that labor force without a job:

> unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100

Notice the denominator: it is the labor force, not the whole population. People not in the labor force never enter the calculation, which is why the rate can fall for two opposite reasons — unemployed people finding jobs (good news) or unemployed people giving up the search and dropping out of the labor force (not good news). The **labor-force participation rate** fixes this blind spot by measuring how many adults are in the labor force at all:

> participation rate = labor force ÷ adult (civilian noninstitutional) population × 100

Reading the two together is essential. If the unemployment rate falls while participation also falls, people may simply be leaving the count rather than finding work. In the United States the participation rate was about 61 to 62 percent in recent years, per BLS.

### Frictional, structural, cyclical, and seasonal

Economists split unemployment by cause. **Frictional** unemployment is the short-term joblessness of people moving between jobs or entering the labor market — a new graduate searching, or someone who quit to find a better fit. It exists even in a strong economy because matching workers to jobs takes time, and it is generally seen as healthy. **Structural** unemployment is a lasting mismatch between the skills or locations workers have and the skills or locations employers want; it arises when technology or trade shifts demand away from certain skills, or when workers never acquired the skills now in demand. **Cyclical** unemployment is the part that rises and falls with the business cycle: in a recession, firms facing weak demand cut jobs, and because wages are slow to fall, more people want work at the going wage than can find it. **Seasonal** unemployment follows the calendar — ski instructors in summer, farm labor in winter — and the BLS removes it through seasonal adjustment so month-to-month changes reflect the economy rather than the season. Cyclical unemployment is the one recessions create and expansions cure; the business-cycle topic owns that story, and this lesson connects to it rather than re-teaching it.

### The natural rate and full employment

Even a healthy economy never reaches zero unemployment, because frictional and structural unemployment persist while people search and while skills realign. Economists call the unemployment that remains when cyclical unemployment is zero the **natural rate of unemployment** — essentially the frictional plus structural components. When the actual unemployment rate equals the natural rate, the economy is said to be at **full employment**, and real output is at its potential level. Full employment therefore does not mean everyone has a job; it means no one is out of work purely because the economy is in a downturn. The natural rate is not a fixed constant. It drifts over time with demographics, technology, the generosity and design of unemployment benefits, and how efficiently the labor market matches workers to openings. Because it cannot be observed directly, it is estimated rather than measured, which is one reason economists disagree about exactly where it sits at any moment. (The trade-off between unemployment and inflation — the Phillips curve — belongs to the inflation topic.)

### How the BLS measures it: the household survey and U-3 vs. U-6

The headline number comes not from counting everyone but from a survey. Each month the Census Bureau conducts the **Current Population Survey (CPS)**, often called the household survey, interviewing roughly 60,000 households and asking about each member's recent work and job search. From those answers the BLS estimates the national totals and computes the rate. The official, most-cited figure is called **U-3**: the unemployed as a share of the labor force under the standard definition. But U-3 leaves out people whose difficulty finding full work is real. The BLS therefore also publishes broader measures. **Marginally attached** workers want and are available for work and looked within the past 12 months but not the past 4 weeks, so U-3 excludes them; **discouraged** workers are the subset who stopped looking specifically because they believed no jobs were available. The broadest measure, **U-6**, adds all marginally attached workers and people working part time who want full-time work ("part time for economic reasons") to the unemployed, as a share of a correspondingly enlarged labor force. U-6 runs well above U-3 — often close to double — which is why looking only at the headline rate understates how many people are underused. In July 2026, the official U-3 rate was 4.1 percent, per BLS.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Labor force** — The total of employed people plus unemployed people: everyone who is either working or actively seeking work.
- **Unemployed (BLS definition)** — A person with no job who is available for work and has actively looked for work at some time in the prior four weeks.
- **Not in the labor force** — People who are neither employed nor actively seeking work, such as retirees, full-time students, and discouraged workers.
- **Unemployment rate** — The number of unemployed people divided by the labor force, expressed as a percentage.
- **Labor-force participation rate** — The labor force divided by the adult civilian noninstitutional population, expressed as a percentage.
- **Frictional unemployment** — Short-term unemployment as people move between jobs or enter the labor market and search for a match.
- **Structural unemployment** — Long-lasting unemployment caused by a mismatch between the skills or locations of workers and the jobs available.
- **Cyclical unemployment** — Unemployment that rises in recessions and falls in expansions as overall demand for labor changes with the business cycle.
- **Natural rate of unemployment** — The unemployment rate that remains when cyclical unemployment is zero, made up of frictional and structural unemployment; the rate at full employment.
- **U-6** — The broadest BLS labor-underutilization measure, adding marginally attached workers and part-time-for-economic-reasons workers to the unemployed.

## Eli-10

Imagine everyone old enough to work stands in one of three lines. Line one is people with a job. Line two is people with no job who are trying to get one right now. Line three is people who aren't trying — they're retired, in school, or have given up. The unemployment rate only compares line two to lines one and two together. Line three is left out completely. So if someone in line two stops trying and walks over to line three, the unemployment rate actually goes down, even though they didn't find a job. That is why smart people always check a second number — how many people are in the working lines at all — before deciding whether a falling rate is good news.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a job search like waiting for a bus. Frictional unemployment is the normal few minutes everyone waits between buses. Structural unemployment is showing up at a stop the bus no longer serves — the route changed and your stop is gone. Cyclical unemployment is a snowstorm that makes every bus run late at once. The natural rate is the ordinary waiting that never disappears even on a perfect day.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** buses run on a fixed schedule while the job market has no timetable, and a snowstorm ends on its own, whereas ending a recession can take deliberate policy. It also treats each waiting person separately, while real unemployment is affected by how everyone searches at once.

## Worked Example

Take the imaginary town of Rivertown. Its adult civilian population (age 16+, not institutionalized) is 200,000. Of these, 120,000 are employed and 8,000 have no job but are available and actively searched in the last four weeks. The remaining 72,000 are retired, in school, caring for family, or otherwise not looking.

First find the labor force: employed plus unemployed = 120,000 + 8,000 = **128,000**. The other 72,000 are not in the labor force.

- Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force = 8,000 ÷ 128,000 = **6.25 percent**
- Labor-force participation rate = labor force ÷ adult population = 128,000 ÷ 200,000 = **64 percent**

Notice that if 2,000 of the unemployed gave up searching and left the labor force, the labor force would fall to 126,000 and the unemployment rate would drop to 6,000 ÷ 126,000 = **4.76 percent** — lower, even though no one found a job. For a real anchor: the BLS reported an official U-3 unemployment rate of 4.1 percent for July 2026.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking anyone without a job counts as unemployed.** Only people who are available and actively searched in the prior four weeks count. Retirees, students, and discouraged workers who stopped looking are not in the labor force and are not counted as unemployed.
- **Dividing the unemployed by the whole population instead of by the labor force.** The denominator of the unemployment rate is the labor force (employed + unemployed), not the total or adult population. Dividing by the whole population gives a different, smaller number.
- **Assuming a falling unemployment rate always means more people found jobs.** The rate also falls when unemployed people give up searching and leave the labor force. Check the labor-force participation rate alongside it to tell the two apart.
- **Believing full employment means a zero percent unemployment rate.** Full employment is when the actual rate equals the natural rate — the frictional plus structural unemployment that persists even in a healthy economy. Some unemployment always remains.
- **Treating U-3 as the only measure of joblessness.** U-3 is the official headline rate, but the broader U-6 adds discouraged and marginally attached workers plus involuntary part-timers, and runs well above U-3.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Unemployed vs. Not in the labor force** — The unemployed are jobless but actively searching and available; those not in the labor force are jobless and not searching. Only the first group enters the unemployment rate.
- **Frictional vs. Cyclical unemployment** — Frictional is the short search time of people moving between jobs and exists in any economy; cyclical rises and falls with recessions and expansions and is what a downturn adds.
- **U-3 vs. U-6** — U-3 counts only the officially unemployed against the labor force; U-6 also counts discouraged and marginally attached workers and involuntary part-timers, giving a broader, higher figure.
- **Unemployment rate vs. Labor-force participation rate** — The unemployment rate measures joblessness within the labor force; the participation rate measures how large the labor force is relative to the adult population.

## Key Takeaway

The unemployment rate counts only people who are jobless AND actively searching, divided by the labor force — so read it alongside the participation rate, and remember that frictional and structural unemployment persist even at full employment.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Under the BLS definition, which person is counted as UNEMPLOYED?**
   - A. A 68-year-old who retired last year and is not looking for work
   - B. A recent graduate with no job who applied to several openings this week and is available to start
   - C. A full-time college student with no job who is not searching
   - D. A worker who stopped looking six months ago because she believed no jobs were available

   **Answer: B.** To be counted as unemployed a person must have no job, be available for work, and have actively looked within the prior four weeks. The graduate meets all three. The retiree and the student are not searching, so they are not in the labor force; the worker who gave up is a discouraged worker, also not in the labor force because she has not searched recently.

2. **A country has 150,000 employed people, 10,000 unemployed people, and 40,000 adults not in the labor force. What is its unemployment rate?**
   - A. 5.0 percent
   - B. 6.7 percent
   - C. 6.25 percent
   - D. 20.0 percent

   **Answer: C.** The labor force is employed plus unemployed = 150,000 + 10,000 = 160,000. The unemployment rate is 10,000 ÷ 160,000 = 6.25 percent. Dividing by the total adult population of 200,000 would wrongly give 5.0 percent, and dividing by 150,000 would give 6.7 percent; both use the wrong denominator.

3. **A factory closes because automation has permanently replaced its assembly workers, and those workers' skills are no longer in demand anywhere nearby. This is an example of which type of unemployment?**
   - A. Frictional unemployment
   - B. Cyclical unemployment
   - C. Seasonal unemployment
   - D. Structural unemployment

   **Answer: D.** Structural unemployment is a lasting mismatch between the skills workers have and the skills employers want, exactly what happens when technology permanently eliminates demand for a skill. Frictional unemployment is the brief search between jobs; cyclical comes from a recession; seasonal follows the calendar.

4. **In one month, many discouraged workers stop searching and leave the labor force, while the number of employed people does not change. Holding everything else constant, what happens to the official unemployment rate and the labor-force participation rate?**
   - A. The unemployment rate falls and the participation rate falls
   - B. The unemployment rate rises and the participation rate rises
   - C. Both rates stay exactly the same
   - D. The unemployment rate rises and the participation rate falls

   **Answer: A.** Workers who stop searching are no longer counted as unemployed and leave the labor force. That shrinks both the numerator and the denominator of the unemployment rate, and with employment unchanged the rate falls. The participation rate falls too, because the labor force is now a smaller share of the adult population. This is why a falling unemployment rate is not automatically good news.

5. **Which statement best describes the natural rate of unemployment and the U-6 measure?**
   - A. The natural rate is zero percent unemployment, and U-6 is the narrowest official measure
   - B. The natural rate includes cyclical unemployment, and U-6 excludes part-time workers
   - C. The natural rate equals whatever U-3 reports each month, and U-6 is always lower than U-3
   - D. The natural rate is the frictional plus structural unemployment that remains at full employment, and U-6 is broader than U-3 because it adds marginally attached and involuntary part-time workers

   **Answer: D.** The natural rate is the unemployment left when cyclical unemployment is zero — the frictional and structural components — and the economy is at full employment when the actual rate equals it. U-6 is broader than the official U-3 because it adds marginally attached workers and people working part time for economic reasons, so it runs above U-3, not below.
