# Seismic Waves

## In 30 Seconds

Earthquake energy travels as seismic waves. Fast P waves compress material, slower S waves shear material, and surface waves move along Earth’s surface. Seismograms record their arrivals; a P–S time gap is a clue to source distance.

## Why This Matters

Wave behavior turns an unseen rupture into measurable evidence. It helps scientists infer distance and material properties while showing why one station cannot provide a complete location.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish body and surface waves.
- Compare P and S motion.
- Explain S-wave behavior in liquid.
- Interpret a P–S arrival gap.
- Explain why multiple stations locate an event.

## The College Version

### Three useful wave categories

P and S waves are body waves because they travel through Earth’s interior. A P wave is compressional: material oscillates parallel to the direction the wave travels. It is fastest and normally arrives first. An S wave is a slower shear wave: material moves perpendicular to travel direction. Surface waves move along or near Earth’s surface and arrive later than principal body waves.

The categories are useful paths and motions, not a universal damage ranking. Surface waves can have relatively large amplitudes because their energy is confined near the surface. P waves can pass through solids and liquids; S waves require shear resistance and cannot travel through liquid. This evidence helped scientists infer properties of Earth’s interior.

### Read arrivals as evidence

A seismometer records ground vibration, creating a seismogram. One event can show P, S, and surface-wave arrivals; later waves do not mean a second event. Because P waves travel faster, the P–S arrival gap grows with distance from source to station. Travel-time relationships turn that gap into an estimated distance.

One distance defines a circle of possible locations around a station, not a unique point. Several station distances can be combined to constrain a location. Professional analysis uses more data and models. Analysts also distinguish real arrivals from wind, traffic, ocean microseisms, and transmission noise.

### Avoiding common wave errors

Wave labels do not predict a particular building’s response or a future earthquake. Actual effects depend on source, distance, frequency content, local geology, and structures. A station with a larger P–S gap is generally farther from the source, but its record alone cannot provide source direction, magnitude, fault type, or a forecast.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Seismic wave:** Vibration carrying seismic energy.
- **Body wave:** Wave traveling through Earth’s interior.
- **P wave:** Fast compressional body wave.
- **S wave:** Slower shear body wave.
- **Surface wave:** Wave traveling along Earth’s surface.
- **Seismometer:** Instrument recording ground vibration.
- **Seismogram:** Recorded trace of ground motion.
- **Travel time:** Time from source to station.

## Eli-10

An earthquake sends several kinds of shakes outward. P waves are fast squeezes, S waves shake sideways, and surface waves move near the ground’s surface. Instruments record them, and comparing P with S arrival gives a clue to distance.

## Eli's Analogy

A slinky shows push-and-squeeze motion like P waves, and a rope shows side-to-side motion like S waves. Two runners leaving together at different speeds show why arrival gaps grow with distance.

These objects do not reproduce Earth’s layers or real seismic paths. The analogy shows only relative motion and timing; one station cannot give direction or predict a future event.

## Worked Example

Station A has a short P–S gap and Station B has a longer gap for the same earthquake. Since P is faster, B is farther from the source. Several stations are then needed to locate the event, and records must be checked for non-earthquake noise.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling P and S separate earthquakes:** One rupture can generate several wave types.
- **S means surface:** S means shear; surface waves are separate.
- **One station gives an exact epicenter:** It gives distance only.
- **Every wiggle is seismic:** Records can include environmental and recording noise.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P wave | S wave | Faster compression parallel to travel versus slower shear perpendicular to travel. |
| Body wave | Surface wave | Interior path versus surface path. |
| Distance estimate | Location | One station versus a network. |

## Key Takeaway

P, S, and surface waves carry one event’s energy by different paths and motions. Arrival order is evidence when interpreted with suitable limits.
