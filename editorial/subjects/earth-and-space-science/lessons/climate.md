# Climate

## In 30 Seconds

Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere: today's temperature, wind, and rain. Climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region, usually described with statistics such as 30-year climate normals. What sets a region's climate includes latitude, solar energy, atmospheric circulation, oceans, topography, and altitude. The Köppen system groups climates into tropical, dry, temperate, continental, and polar families. Climate also varies naturally through seasons, El Niño and La Niña, and occasional volcanic eruptions.

## Why This Matters

Climate shapes the world we build. Farmers choose crop varieties using climate normals; utilities plan seasonal energy use; cities and settlements are sited and designed around the weather they expect. Ecosystems, from rainforest to tundra, reflect the long-term temperature and rainfall patterns of their regions. Understanding climate as long-term statistics, rather than a single day's weather, is also the foundation for reading any discussion of droughts, floods, or long-term change. The distinction matters for every decision that looks beyond the forecast.

## Learning Objectives

- Distinguish weather from climate, and explain how 30-year climate normals describe climate.

## The College Version

### Weather versus climate: it's about time

Weather is what the atmosphere is doing right now: the temperature, humidity, wind, clouds, and precipitation at a particular place and time. It changes over minutes, hours, days, and weeks. Climate differs in one essential way: it is not a snapshot but a statistical description. Climate describes what the weather is like in a region over a long period, meaning the typical temperatures, how much rain or snow usually falls, and how windy it tends to be. NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information captures the idea simply: climate is what you expect; weather is what you get. When scientists describe climate, they often use climate normals, averages of weather observations over a uniform 30-year period. The current official U.S. normals, for example, are computed from the years 1991 through 2020 using records from nearly 15,000 stations, and they are recalculated every decade in keeping with World Meteorological Organization conventions. Normals tell you what to expect, not what any single day will bring: the average July temperature is a statement about climate; this afternoon's high is weather.

### What sets a region's climate

A region's climate is the product of several factors working together. Latitude comes first: the Sun's rays strike the equator more directly than the poles, so each square meter near the equator receives more solar energy than one at high latitude. That is why the warmest climates hug the equator. Atmospheric circulation redistributes that heat: winds and ocean currents carry warmth from the tropics toward the poles, so a coastal region at high latitude can be far milder than its latitude alone would suggest. Oceans matter because water absorbs and releases heat slowly: places near the ocean tend to have smaller swings between summer and winter than places far inland at the same latitude. Topography also reshapes climate, because mountains block moisture and create wet windward and dry rain-shadow sides. Altitude matters too: temperature drops with elevation, which is why tropical highlands can be cool even close to the equator. No single factor decides a climate; each region is the sum of all of them.

### Sorting climates: the Köppen framework

Because climate varies so much from place to place, scientists group similar climates together. The most common framework is the Köppen system, developed by climatologist Wladimir Köppen and later refined with Rudolf Geiger, so it is often called the Köppen–Geiger classification. It sorts climates mainly by temperature and precipitation patterns, and its major groups are usually named tropical, dry, temperate, continental, and polar. Tropical climates (A) have every month averaging above about 18 degrees Celsius (64 degrees Fahrenheit) and are found in a band around the equator. Dry climates (B) lose more water through evaporation than they gain from precipitation. Temperate climates (C) have warm summers and mild winters, while continental climates (D) have cold winters with warm to cool summers. Polar climates (E) have year-round cold, with the warmest month averaging below 10 degrees Celsius (50 degrees Fahrenheit). The system also includes a highland group (H) for mountainous regions where elevation creates rapid climate changes over short distances. Classification is descriptive, not judgmental: a dry climate is not worse than a tropical one. The groups simply organize how the world's climates differ.

### Climate varies naturally

Climate is a long-term pattern, but it is not frozen. The most familiar variation is the seasons, caused by Earth's tilted axis: as the planet orbits the Sun, different hemispheres receive more direct sunlight at different times of year. On shorter cycles, the tropical Pacific swings between El Niño and La Niña, the warm and cool phases of ENSO (the El Niño–Southern Oscillation), roughly every three to seven years, shifting rainfall and temperature patterns around the globe. Volcanic eruptions can also nudge climate: when Mount Pinatubo erupted in June 1991, it injected about 20 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, where it formed sulfate aerosols that reflected sunlight and cooled the surface by as much as about 1.3 degrees Fahrenheit (0.7 degree Celsius) for a few years. These are natural fluctuations within a region's climate. The long-term shift of climate itself, climate change, is a separate topic that builds on the same distinction between weather and climate.

### Why climate matters

Climate sets the stage for life and human activity. Ecosystems reflect their climate: tropical rainforests, deserts, grasslands, and tundra each exist where long-term temperature and rainfall patterns favor certain plants and animals. Agriculture depends on the same patterns: farmers use climate normals to choose which crop varieties to plant, and utilities and planners use them to anticipate seasonal energy demand. Human settlement follows climate too: where water is reliably available and temperatures are tolerable, populations cluster; extreme climates support fewer people or demand different ways of living. Climate statistics never predict a specific day, but they describe the conditions a society can count on, which is why knowing the difference between weather and climate is practical knowledge, not just a vocabulary exercise.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Weather:** The state of the atmosphere at a particular place and time, including temperature, humidity, wind, and precipitation; it changes over short periods.
- **Climate:** The long-term pattern of weather in a region, described with averages and other statistics such as temperature and precipitation over decades.
- **Climate normal:** An average of weather observations over a uniform 30-year period, used as a reference for what is typical at a location.
- **Latitude:** Distance north or south of the equator, which determines how directly sunlight strikes a place.
- **Altitude:** Height above sea level; higher altitude generally means cooler temperatures.
- **Köppen system:** The common climate classification framework, whose main groups are tropical, dry, temperate, continental, and polar, with a separate highland group based on elevation.
- **ENSO:** El Niño–Southern Oscillation, the natural swing of the tropical Pacific between warm (El Niño) and cool (La Niña) phases that alters weather patterns around the world.
- **Climate variability:** Natural, temporary swings in climate, such as seasons, ENSO cycles, and the short-term cooling effects of large volcanic eruptions.

## Eli-10

Think of weather as the mood of the sky today and climate as the sky's personality over decades. One hot afternoon tells you nothing about whether a place is usually hot; you need years of records to see the pattern. That pattern comes from a few big influences: how much sunlight a place gets, how winds and ocean currents move heat around the planet, how close the sea is, and how high the land rises, because mountains and elevation cool and dry things in predictable ways. Scientists sort the world's climate patterns into families — tropical, dry, temperate, continental, and polar — using the Köppen system. The pattern is not perfectly steady: seasons swing every year, El Niño and La Niña every few years, and a big volcano can cool the planet briefly. But those are wobbles around the long-term pattern, not the pattern itself.

## Eli's Analogy

Your wardrobe is a climate; today's outfit is the weather. You choose each morning's clothes from the range your closet offers: the climate sets the possibilities, and the weather picks one of them. A single cold snap is one outfit; the fact that your closet holds coats at all is climate.

The analogy has limits. A wardrobe is something you own and can change at will, while climate is a statistical description of a place, not a thing, and it changes slowly on its own through natural variability and, over long timescales, long-term change. Your closet's contents were chosen deliberately; no one designs a region's climate. And one cold day never proves the climate shifted, because statistics need decades.

## Worked Example

North Texas in 2015 shows the difference between weather and climate in one record. The climate normal for annual rainfall at Dallas–Fort Worth is 36.14 inches (918 mm), the 30-year average. In 2015, the airport measured 62.61 inches (1,590 mm), nearly 1.7 times the normal, and roughly a normal year's rain fell in just three months: May, October, and November. The record year was weather, an extreme swing within the region's climate. The normal itself, built from three decades of observations, is the climate statement: what North Texas can expect on average, and what its farms, reservoirs, and flood planning are designed around.

## Common Mistakes

- **A cold week means the climate is changing.** One week is weather, not climate. Climate is judged from decades of statistics; a single cold spell is a fluctuation within the pattern. Long-term change is a separate, slower question.
- **Climate is just the average temperature.** Climate statistics include precipitation, humidity, wind, sunshine, and other measures, not temperature alone.
- **All tropical places are hot because they sit near the equator.** Altitude matters too: tropical highlands can be cool or even cold despite their latitude, which is why the Köppen system includes an elevation-based highland group.
- **The Köppen groups rank climates from best to worst.** The groups are descriptive categories based on temperature and precipitation — a classification, not a value judgment.
- **Summer is hot because Earth is closer to the Sun then.** Seasons come from Earth's tilted axis, which changes how directly sunlight strikes each hemisphere. Earth is actually closest to the Sun during Northern Hemisphere winter.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weather | Climate | Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere, changing over hours to weeks; climate is the long-term statistical pattern of weather, described over decades. |
| El Niño | La Niña | El Niño is the warm phase of ENSO, with warmer-than-average central and eastern tropical Pacific water; La Niña is the cool phase, with cooler-than-average water there. |
| Tropical climate | Polar climate | Tropical climates have every month averaging above about 18 degrees Celsius (64 degrees Fahrenheit); polar climates have year-round cold, with the warmest month below 10 degrees Celsius (50 degrees Fahrenheit). |
| Climate normal | Daily forecast | A climate normal averages weather observations over 30 years and describes what to expect; a forecast predicts the specific weather for coming hours or days. |
| Natural climate variability | Climate change | Natural variability (seasons, ENSO, volcanic effects) is temporary fluctuation around the long-term pattern; the ongoing long-term shift of that pattern is the climate-change sibling topic. |

## Key Takeaway

Climate is the long-term statistical pattern of weather, shaped by latitude, solar energy, circulation, oceans, topography, and altitude, and it sets the conditions that ecosystems, farms, and communities depend on.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which statement best distinguishes weather from climate?**
   - Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere; climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region
   - Weather is global; climate is local
   - Weather is measured in decades; climate is measured in hours
   - Weather and climate are different names for the same measurements
   - **Answer:** Weather is the short-term state of the atmosphere; climate is the long-term pattern of weather in a region.

2. **What is a climate normal?**
   - The single hottest day ever recorded at a location
   - An average of weather observations over a uniform 30-year period
   - A forecast of tomorrow's temperature and precipitation
   - A measurement of ocean temperature at the equator
   - **Answer:** An average of weather observations over a uniform 30-year period.

3. **A city sits at high elevation in the tropics, close to the equator. Compared with tropical lowlands at the same latitude, its climate is likely to be**
   - hotter, because the equator receives direct sunlight
   - identical, because latitude is the only factor that matters
   - cooler, because temperature decreases with altitude
   - drier only, because mountains never affect temperature
   - **Answer:** cooler, because temperature decreases with altitude.

4. **Two cities lie at nearly the same latitude, but one is on the coast and the other far inland. The coastal city has milder winters. Which factor best explains the difference?**
   - The inland city receives more direct sunlight
   - The coastal city is closer to the equator
   - The coastal city is at higher altitude
   - The ocean stores and releases heat slowly, moderating coastal temperatures
   - **Answer:** The ocean stores and releases heat slowly, moderating coastal temperatures.

5. **Which observation is evidence about climate rather than about weather?**
   - Today's high temperature reached 34 degrees Celsius with thunderstorms by evening
   - A single storm dropped 150 mm of rain on the city in one day
   - The 30-year average July rainfall for the region is 110 mm
   - This week's wind speeds were unusually high
   - **Answer:** The 30-year average July rainfall for the region is 110 mm.
