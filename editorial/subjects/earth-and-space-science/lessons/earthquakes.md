# Earthquakes

## In 30 Seconds

An earthquake begins when rock suddenly slips along a fault after accumulated stress overcomes friction. The slip releases seismic waves that shake the ground. Magnitude describes source size; intensity describes shaking at a particular place, so one event can have many reported intensities.

## Why This Matters

The distinction between magnitude and intensity makes reports and maps more accurate. It explains why one earthquake can be felt differently in different places, without promising a date-specific prediction or site-specific advice.

## Learning Objectives

- Explain sudden fault slip.
- Distinguish hypocenter and epicenter.

## The College Version

### From stored stress to shaking

An earthquake is ground shaking caused by sudden slip on a fault. Tectonic motion can continue while rough fault surfaces remain held by friction, storing stress in surrounding rock. When driving forces overcome resistance, part of the fault slips and releases energy. Seismic waves carry that energy outward through Earth and can produce surface shaking.

The rupture starts within Earth at the hypocenter, or focus. The epicenter is the surface point directly above it. These terms locate an event; they are not measures of magnitude, damage, or shaking. A fault can extend beyond the starting point, and later lessons examine the different kinds of waves. Many earthquakes occur on faults associated with plate motion, but the immediate process is fault slip.

### Magnitude and intensity answer different questions

Magnitude measures the size of an earthquake at its source. On a given magnitude scale, each event has one value, estimated from instrumental recordings and properties of the rupture. Seismometers record ground vibration, and networks of instruments help determine time, location, and magnitude. Magnitude is not a report of what one person felt.

Intensity measures shaking or effects at a particular location. A single earthquake creates many intensity values across a region. Distance from rupture matters, but surface geology, rupture direction, depth, and other features can change shaking. A station on soft sediment can record different motion from one on bedrock at a similar distance. The Modified Mercalli Intensity scale commonly used in the United States describes site-specific effects with Roman numerals.

### Use measurements with limits

Instruments, maps, and physical models let scientists locate events and describe shaking, but they do not make exact short-term prediction routine. A seismogram or intensity map is evidence, while a claim about damage or a future event requires additional, location-specific information.

Imagine two communities affected by one earthquake. One is nearer the rupture and on soft sediment; the other is farther away on firmer rock. The first may experience stronger shaking, but that does not change the event’s magnitude or establish a personal risk forecast. The accurate foundation is bounded: fault slip releases waves, instruments describe the source, and local conditions shape experienced shaking.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Earthquake:** Ground shaking caused by sudden fault slip and radiated seismic energy.
- **Fault:** A fracture or fracture zone across which movement can occur.
- **Hypocenter:** The point within Earth where rupture starts.
- **Epicenter:** The surface point directly above the hypocenter.
- **Seismic wave:** Energy traveling through or along Earth after release.
- **Magnitude:** A measure of source size.
- **Intensity:** A measure of site-specific shaking or effects.
- **Seismometer:** An instrument that records ground vibration.

## Eli-10

Two rough pieces of rock can stay stuck while forces keep pushing. When they slip, stored energy makes waves through the ground. Scientists measure the event with instruments, but different places can feel different shaking from the same earthquake.

## Eli's Analogy

A snapped ruler against a desk makes vibrations that spread through the desk. The snap represents a sudden release, and locations at different distances can shake differently.

A ruler is not a fault, and desk vibrations do not reproduce Earth’s rock properties, rupture area, or real seismic waves. The analogy illustrates release and vibration travel, not forecasting or damage.

## Worked Example

Two stations record one earthquake. The source is assigned one magnitude. A nearer station on soft sediment records stronger shaking than a farther station on firm bedrock, so their intensities differ. That does not mean two earthquakes occurred or that the event acquired two magnitudes.

## Common Mistakes

- **Giving one event many magnitudes:** Magnitude is one source value on a chosen scale; intensity varies by location.
- **Using epicenter for rupture start:** The hypocenter is below ground; the epicenter is above it.
- **Reducing intensity to distance:** Local ground conditions also matter.
- **Predicting an exact local event:** General science does not supply that ability.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Magnitude | Intensity | Source size versus site-specific shaking. |
| Hypocenter | Epicenter | Subsurface start versus surface point above it. |
| Fault slip | Seismic wave | Rupture process versus energy traveling away. |

## Key Takeaway

Earthquakes result from sudden fault slip. Magnitude describes the source event and intensity describes shaking at specific places.
