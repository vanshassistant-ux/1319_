# Motivation

## In 30 Seconds

Motivation is the force that energizes, directs, and sustains what people do — the reason someone starts a task, aims at a goal, and keeps going when it gets hard. Classic theory splits it into intrinsic motivation, doing something for its own sake, and extrinsic motivation, doing it for an outside reward. Maslow, Herzberg, and McClelland each mapped human needs to explain what drives people at work. The honest framing: motivation is personal, and no single theory fits everyone — but fair pay, meaningful work, and recognition cover most of the bases.

## Why This Matters

Every organization runs on the effort people choose to give. Pay and policies can buy presence; they cannot buy care, persistence, or initiative. The classic motivation theories matter academically because they turned a vague problem — what makes people perform well — into testable ideas about needs and rewards, and they still frame how researchers talk about work today. They matter practically because the distinction between a raise and recognition changes how workplaces are designed: fair pay stops complaints, but challenging work and honest credit produce the effort employers actually want. And as jobs, teams, and careers keep changing, the ability to read what drives a person — including yourself — is a skill that keeps paying off.

- Apply the honest framing that motivation is personal and no single theory fits everyone.

## The College Version

### What motivation is: the force behind effort

The working definition used in management teaching comes from OpenStax's Principles of Management: motivation is a force within or outside of the body that energizes, directs, and sustains human behavior. The word itself comes from the Latin movere, meaning to move — motivation is what gets people moving. The definition carries three verbs on purpose. Energizing is the spark: a hungry person feels pushed to find food, an under-challenged employee feels pulled toward a harder project. Directing is the aim: the energy points at a target, such as a deadline, a sales goal, or a skill to master. Sustaining is the staying power: the effort continues past the first burst, through setbacks and boring stretches. OpenStax adds a practical companion definition: work motivation is the amount of effort a person exerts to achieve a certain level of job performance. The same section splits motivation into two components — direction, what a person wants to achieve, and intensity, how hard the person tries. Direction answers where; intensity answers how much.

### Intrinsic and extrinsic motivation

OpenStax describes extrinsic motivation as the drive to acquire something outside the person — rewards that satisfy lower-order needs, such as pay, bonuses, and safe working conditions. Intrinsic motivation arises out of performing the task itself, because it is interesting or enjoyable; the reward is the feeling of doing it well, what OpenStax calls rewards we give ourselves. Two original examples carry the distinction. Priya works the evening shift at a warehouse and hits her packing quota steadily because a monthly bonus depends on it — extrinsic, because the reward sits outside the task. Diego, a junior designer, rebuilds his portfolio site at night for the third time; no one asked, no one pays, and he keeps going because each version teaches him something — intrinsic, because the doing is its own reward. The two are not pure opposites: a person can be paid to do work they also love, and both drives can operate at once. Self-determination theory, which OpenStax presents, holds that tasks feel intrinsically motivating when they satisfy three higher-order needs: competence, autonomy, and relatedness.

### Maslow's hierarchy of needs

Psychologist Abraham Maslow proposed that human needs are arranged in a hierarchy: lower needs dominate attention until they are reasonably satisfied, then the next level takes over — the idea OpenStax calls prepotency. Five levels run from basic to growth. Physiological needs are the body's basics: food, water, sleep, oxygen. Safety needs cover freedom from danger and stability — at work, job security and safe conditions. Social needs are belonging, affection, and friendship — good coworkers and teams. Esteem needs are respect from others and self-respect — recognition, titles, and achievement. Self-actualization is becoming everything one is capable of becoming — growth and fulfilling one's potential. The hierarchy is famous partly because it is easy to picture as a pyramid and easy to apply: people act to satisfy unmet needs, and a satisfied need stops driving behavior. OpenStax also reports the honest research caveat: the strict everyone-climbs-in-the-same-fixed-order version is not supported — people do not march through the five levels uniformly — though there is evidence that lower-order needs generally get attention before higher-order ones.

### Herzberg's two factors and McClelland's three needs

Frederick Herzberg's motivator-hygiene theory splits the workplace into two sets of factors. Hygiene factors belong to the job context: pay, working conditions, supervision, and security. When they are missing or poor, people are dissatisfied; when they are adequate, dissatisfaction disappears — but satisfaction does not appear. They prevent pain; they do not produce motivation. Motivators belong to the job content: achievement, recognition, responsibility, and growth. These produce satisfaction and drive effort. Herzberg's striking conclusion is that satisfaction and dissatisfaction are not opposite ends of one scale — fixing low pay is not the same lever as fixing boring work. David McClelland's learned needs theory names three needs that vary across individuals and are learned over time. The need for achievement drives people to excel at difficult tasks, take moderate risks, and demand feedback. The need for affiliation drives people to build warm relationships and to do well in teamwork. The need for power drives people to influence and control others — and McClelland distinguishes personal power seekers from social power seekers, who use influence to reach group goals; social power is the variant associated with effective managers.

### Motivation at work, and the honest framing

Put the theories to work and the same practical themes appear. Fair pay and safe, decent conditions form the floor: they remove dissatisfaction and meet lower-order needs, but they do not by themselves make people care. Meaningful work — challenging duties, responsibility, a visible contribution — feeds achievement, esteem, and growth needs. Recognition — credit for good work, respect from others — feeds esteem and works as a motivator in Herzberg's sense. OpenStax's summary of Maslow's implications puts it directly: adequate pay and safe working conditions help people satisfy lower-order needs, and once those are met, challenging jobs, added responsibility, and meaningful recognition support the higher-order needs that drive real effort. And then comes the honest framing, which the same evidence forces: motivation is personal. Needs differ in strength from person to person — McClelland's three needs vary in intensity across individuals — and research supports no single fixed sequence for everyone. Theories are maps, not molds: each explains a slice of why people work, and real workplaces use all of them, tuned to the person in front of them.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Motivation** — A force within or outside of the body that energizes, directs, and sustains human behavior; at work, the effort a person exerts toward job performance.
- **Intrinsic motivation** — The drive to do a task for its own sake, because the activity itself is interesting or enjoyable, with no outside reward required.
- **Extrinsic motivation** — The drive to do a task in order to gain an outside reward, such as pay, a bonus, a grade, or praise.
- **Need** — A human condition that becomes energized when a person feels deficient in some respect, such as hunger or the desire for safety.
- **Hygiene factors** — Herzberg's term for job-context conditions such as pay, working conditions, supervision, and security; adequate levels prevent dissatisfaction but do not create satisfaction.
- **Motivators** — Herzberg's term for job-content factors such as achievement, recognition, responsibility, and growth; they produce satisfaction and drive effort.
- **Need for achievement** — McClelland's term for the drive to excel at difficult tasks, take moderate risks, and seek feedback on performance.
- **Need for affiliation** — McClelland's term for the desire to establish and maintain warm, friendly relationships with other people, which supports teamwork.
- **Need for power** — McClelland's term for the desire to influence, control, and take responsibility for other people, which can serve personal or group goals.
- **Self-actualization** — The top of Maslow's hierarchy: the desire to become everything one is capable of becoming, a need that is never fully satisfied.

## Eli-10

Motivation is the reason someone does something and keeps doing it. Think of a school project: you finish it because you genuinely want to understand the topic — that is intrinsic, because the drive comes from inside — or because you want the good grade and the scholarship money — that is extrinsic, because the drive comes from outside. The classic theories sort out the details. Maslow said people work through needs in order, from basic ones like food and safety up to growth. Herzberg said some things only stop you complaining, like pay and working conditions, while other things actually make you care, like achievement and recognition. McClelland said people differ in what they want most: to succeed, to belong, or to lead. None of the theories is the whole truth on its own, but together they explain most of why people work.

## Eli's Analogy

Motivation is like the engine and the steering wheel of a delivery van. The engine supplies the power — that is intensity, how hard the van can push. The steering wheel sets the route — that is direction, where the effort goes. Fuel and maintenance keep the van running without complaints, a bit like hygiene factors; but fuel alone never decides that the driver wants to reach the new neighborhood before dark. That desire comes from inside the driver, the way intrinsic motivation comes from inside a person.

The analogy breaks down because a van is mechanical: give it fuel and it behaves the same every day. People are not. The same reward that delights one employee bores another, needs change over time, and a person can be driven by several motives at once. A van never grows, never changes its mind, and never cares about the route — people do all three.

## Worked Example

Nia manages a bakery with four bakers. First she fixed the floor: new ovens, steady schedules, and wages brought in line with the local market. Complaints stopped, but nothing else changed — the bakers were neutral, not enthusiastic. That is hygiene in action. Then she read her people. Tom lit up when she asked him to develop a new sourdough line with a real deadline; he wanted the hard problem and the feedback — achievement needs. Maya, quiet on the line, ran the weekend market stall and came back energized; the customer contact fed her affiliation needs. When the sourdough sold out, Nia put Tom's name on the label and mentioned Maya's stall in the weekly note to staff — recognition, a motivator. Sales rose, but the real change was steadier: people brought ideas instead of complaints. Nia still pays fairly and keeps the ovens running — the floor stays solid — but the effort comes from the part of work that feeds each person's own needs.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Believing that more pay is the main way to motivate people.** Herzberg classifies pay as a hygiene factor: fair pay prevents dissatisfaction but does not itself create satisfaction or motivation. Lasting effort usually comes from motivators like achievement, responsibility, and recognition — with fair pay as the floor beneath them.
2. **Treating Maslow's hierarchy as a strict ladder that everyone climbs in the same order.** Maslow proposed the five levels and the prepotency idea, but research does not support a fixed sequence for everyone. People can have several active needs at once, and lower-order needs can re-energize. Use the levels as a checklist of what people generally need, not a script.
3. **Assuming intrinsic motivation means the work itself must be fun or glamorous.** Intrinsic motivation is about where the drive comes from — interest, pride, mastery — not about how entertaining the task is. A careful accountant who takes quiet pride in a perfectly balanced ledger is intrinsically motivated even though the work is routine.
4. **Reading McClelland's need for power as a warning sign in a manager.** McClelland distinguishes personal power from social power. Social power — influencing others toward group goals — is the variant associated with successful managers; it is personal power-seeking, controlling others for one's own sake, that causes problems.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Intrinsic motivation vs. extrinsic motivation.** The drive comes from inside the person — interest, mastery, pride in the task — versus from outside, such as pay, bonuses, grades, or praise. Both can operate at once, and research suggests heavy external rewards can crowd out intrinsic interest.
- **Hygiene factors vs. motivators.** Hygiene factors (pay, conditions, security) remove dissatisfaction when adequate but do not create satisfaction; motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, growth) create satisfaction and drive effort. They act on different scales.
- **Maslow's hierarchy vs. Herzberg's two factors.** Maslow arranges human needs in five stacked levels from physiological to self-actualization; Herzberg splits work factors into job-context hygiene and job-content motivators. Herzberg's motivators roughly correspond to Maslow's higher needs — esteem and self-actualization.

## Key Takeaway

Motivation is personal: the forces that drive people differ, and no single theory fits everyone. Fair pay, meaningful work, and genuine recognition cover most of what work can offer — and the rest depends on the person.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **OpenStax's Principles of Management defines motivation as a force that does which of the following?**
   - A. Energizes, directs, and sustains human behavior
   - B. Replaces ability whenever a worker lacks the right skills
   - C. Rewards employees in proportion to their output
   - D. Measures how many tasks an employee completes in a day
   - **Correct: A.** OpenStax defines motivation as a force within or outside of the body that energizes, directs, and sustains human behavior. The other options confuse motivation with ability, reward systems, or output measurement, which the definition does not claim.

2. **A bakery owner replaces the flickering lights, repairs the leaky roof, and raises wages to match other shops. Staff stop complaining, but no one seems more enthusiastic. In Herzberg's terms, these changes are which of the following?**
   - A. Esteem needs, because they show respect for staff
   - B. Need for achievement, because they set a higher standard
   - C. Hygiene factors, because they remove dissatisfaction without creating satisfaction
   - D. Motivators, because they improved the work environment
   - **Correct: C.** Herzberg classifies pay, working conditions, and other job-context factors as hygiene factors: adequate levels prevent dissatisfaction but do not produce satisfaction or motivation. Motivators, which create satisfaction, are job-content factors like achievement and recognition, so they do not fit this scenario.

3. **Rosa volunteers for the hardest project in her unit, asks for feedback after every milestone, and prefers goals that stretch her without being impossible. Which of McClelland's learned needs best explains her behavior?**
   - A. Need for affiliation
   - B. Need for power
   - C. Need for safety
   - D. Need for achievement
   - **Correct: D.** McClelland's need for achievement drives people to take on difficult tasks, prefer moderate risk, and seek feedback — exactly Rosa's pattern. Affiliation is about warm relationships and power about influencing others; safety is not one of McClelland's three learned needs.

4. **Marcus assembles custom bikes after hours and spends his own money on parts, because he loves getting the gears and brakes perfect — no one pays him extra for it. This behavior is best described as which of the following?**
   - A. Extrinsic motivation, because the finished bike is a tangible result
   - B. Intrinsic motivation, because the drive comes from the task itself
   - C. A hygiene factor, because it relates to working conditions
   - D. Need for power, because he controls the final product
   - **Correct: B.** Intrinsic motivation arises from performing the task itself — interest and mastery — with no external reward. Marcus acts for the satisfaction of the work, so the motivation is intrinsic; extrinsic motivation would require an outside reward such as pay or a bonus.

5. **New call-center hires keep worrying about unstable schedules and poorly maintained headsets, and they struggle to focus during training. According to Maslow's hierarchy, which level of need is most active for them?**
   - A. Esteem needs
   - B. Self-actualization needs
   - C. Safety needs
   - D. Social needs
   - **Correct: C.** Maslow's safety needs cover freedom from danger and stability — at work, job security and safe conditions. Unstable schedules and unsafe equipment activate safety needs, which dominate attention until reasonably satisfied, before social, esteem, or self-actualization needs can drive behavior.
