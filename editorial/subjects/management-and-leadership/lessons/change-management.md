# Change Management

## In 30 Seconds

Change management is the structured approach to moving an organization from its current state to a desired future state — on purpose, and through people, not just processes. The working definition here draws on SHRM and the OpenStax textbook. Change is hard because people resist loss and uncertainty. Changes come in two sizes: incremental and transformational. The classic playbook, from the Kotter tradition, runs: create urgency, build support, communicate, enable, sustain. The honest framing: change is managed in the minds of the people.

## Why This Matters

Organizations change constantly — new systems, new structures, new markets — and most of those changes succeed or fail on the people side. A new billing system only works if the people using it actually work differently. Leaders who ignore the human side get resistance, slowdowns, and quietly abandoned initiatives; leaders who manage it get adoption. Understanding change management shows you why people dig in, what leaders actually do about it — urgency, support, communication, enablement, and staying power — and why a plan on paper is not a change until people accept it.

## Learning Objectives

- Define change management as the structured approach to moving an organization from a current state to a desired future state, and attribute that working definition.
- Explain why change is hard: people resist loss and uncertainty, and prefer stable, predictable routines.
- Distinguish incremental change from transformational change, with an original example of each.
- Name the five classic steps — create urgency, build support, communicate, enable, sustain — and attribute them to the Kotter tradition.
- Explain why people resist change and describe, in general terms, how leaders respond.
- Apply the honest framing: change is managed in the minds of the people, so a plan is not a change until people accept and act on it.

## The College Version

### A working definition, attributed

Change management is the structured approach to moving an organization from a current state to a desired future state. Two sources build this definition. SHRM, the Society for Human Resource Management, defines change management as the systematic application of knowledge, tools, and resources to deal with change, stressing that it leads the “people side” of major change — not just the technical tasks. The OpenStax Principles of Management textbook defines planned change as intentional activity designed to create movement toward a specific goal or end. An organization sits in a current state — the way things are done today — and wants a desired future state; change management is the structured, intentional work of getting from one to the other, and it succeeds or fails through the people in the middle. A clinic moving from paper charts to digital records is a current state; records any staff member can call up in seconds is the future state; the change management is everything between the decision and the paper stopping.

### Why change is hard: loss and uncertainty

Change is difficult, and people often resist it — SHRM says so in plain terms. The deeper reason comes from Kurt Lewin's classic model, as OpenStax presents it: most people prefer a stable, predictable organization and grow accustomed to its routines. Those routines are not laziness; they are working knowledge. A change asks people to give up what they do well and trade it for something unproven — loss — and the new thing brings uncertainty. Lewin's point: existing norms and routines must be disrupted first. OpenStax adds a second, honest layer: employees often resist when they had no hand in shaping the plans or do not fully understand why the change is urgent or where it is headed. Resistance is not usually stubbornness — it is a normal human reaction to losing the familiar and facing the unknown.

### Two sizes of change: incremental and transformational

OpenStax names two very different sizes of change. Incremental change means small refinements in current practices or routines that build on what already exists rather than challenging it. A bakery that rearranges its prep stations and standardizes its dough checklist to shave ten minutes off the morning rush is making incremental change. Transformational change is a significant shift that disrupts an underlying structure, process, or system. When a regional newspaper that has printed for eighty years stops the presses and publishes digital-only, that is transformational: the product, the skills, and the daily rhythm change at once. OpenStax notes transformational change can invigorate some employees but is highly disruptive and stressful for others — one change can split a workforce into champions and resisters. Both sizes need managing; the bigger the disruption, the more the people side matters.

### The classic steps: the Kotter tradition

The most widely used playbook for leading change comes from John Kotter, a Harvard Business School professor; SHRM calls it a well-known, widely adopted approach. Kotter's original model has eight steps: urgency, a guiding coalition, a strategic vision, communicating that vision, removing obstacles, short-term wins, consolidating improvements, and anchoring the changes in the culture. This lesson groups those eight steps into five moves. Create urgency: make the case that the status quo cannot continue. Build support: assemble a guiding coalition of influential people to champion the change. Communicate: explain the why, the what, and the how to everyone affected, clearly and repeatedly. Enable: remove obstacles and give people the skills and resources to act. Sustain: celebrate short-term wins, consolidate progress, and hold the new way until it sticks. The five-move version is a simplification; the spine — urgency, coalition, vision, communication, enablement, wins, consolidation, anchoring — is Kotter's.

### Resistance: why people dig in, and how leaders respond

Resistance shows up in small and large forms: repeating questions, work that quietly returns to the old way, open refusal. The general practice is not to argue people into submission but to work with the reasons underneath. SHRM's guidance is concrete: build visible senior sponsorship, involve front-line managers early, train and communicate with them regularly, coach employees to adapt, and reward the behaviors the change needs. OpenStax's account of Kotter's model adds the same lesson from the other side — removing obstacles reduces resistance, and people who helped shape the plans rarely resist them. Leaders also name the loss honestly: what stays, what gets harder, what the payoff is supposed to be. The goal is not to eliminate resistance; it is to keep it from quietly burying the change.

### Communication in change — and the honest framing

Communication is built into the Kotter tradition as the vision-sharing step; the sibling topic on communication owns the mechanics, so here it is one practice: clear, repeated, honest. Clear means plain words about why the change is happening and what will differ. Repeated means more than once — SHRM advises multiple forms, multiple sources, and built-in repetition, and cites a Robert Half survey in which 65 percent of managers called clear and frequent communication the most important element in leading change. Honest means acknowledging the costs: what is given up, what is uncertain, what might go wrong. When a warehouse switched inventory systems, the manager explained why the old one was losing money, repeated the message in bulletins and one-on-one talks for three weeks, and said plainly the first month would be slower. That brings the reality check: change is managed in the minds of the people. The plan, the software, the memo — none of it is the change. The change is what people actually do and believe differently; until they accept it, nothing has changed.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Change management** — The structured approach to moving an organization from a current state to a desired future state; the working definition in this lesson draws on SHRM and the OpenStax textbook.
- **Current state** — The way an organization does things today, before a change; the starting point that the change leaves behind.
- **Desired future state** — The target condition the organization is trying to reach — the new process, structure, or way of working the change is meant to produce.
- **Planned change** — Intentional activity designed to create movement toward a specific goal or end, rather than change that happens by accident (OpenStax).
- **Incremental change** — Small refinements in current practices or routines that build on what already exists instead of challenging it (OpenStax).
- **Transformational change** — A significant shift that disrupts an underlying part of the organization — its processes, structures, or systems (OpenStax).
- **Guiding coalition** — The group of influential people a leader assembles to shape and champion a change; a key element of Kotter's model.
- **Resistance** — The human pushback to change — reluctance rooted in loss, uncertainty, and disrupted routines rather than simple stubbornness.
- **Adoption** — The point where people actually use the new way as their normal way of working, which is the real sign that a change has landed.

## Eli-10

Change management is how an organization moves from the way things are to the way they need to be — without leaving its people behind. It exists because change is hard: people get attached to their routines, and a new system or structure asks them to give up what they know for something uncertain. Leaders who do it well follow a pattern: make the case for why the old way cannot continue, gather influential people to champion it, explain it clearly and more than once, clear the obstacles out of the way, and keep pushing until the new way sticks. The uncomfortable truth is that a plan, a memo, or new software is not the change. The change is what people actually do differently, which is why change is managed in the minds of the people.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a family moving from their old house to a new one. The moving truck is the plan, but the move only happens when each person actually changes rooms, closets, and habits. The kid who knows exactly which floorboard creaks and the parent who can find anything in the old kitchen are the ones who resist, because the new house makes their hard-won knowledge useless until they learn it again. The parents leading the move have to explain why, win everyone over, repeat the plan, carry the boxes with everyone else, and keep unpacking until the new house feels like home.

Where the analogy breaks down: The moving-house analogy breaks down because a move is a one-weekend event and organizational change is not. You cannot unpack an organization in a weekend. People re-learn at different speeds, some quietly keep the old way in reserve, and the new routine stays fragile for months. A house move ends when the last box is unpacked; a change ends only when the new way has become the ordinary way — which is exactly why the honest framing matters.

## Worked Example

The Meridian Clinic decides to replace its paper patient charts with a digital records system, and the leadership works the five steps. Urgency: an internal review finds that three patient charts were misfiled in the past year, and the board explains that the clinic cannot keep accepting that risk. Support: the clinic director forms a small coalition — the lead nurse, the front-desk supervisor, and the oldest doctor on staff, the one everyone trusts. Communicate: the director explains at a staff meeting why the system is coming, what it will and will not change, and that the first month will be slower for everyone; she repeats the essentials in a written handout and in one-on-one talks over three weeks. Enable: the clinic schedules training in fifteen-minute blocks so shifts are not left short, and the lead nurse sits with anyone who is struggling. Sustain: the clinic celebrates the first full week with no paper charts, keeps a visible adoption board, and reviews the new routines at monthly meetings until the digital way becomes the ordinary way. The software was installed in one weekend; the change took four months, because it happened in the minds and habits of the staff.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the announcement as the change — “we told them, so it’s done.”** Fix: A memo or a meeting is not a change. The change is what people do differently, and that takes support, repetition, and time.
- **Blaming resistance on stubborn people.** Fix: Resistance is usually loss and uncertainty speaking. Find out what people are afraid of losing, and address that, instead of arguing them into submission.
- **Skipping urgency and going straight to instructions.** Fix: Without a convincing why, people have no reason to trade the known for the unknown. Make the case before you give the how.
- **Communicating once, then moving on.** Fix: Under-communication is the normal failure mode. Repeat the message through multiple channels, from multiple people, and answer the same question as many times as it takes.
- **Celebrating the rollout instead of the adoption.** Fix: A rollout is a beginning. Keep generating wins, consolidating progress, and anchoring the new routines, or the old way quietly returns.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Incremental change** versus **Transformational change**: Incremental change is small refinements that build on current practices — a bakery streamlining its morning prep. Transformational change is a significant shift that disrupts underlying structures — a newspaper going digital-only. Both need managing; transformational change disrupts more and stirs stronger resistance.
- **Planned change** versus **Unplanned change**: Planned change is intentional activity aimed at a specific goal, with objectives, steps, and indicators of success (OpenStax). Unplanned change happens by accident or informal organizing and may help or harm the organization. Change management is the discipline of making change planned rather than hoped-for.

## Key Takeaway

Change is managed in the minds of the people. Make the case, build support, communicate clearly and repeatedly, enable people to act, and sustain the new way until it sticks — because the plan is not the change; the change is what people actually do differently.

## Practice Question Bank

### Question 1

A regional clinic is replacing its paper patient charts with a digital records system over the next year. Under the working definition used in this lesson, change management is the —

- structured approach to moving the clinic from its current paper-based state to the desired digital state
- purchase and installation of the new records software
- decision by the clinic board that the paper system must end
- training session that teaches staff to open the new screens

**Answer:** structured approach to moving the clinic from its current paper-based state to the desired digital state

**Why:** The working definition, built from SHRM and the OpenStax textbook, is the structured approach to moving an organization from a current state to a desired future state — through people, on purpose. Buying software, making a decision, and running a training session are pieces of it, but none is the whole structured approach.

### Question 2

A city bus company replaces its entire diesel fleet with electric buses, rewrites every route, and retrains every driver in a single year. Which type of change is this?

- Incremental change
- A short-term win
- Transformational change
- An unplanned change

**Answer:** Transformational change

**Why:** OpenStax defines transformational change as a significant shift that disrupts underlying aspects of the organization — its processes, structures, or systems. Replacing the fleet, the routes, and the training all at once is a large disruption, not a small refinement.

*Difficulty: application · Skill: Applying the incremental-versus-transformational distinction*

### Question 3

A public library announces that it will stop charging late fines next month. A week later, several staff members are still telling patrons that fines will be charged — they missed the announcement entirely. Which practice from the change playbook would most directly have prevented this?

- Generating short-term wins
- Building a guiding coalition
- Removing obstacles
- Repeating the message through multiple channels

**Answer:** Repeating the message through multiple channels

**Why:** Communication in change must be repeated: SHRM advises communicating in multiple forms, from multiple sources, with repetition built in. A single announcement that reached only part of the staff is the classic under-communication failure. Wins, coalitions, and obstacle removal do not put the message in front of the people who missed it.

*Difficulty: application · Skill: Applying repeated, multi-channel communication to a change scenario*

### Question 4

A factory manager wants crews to adopt a new safety checklist. Three veteran workers keep skipping it, saying the old way is faster. Under this lesson’s honest framing, what does the manager most need to change?

- The wording of the checklist document
- The workers’ routines and their belief that the new checklist is worth using
- The factory’s machines and tools
- The length of the work shift

**Answer:** The workers’ routines and their belief that the new checklist is worth using

**Why:** The honest framing is that change is managed in the minds of the people: the plan and the document are not the change — the change is what people actually do and believe. The workers’ routines and their assessment of the checklist are the real targets; the document’s wording is secondary, and machines and shifts are not the issue.

*Difficulty: analysis · Skill: Analyzing a resistance situation through the honest framing*

### Question 5

A warehouse adopts a new order-picking system, and it works well for two months. Then crews slowly drift back to the old routes, and within six months only a few people still use the new system. Which step of the Kotter tradition was most likely neglected?

- Creating urgency
- Building support
- Sustaining the change until it sticks
- Removing obstacles

**Answer:** Sustaining the change until it sticks

**Why:** Kotter’s later steps are about making change last: generating short-term wins, consolidating improvements, and anchoring the new norms until they become the ordinary way. The system worked but was never anchored, so the old routines returned. Urgency, support, and obstacle removal got the change started but did not keep it.

*Difficulty: analysis · Skill: Analyzing a failed adoption against the Kotter steps*
