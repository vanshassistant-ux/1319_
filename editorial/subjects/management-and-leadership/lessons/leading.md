# Leading

## In 30 Seconds

Leading is the people function of management: influencing and motivating people to work toward the organization's goals. The working definition is attributed to OpenStax, which describes leadership as a social influence relationship among people who depend on each other to reach shared goals. Leading is not managing: managing coordinates resources and processes, while leading inspires people. Leaders set direction, motivate, communicate, and develop people. Leading draws on position and on personal influence, and it depends on followers as much as on the leader. Styles differ by situation.

## Why This Matters

Plans, schedules, and budgets do not move themselves; people move them. Leading is the part of management that decides whether people move willingly or just go through the motions. Almost every group you join — a work team, a club committee, a volunteer crew — runs on the same machinery: someone points the way, someone keeps the energy up, someone is trusted enough to be followed. Understanding what leading is helps you see that machinery working, tells you why a capable manager can still fail to inspire anyone, and prepares you for the day you are the one others look to. And because leading is a relationship, learning about it also teaches you about being a good follower — which most of us are, most of the time.

## Learning Objectives

- Define leading as influencing and motivating people to work toward the organization's goals, and attribute that working definition.
- Distinguish leading from managing: inspiring people versus coordinating resources and processes.
- List what leaders do — set direction, motivate, communicate, and develop people — with one line of evidence for each.
- Explain how leading draws on both position-based and personal sources of influence.
- Explain why leadership is a two-way relationship between leaders and followers rather than a one-way act.
- Recognize that the right way to lead depends on the situation and the people.

## The College Version

### A working definition, attributed

Leading is the third of the four classic management functions. After planning sets the direction and organizing builds the structure, leading is the people function: the part of management that works through human beings rather than through schedules, budgets, or procedures. OpenStax's Principles of Management defines leadership as a social (interpersonal) influence relationship between two or more persons who depend on each other to attain certain mutual goals in a group situation. In plainer words, leading is influencing and motivating people to work toward the organization's goals. Two parts of that definition do the real work. First, it is influence, not command: the people being led go along because they are persuaded, not merely because they must. Second, it is social and mutual: the leader and the followers depend on each other, and both are aiming at goals the group shares. That is the working definition this lesson uses, and it comes from OpenStax.

### Leading versus managing: inspiring versus coordinating

Leading and managing are close relatives, and everyday speech uses the words interchangeably, but the distinction matters. Managing is about coordination: resources, processes, schedules, budgets, procedures. Leading is about people: getting them to want to do the work. OpenStax notes that managers are generally appointed to their role, while leadership per se is a relationship that revolves around the followers' acceptance or rejection of the leader. A manager can get compliance through the position — the authority to assign work, reward, and correct. A leader gets willingness through influence. The two overlap in practice: the best managers also lead, and people without any managerial title lead all the time. This lesson keeps the contrast simple — managing coordinates things, leading inspires people — and leaves the full comparison to the sibling topic management-versus-leadership.

### What leaders do

Textbooks compress the work of leading into a short list, and OpenStax's account supports four items. Set direction: a good leader develops a vision for the group and keeps everyone pointed at it. Motivate: the leader orchestrates the group's energy and activity toward goal attainment, making the work feel worth doing. Communicate: a vision only counts if it is explained, repeated, and translated into everyday decisions — OpenStax describes the model leader as one who develops a vision and communicates that vision. Develop people: leaders build up the people around them; OpenStax describes transformational leaders' individualized consideration as a focus on the development of the follower, and the everyday version is the supervisor who coaches, the senior who mentors, the lead who hands over the interesting parts of the work. Four lines, one idea: leadership work is people work.

### Where the influence comes from: position and person

If leading works through influence, where does the influence come from? OpenStax distinguishes two broad sources. Position-based influence comes from the organization: the job carries rights to act — schedule production, contract to sell, hire and fire — and the organization legitimizes rewards and punishments that secure compliance. Personal influence comes from the followers themselves: their perception of the leader's knowledge and expertise, the leader's personality, and the working relationship built up over time. The shorthand is that position gets compliance while personal influence earns willingness, and most real leading runs on a mix of both. The deep treatment of power belongs to the sibling topic power-and-influence; this lesson only needs the general idea that leading draws on both sources.

### Leading is a relationship: the follower side

OpenStax describes leadership as a process — a complex and working relationship that develops over time between leader and follower. The components of that process are the leader, the followers, the context, the process itself, and the outcomes. Crucially, the influence runs both ways: the leader influences the followers, and the followers influence the leader; the situation shapes both. Follower behavior plays a major role in determining what leaders do. OpenStax is blunt about the consequence: leadership per se revolves around the followers' acceptance or rejection of the leader. A team that quietly ignores instructions has, in effect, declined the leadership on offer. That is the honest note this lesson wants to leave: leading is a relationship, not a one-way act.

### A preview: styles differ by situation and people

People do not lead in one fixed way. A leadership style is a person's recurring pattern of behavior toward the people they lead — directive, consultative, hands-off, and many variations in between. The important preview here is that no single style fits every organization or every situation. Corporate Finance Institute's explainer reaches that conclusion explicitly: the right approach depends on factors such as the type of organization and the people involved, and even a strong style fails when it is applied to the wrong context. OpenStax makes the same point — the leadership tactics that work for one group might fail for another. The styles themselves, their names, and their trade-offs belong to the sibling topic leadership-styles; this lesson only plants the idea that leading adapts.

## Key Vocabulary

- **leading** — The management function of influencing and motivating people to work toward the organization's goals.
- **leadership** — The process by which one person influences others to pursue shared goals, understood as a social relationship between leader and followers.
- **influence** — The capacity to shape what other people do or decide, without relying on formal authority alone.
- **follower** — A person who chooses to go along with a leader's direction, and whose acceptance or rejection of the leader shapes the leadership relationship.
- **vision** — A picture of a desired future that a leader develops and communicates to give the group direction.
- **position-based influence** — Influence that comes from an official role, such as the authority to assign work, reward, or discipline.
- **personal influence** — Influence that comes from who the leader is, including expertise, character, and the trust built in relationships.
- **leadership style** — A person's recurring way of behaving toward the people they lead, which should fit the situation and the group.

## Eli-10

Leading is the part of work where people pull together because they want to, not only because they have to. A manager can make sure supplies are ordered and the schedule is posted — that is coordinating. Leading is the human side: explaining where the group is going, caring about the people doing the work, and helping them get better at it. Leading runs on influence, the ability to shape what people do. Influence can come from your position, like being the boss with the power to assign work or correct mistakes, or from who you are — your knowledge, your honesty, the trust you have built over time. Both work, and most real leading uses a mix of the two.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a group of hikers on a trail. The person holding the map can get everyone to the summit — that is like managing. But on a steep, muddy stretch, the group follows the hiker who scrambles ahead, calls back encouragement, and reaches a hand down to slower members. That hiker has no map and no title, yet the group moves because she makes them want to reach the top together. That is leading.

Where the analogy breaks down: The analogy breaks down because a hike has one clear summit and a short time frame. In an organization, goals change, people stay for years, and influence is tested again and again. The hiking leader's pull is almost entirely personal; in a workplace, position-based influence — the authority to assign work or approve pay — matters too, and leaders have to balance the two rather than rely on goodwill alone.

## Worked Example

Marta runs the evening shift at a regional delivery warehouse. Half her job is managing: she assigns stations, checks packing lists, and records output. The shift only hums when she also leads. At the start of each shift she walks the floor, tells the crew what the day's orders are for and why they matter, and asks a veteran packer to show two new hires the folding technique rather than doing it herself. When the crew proposed music in the packing area, she ran a one-week trial and compared error rates before deciding. Her crew works harder on her shift not because she can write them up — she can — but because they want the shift to go well. That is leading: influence the crew accepts, pointed at the warehouse's goals.

## Common Mistakes

**Treating leading as the same thing as holding a title — assuming a supervisor is automatically a leader.**

Correction: The position gets compliance, not willingness. Leadership is a relationship that depends on followers' acceptance, so a titled manager can fail to lead and an untitled colleague can lead well.

**Using 'leading' and 'managing' as exact synonyms.**

Correction: Managing coordinates resources, processes, and schedules; leading influences and motivates people. The best managers also lead, but the two are distinct enough that a manager can coordinate everything and inspire no one.

**Thinking leadership is one-way: the leader acts and everyone else obeys.**

Correction: Leadership is an exchange relationship. Followers influence the leader too — their skills, expectations, and reactions shape what the leader does — and they can accept or reject the leader's direction.

**Assuming one way of leading works in every situation.**

Correction: The right approach depends on the task, the people, and the context; a tactic that works with one group can fail with another.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Managing** vs **Leading**: Managing coordinates resources, processes, and schedules; leading influences and motivates people. Both are needed, and the best managers also lead, but coordination without inspiration is management without leadership.
- **Compliance** vs **Commitment**: Compliance means doing the work because the position requires it; commitment means doing the work because you want the group to succeed. Leading aims at commitment — which is why the followers' acceptance matters.

## Key Takeaway

Leading is the people function of management: it works through influence rather than orders, it draws on both position and personal sources, and it is a relationship — leaders make others want to do the work, and followers make leaders effective.

### Question 2

A supervisor posts the weekly schedule, orders supplies, and enforces the safety checklist. A colleague with no title talks the crew into trying a new way of unloading trucks that everyone had resisted. Which statement best captures the difference between the two?

- The supervisor is leading and the colleague is managing
- Both are leading, because both influence the crew
- The supervisor is managing — coordinating resources and procedures — while the colleague is leading — influencing people to act
- Neither is doing management work, because neither is an executive

**Answer:** The supervisor is managing — coordinating resources and procedures — while the colleague is leading — influencing people to act

**Why:** Managing coordinates resources, processes, and schedules, which is what the supervisor does. Leading influences and motivates people, which is what the colleague does — and it can happen without any title. Option A reverses the pair, B ignores the coordination role, and D is wrong because management and leadership happen at every level.

### Question 3

Aya leads a volunteer crew building sets for a school play. The crew is experienced and enthusiastic, but the paint colors keep getting mixed up. Aya explains once why the color-coding system matters to the final show and asks the crew to try it. Which action of Aya's is an act of leading?

- Checking the supply list against the budget
- Writing the build-night schedule
- Ordering the missing paint
- Explaining the purpose of the color-coding and asking the crew to adopt it

**Answer:** Explaining the purpose of the color-coding and asking the crew to adopt it

**Why:** Leading works through influence: Aya persuades the crew by connecting the change to the goal and asking for their cooperation. Checking budgets, writing schedules, and ordering supplies are coordinating activities — useful managing, but not the influence-based work this lesson calls leading.
