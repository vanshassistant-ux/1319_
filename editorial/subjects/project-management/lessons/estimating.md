# Estimating

## In 30 Seconds

A project estimate is a reasoned approximation of an effort, duration, quantity, or cost made from information available at a particular time. It differs from a commitment: an estimate is a current forecast, while a commitment is a promise or obligation whose meaning depends on context. An estimate may use similar past work, a rate per unit, detailed components, or relevant expertise. Its assumptions and uncertainty should be visible.

## Why This Matters

Estimates help people discuss what a proposed project may require before every detail is known. Without them, a rough forecast can be mistaken for a fixed fact, or a precise-looking number can seem more reliable than its inputs. Ask what prior work is comparable, what rate and unit are used, which details are included, and which assumptions could change the result. These questions improve interpretation; they do not guarantee an outcome or determine what anyone should approve, promise, or spend.

## Learning Objectives

- Define an estimate and distinguish it from a commitment.
- Compare analogous, parametric, bottom-up, and expert-judgment approaches.
- Explain how assumptions and uncertainty affect an estimate.
- Calculate a simple parametric hypothetical estimate and state its conditions.
- Evaluate why a documented basis and range can be more informative than one unsupported number.

## The College Version

### An estimate is a current model, not a promise

An estimate is a reasoned approximation of something a project may require, such as work time, elapsed duration, materials, quantity, or cost. It uses information available when it is made. GAO's estimating guide identifies purpose, scope, schedule, technical baseline, assumptions, data, methodology, risk, documentation, and updates as relevant to developing an estimate. A displayed number is therefore the result of a model, not a fact detached from its basis. Two decimal places do not make weak scope or weak data reliable.

An estimate and a commitment are different kinds of statement. An estimate expresses what is currently expected under stated conditions. A commitment can mean a promise, authorization, contract term, or target, depending on context. This lesson offers no advice about obligations or procurement; it only notes that relabeling an estimate as a commitment can conceal uncertainty. A reader should ask whether a statement is a forecast, target, or obligation and what evidence supports it.

Estimates can change when material information changes. A clearer work description, revised quantity, new actual result, or failed assumption may alter the current approximation. Updating records what is known now; it neither proves earlier planning was pointless nor guarantees that a future outcome will occur.

### Four approaches fit different information situations

Analogous estimating starts with sufficiently similar prior work and adjusts for relevant differences. The Project Management for Instructional Designers OER describes it as scaling a similar prior estimate to the current work. It can be quick early in planning, but the comparison requires scrutiny: differences in scale, venue, accessibility needs, or content can make a direct copy misleading.

Parametric estimating applies a rate or relationship to a measurable quantity. A simple case is units multiplied by a rate per unit. The arithmetic must be correct, but the rate, unit, and conditions must also apply. A rate drawn from different work conditions can yield a precisely calculated but poorly grounded result.

Bottom-up estimating begins with defined smaller components and aggregates them. It can make the basis easier to inspect, but omitted work, duplicate work, and weak component assumptions can still distort the total. Expert judgment brings relevant experience to an estimate, especially when data are sparse, but it is not an unexplained certainty. These approaches can be combined as cross-checks; no one is universally best.

### Assumptions and ranges make uncertainty visible

An estimate is conditional on assumptions about scope, quantity, availability, productivity, calendar, historical data, or the way units are counted. GAO emphasizes documenting assumptions, methodology, limitations, sensitivity, risk, and uncertainty. A reader should be able to identify what must remain true for a number to make sense and what would change it. A range can communicate that several plausible values remain, although it is not a guarantee that reality will stay inside it.

Consider a hypothetical campus poster project. The planning model assumes 120 posters, a rate of $2.40 per poster, and one $36 setup amount. The variable portion is 120 × $2.40 = $288. Adding setup gives $288 + $36 = $324. This is only conditional: all posters must qualify for the rate, setup must apply once, and delivery, tax, design, and changed specifications must remain outside the illustrated scope. A changed quantity or inapplicable rate requires reconsideration.

The example does not direct a real purchase or create a commitment. It shows a transparent basis of estimate: quantity, rate, fixed component, calculation, and assumptions. Estimation supports a conversation about uncertainty; it does not remove uncertainty or replace contextual judgment.

## Key Vocabulary

- **estimate:** A reasoned approximation based on information and assumptions available at a stated time.
- **commitment:** A promise, obligation, target, or authorized undertaking whose meaning depends on context.
- **analogous estimate:** An approximation adjusted from similar prior work.
- **parametric estimate:** An approximation applying a documented rate or relationship to a measured input.
- **bottom-up estimate:** An approximation formed by adding estimates for defined smaller components.
- **expert judgment:** An informed assessment from people with relevant experience.
- **assumption:** A condition treated as true for planning even though later evidence may change it.
- **range:** A span of plausible estimated values that communicates uncertainty.

## Eli-10

An estimate is a careful guess, not a magic answer. Planning a bake sale, you might guess how many cookies to make and what ingredients may cost. The guess depends on what you know today. You can use a similar bake sale, multiply cookies by cost per cookie, add every ingredient, or ask someone experienced. Each can help, but each can miss something. A commitment is different: it is a promise or obligation.

### Eli's Analogy

An estimate is like predicting blocks for a model bridge. You might copy a similar bridge, multiply sections by blocks per section, count each piece, or ask an experienced builder.

The analogy has limits. Projects involve changing scope, people, conditions, and sometimes money or time. Blocks are simple, so the analogy cannot decide a real commitment, prove a number is correct, or guarantee a forecast.

## Worked Example

A student club hypothetically plans 120 identical event posters. Its preliminary model uses a printing rate of $2.40 per poster plus one setup amount of $36. The variable amount is 120 × $2.40 = $288; the total is $288 + $36 = $324. This is not a commitment or purchase instruction. It assumes the quantity is final, every poster qualifies for the same rate, setup occurs once, and delivery, tax, design, and changed specifications are outside scope. If those assumptions change, reconsider the estimate.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating an estimate as an automatic promise.** Identify whether a statement is a forecast, target, or obligation and retain its assumptions.
- **Using a prior project without checking comparability.** Explain differences in scope, scale, conditions, or complexity.
- **Assuming correct multiplication proves reliability.** Check the rate, quantity, inclusions, exclusions, and conditions too.
- **Reporting one number without a basis.** Document the approach, inputs, assumptions, limitations, and uncertainty.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Estimate | Commitment | An estimate is a conditional current approximation; a commitment is a promise or obligation set by context. |
| Analogous | Parametric | Analogous estimating adjusts a similar prior case; parametric estimating applies a rate or relationship to a measured input. |
| Bottom-up | Expert judgment | Bottom-up aggregates defined components; expert judgment contributes relevant experienced assessment. |

## Key Takeaway

An estimate is a transparent, conditional approximation rather than a certainty or automatic commitment. The method, data, assumptions, exclusions, and uncertainty matter as much as the displayed number.

   - B. A final number that cannot change once recorded.
   - C. A reasoned approximation based on available information and assumptions. **Correct.**
   - D. A list of every possible risk in a project.

2. **A planner adjusts the cost of similar earlier work for a larger quantity. Which approach is used?**
   - A. Bottom-up estimating.
   - B. Parametric estimating.
   - C. A commitment calculation.
   - D. Analogous estimating. **Correct.**

3. **A model has 75 units at $4 per unit and one fixed $50 amount. What total does it produce?**
   - A. $350. **Correct.**
   - B. $300.
   - C. $125.
   - D. $4,050.

4. **Why can a precise-looking estimate still be uncertain?**
   - A. Estimates may never use data.
   - B. Its scope, data, assumptions, and conditions may be incomplete or change. **Correct.**
   - C. Bottom-up estimates cannot be added together.
   - D. An estimate is always an organizational commitment.

   - A. State the method, data, calculation, assumptions, and important exclusions or uncertainty. **Correct.**
   - B. Replace every estimate with a promise.
   - C. Use only expert judgment and omit its basis.
   - D. Report a number without explaining what it includes.
