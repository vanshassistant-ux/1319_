# Stakeholders

## In 30 Seconds

Stakeholders are people, groups, or institutions that can affect a project, are affected by it, or have a legitimate interest in its result. Identifying them means looking beyond the project team to understand relevant users, operators, sponsors, partners, and others. Interests, influence, and impact help a team ask better questions; they do not rank human worth or give anyone automatic control. Respectful, honest, context-aware communication keeps useful information visible.

## Why This Matters

Projects can miss important needs when they focus only on the people funding or building a result. A stakeholder perspective helps learners notice who may use, maintain, depend on, be burdened by, or make decisions about a change. It also discourages a common mistake: treating communication as persuasion. Good engagement is a two-way effort to understand relevant information, explain what is known and unknown, and make room for concerns without promising agreement or a particular outcome.

## Learning Objectives

- Define stakeholder in a project context.
- Identify different ways a person or group can be connected to a project.
- Distinguish interest, influence, impact, and formal decision authority.
- Apply ethical and context-aware communication principles to a hypothetical project.
- Explain why stakeholder information should be revisited as evidence changes.

## The College Version

### Stakeholders are connected to a project in more than one way

A stakeholder is a person, group, or institution that can affect a project, can be affected by it, or has a legitimate interest in its result. The definition is deliberately broader than the project team. A funder, sponsor, staff member, user, neighboring community, partner organization, regulator, maintenance group, or service recipient may each be connected differently. Not every possible connection is equally relevant to every project, and the list should be based on evidence rather than assumptions. The point is to look for material relationships that a narrow team-only view might miss.

Identification is therefore an inquiry, not a ceremony. A project may examine its purpose, intended result, current situation, likely users, operating environment, dependencies, and foreseeable effects. It can ask who provides needed information, who will operate or support the result, who may experience a benefit or burden, and who has formal responsibility for a decision. These questions are useful even when the team does not yet know the answer. An unknown affected group or an unresolved constraint is information to investigate, not an excuse to declare the analysis finished.

Being named as a stakeholder does not mean a person has the same role as a sponsor or a formal decision maker. It also does not mean every preference becomes a requirement. A person who uses a service may have first-hand knowledge of a problem; an operator may know a practical constraint; a decision maker may have authority established elsewhere. These are different kinds of relevance. Keeping them distinct avoids two errors: excluding someone because they lack formal power, and implying that informal influence automatically creates decision authority.

### Interest, influence, and impact are lenses, not labels for controlling people

Stakeholder analysis often considers interests, influence, and impact. Interest asks what outcome, concern, responsibility, or information need is relevant to a stakeholder. Influence asks how a person or group may affect the project or its environment, for example through knowledge, resources, authority, cooperation, public response, or control of a dependency. Impact asks how the project may change a stakeholder's work, access, safety, time, costs, or experience. None of these is a measure of a person's value, and none alone answers what the project should do.

A simple stakeholder record might name a group, describe its connection to the project, note what is known and uncertain, and identify an appropriate opportunity for information exchange. It is a working model, not a psychological profile. Recording that transit riders may need clear outage information, for example, is different from guessing what they will support. Recording that an operations team will maintain a service is different from deciding that team must accept an untested plan. Evidence, humility, and clear limits matter because a project team can easily turn a descriptive map into an unjustified story about people.

Conflicting interests are normal. A proposed change may make a service easier for one set of users while adding work for operators, or it may give a sponsor a desired result while introducing a concern for a community. Listing those differences does not settle the tradeoff. It makes the tradeoff discussable and clarifies what additional evidence may be needed. This general-education lesson does not tell an organization who has final authority, how to negotiate a dispute, or which concern must prevail. It teaches the prior analytical step: do not hide relevant effects or constraints by acting as if only one viewpoint exists.

### Engagement and communication should be honest, accessible, and adaptable

Engagement means creating a way to exchange relevant information with stakeholders, not simply sending announcements. The information and method should fit the context. A person who needs to make a decision may need an accurate summary of options, assumptions, and unresolved risks. People who will use a result may be able to describe conditions a design review cannot reveal. People who will operate it may identify training, handoff, or maintenance constraints. Communication may need attention to accessibility, language, timing, privacy, security, and the difference between a proposal and a final decision. These are prompts for context-sensitive judgment, not a universal checklist.

Ethical engagement avoids manipulation. A team should not present uncertain claims as settled, bury a material concern in jargon, collect input it never intends to consider, or describe disagreement as a personal defect. It should distinguish what is known, what is proposed, what is outside the project boundary, and what remains open. Respect does not require promising that every request will be adopted. It does require treating people as sources of relevant experience and information rather than obstacles to overcome. A clear explanation of a boundary or uncertainty can be more honest than false reassurance.

Stakeholder information can change. A new dependency may bring in an operator, testing may reveal an accessibility condition, or a planned result may affect a group in a way no one recognized initially. Revisiting the analysis can make these changes visible. It cannot guarantee consensus, prevent conflict, or ensure project success. Nor does it replace the separate work of requirements, risk management, communication planning, or change control. Its value is narrower and foundational: a project has a better basis for learning when it can state who may be connected to the work, what is known about that connection, and how it will seek relevant information without pretending to control people.

## Key Vocabulary

- **stakeholder:** A person, group, or institution that can affect a project, is affected by it, or has a legitimate interest in its result.
- **stakeholder identification:** The ongoing work of finding and describing materially relevant connections between a project and people, groups, or institutions.
- **interest:** A relevant concern, desired outcome, responsibility, or information need connected to a project.
- **influence:** A way a person or group may affect a project or its environment through authority, knowledge, resources, cooperation, or another connection.
- **impact:** A change a project may cause in a stakeholder’s work, access, experience, responsibilities, or circumstances.
- **engagement:** A two-way process for exchanging relevant project information and learning from stakeholder input.
- **formal authority:** Decision-making power assigned by an organization, rule, agreement, or other governing arrangement.

## Eli-10

Imagine a school wants to change how students receive weather closure notices. The project team is not the only group that matters. Students and families need messages they can understand. Teachers may need to know when the message changes a schedule. The technology staff may need to keep the system working. Some people may use screen readers or speak another language at home. All of them can be stakeholders because the project affects them or they can provide information about what will work.

The team should not make a list just to decide who to convince. It should learn what different people need to know, what problems they might experience, and what is still uncertain. A principal may have authority to make a decision, but a student still has valuable knowledge about whether a message is understandable. Listening does not promise that every idea wins. It helps the team make its reasoning more honest.

## Eli's Analogy

A stakeholder map is like a map of a neighborhood before planning a parade. It shows streets, homes, bus stops, and people who may be affected. The map helps planners ask whom to inform and what routes or access needs to consider; it is not a scorecard for deciding whose voice matters most.

The analogy has limits. People are not streets on a map. Their interests can change, they can disagree, and a diagram cannot reveal everything about their experience. The analogy also does not decide who has legal or organizational authority or how a real dispute should be resolved.

## Worked Example

A city library hypothetically plans to replace paper-only event notices with a web page and optional text alerts. Initial stakeholder identification includes visitors who use the events, staff who update schedules, people who use assistive technology, volunteers who help at events, and the team that maintains the website. The group records different connections: visitors need timely, understandable information; staff need a workable way to correct an event change; the accessibility reviewer can test whether the page can be used with a keyboard and screen reader; and the website team knows technical constraints. It does not assume that the loudest request is the best solution or that any one group has authority to approve every choice. It communicates that the plan is a proposal, asks for feedback on access needs and update timing, and records unanswered questions for later requirements and testing work.

## Common Mistakes

- **Listing only the sponsor and project team as stakeholders.** Look for people and groups who use, operate, support, decide about, depend on, or are materially affected by the result.
- **Treating influence as proof that someone’s view is automatically correct.** Use influence as a reason to understand a connection, then examine evidence, authority, impact, and uncertainty separately.
- **Using engagement to pressure people into agreement.** Communicate honestly, invite relevant information, and distinguish listening from promising a particular outcome.
- **Treating a stakeholder record as permanent.** Revisit it when the project, its evidence, or its likely effects change.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Interest | Formal authority | Interest describes a relevant concern or information need; formal authority is assigned power to make or approve a decision. |
| Influence | Impact | Influence concerns how a stakeholder may affect the project; impact concerns how the project may affect that stakeholder. |
| Engagement | Persuasion | Engagement seeks two-way understanding and exchange of relevant information; persuasion seeks to change another person’s view or action. |

## Key Takeaway

Stakeholder work begins by identifying material connections between a project and the people or groups around it. Interests, influence, impact, and authority are distinct lenses; ethical engagement uses them to learn and communicate honestly, not to manipulate or guarantee agreement.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which description best fits a project stakeholder?**
   - A. A person, group, or institution that can affect a project, is affected by it, or has a legitimate interest in its result. **Correct.**
   - B. Only a paid member of the project team.
   - C. Only the person with final approval authority.
   - D. Any person who hears about the project.

2. **A museum is planning a new online ticket page. Which observation distinguishes impact from influence?**
   - A. The museum director can authorize the project.
   - B. A web developer knows the existing system’s limits.
   - C. Visitors using screen readers may be unable to complete a purchase if the page is inaccessible. **Correct.**
   - D. A sponsor wants updates at regular intervals.

3. **What is the most ethical purpose of a stakeholder record?**
   - A. To predict which people will resist and isolate them.
   - B. To rank people by personal value to the project team.
   - C. To replace conversations with an unchanging spreadsheet.
   - D. To make relevant connections, known concerns, uncertainties, and information needs available for review. **Correct.**

4. **A project team has evidence that families receive closure notices in several languages, but it has not learned whether the proposed alert system supports those languages. What should the team communicate?**
   - A. That the system will work for every family, to prevent concern.
   - B. That language support is an unresolved condition to investigate and that the proposal is not yet a confirmed capability. **Correct.**
   - C. That families without formal authority need not be consulted.
   - D. That the project must be canceled immediately.

5. **Why should stakeholder identification be revisited during a project?**
   - A. Because every stakeholder must eventually receive final authority.
   - B. Because a completed list guarantees agreement.
   - C. Because new evidence, dependencies, or effects can reveal connections and information needs that were not visible earlier. **Correct.**
   - D. Because the project team should change its definition of stakeholder whenever someone disagrees.
