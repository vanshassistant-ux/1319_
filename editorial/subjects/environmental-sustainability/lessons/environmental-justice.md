# Environmental Justice

## In 30 Seconds

Environmental justice is the fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people, regardless of race, color, national origin, or income, in environmental laws and decisions. The idea grew out of evidence that pollution and hazardous facilities land disproportionately on low-income communities and communities of color. It has two sides: a fair share of environmental burdens (distributive) and a real voice in the decisions that create them (procedural).

## Why This Matters

Where a landfill, highway, or power plant goes shapes the air people breathe and the water they drink for decades. Environmental justice gives students a precise vocabulary for a pattern that data keep confirming: environmental harms are not spread evenly. Courses in public health, planning, law, and policy use it to analyze siting decisions, permits, and cleanup priorities. Federal and state agencies now run screening tools and legal requirements built on these ideas, so understanding the definition, the history, and the distributive-versus-procedural distinction helps you read real regulatory debates rather than slogans.

## The College Version

### What environmental justice means

The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency defines environmental justice as the fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people, regardless of race, color, national origin, or income, with respect to the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and policies. Two phrases carry the weight. Fair treatment, in the EPA's words, means that no group of people should bear a disproportionate share of the negative environmental consequences of industrial, governmental, and commercial operations or policies. Meaningful involvement means that people who might be affected have a genuine opportunity to participate in decisions about activities that could affect their environment or health, that their input can actually influence the outcome, and that decision-makers seek out and facilitate that participation rather than treating a public hearing as a formality. Environmental justice is not a claim that everyone faces identical risks; it is a standard against which the distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, and the fairness of the process that produces that distribution, can be evaluated.

### Two dimensions: distributive and procedural

Scholars of justice separate two questions, and the EPA definition maps onto both. Distributive justice asks how outcomes are shared: who ends up living next to the refinery, the incinerator, or the truck route, and who breathes the cleaner air. When studies find that hazardous facilities cluster in particular neighborhoods, that is a distributive concern. Procedural justice asks how the decision was made: who was in the room, whose comments were translated and taken seriously, and whether the affected community learned about a permit in time to respond. A siting decision can be unfair on either axis independently. A process can be scrupulously open yet still pile burdens on one community; a burden can be shared reasonably yet imposed through a closed, opaque process. Environmental justice insists on both a fair result and a fair procedure, which is why the EPA's single definition pairs "fair treatment" with "meaningful involvement." A third dimension, sometimes called recognition, concerns whether a community's identity, history, and knowledge are acknowledged at all; it is less prominent in the formal U.S. definition but common in the scholarship.

### How the movement began

The U.S. movement has a widely cited catalyst. In 1982, North Carolina decided to bury soil contaminated with PCBs in a landfill in Afton, a mostly Black, low-income community in Warren County. Residents and civil-rights allies lay down in front of the trucks; weeks of protest produced more than 500 arrests. The dump proceeded, but the fight drew national attention to a pattern. In 1987, the United Church of Christ Commission for Racial Justice published *Toxic Wastes and Race in the United States*, which reported that race was the single strongest predictor of where commercial hazardous-waste facilities were located, stronger than income or home values. Benjamin Chavis, who led the Commission, is credited with coining the term environmental racism. Sociologist Robert Bullard, often called a father of the field, documented the same patterns in the South, including in his 1990 book *Dumping in Dixie*. In October 1991, the First National People of Color Environmental Leadership Summit met in Washington, D.C., drew delegates from every state and beyond, and adopted 17 Principles of Environmental Justice that broadened "environment" to mean the places where people live, work, worship, and play.

### From movement to federal policy

In 1994, President Bill Clinton signed Executive Order 12898, "Federal Actions to Address Environmental Justice in Minority Populations and Low-Income Populations," published in the Federal Register at 59 FR 7629. It directs each federal agency to make achieving environmental justice part of its mission by identifying and addressing the disproportionately high and adverse human health or environmental effects of its programs, policies, and activities on minority and low-income populations, and to develop a strategy for doing so. It also created an interagency working group chaired by the EPA Administrator. An executive order is a directive to the executive branch, not a statute, so its force depends on the priorities of each administration, and environmental justice remains an actively contested policy area. Agencies have also built analytical tools. EPA's EJScreen combined local pollution-burden indicators with demographic data so that analysts could flag communities carrying heavy environmental loads; EPA removed EJScreen from its website in early 2025, and outside groups have since hosted mirror copies. Screening tools do not by themselves prove discrimination or dictate a decision; they surface where disparities exist so that agencies, courts, and communities can ask why.

### What the disparities do and do not show

The documented pattern is robust: across many studies, polluting facilities and their health burdens fall more heavily on low-income communities and communities of color. What causes that pattern is genuinely debated. Some analyses emphasize discriminatory siting decisions, the sense captured by "environmental racism." Others emphasize housing markets and land prices, where cheaper land near industry attracts both facilities and residents with fewer options, sometimes over decades of settlement. These explanations are not mutually exclusive, and untangling them matters for remedy: a siting rule addresses one, housing and cleanup policy another. Environmental justice as a framework does not require choosing a single cause. It requires taking the distribution seriously as a question of fairness, ensuring affected people have a real say, and treating the resulting disparities as something to be explained and addressed rather than assumed away. This is why the field sits at the intersection of settled empirical findings about disparities and unsettled debates about the right policy response.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Environmental justice** — The fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people, regardless of race, color, national origin, or income, in the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and policies (EPA).
- **Fair treatment** — The principle that no group should bear a disproportionate share of the negative environmental consequences of industrial, governmental, or commercial operations and policies.
- **Meaningful involvement** — The principle that affected people can genuinely participate in and influence environmental decisions, and that decision-makers actively seek out that participation.
- **Distributive justice** — Fairness in how outcomes, here environmental benefits and burdens, are shared among groups.
- **Procedural justice** — Fairness in the process by which decisions are made, including who is informed, consulted, and able to influence the result.
- **Environmental racism** — Racial discrimination in siting polluting facilities in or near minority communities together with those communities' exclusion from environmental decision-making; a term credited to Benjamin Chavis.
- **Executive Order 12898** — A 1994 order directing each federal agency to identify and address disproportionately high and adverse health or environmental effects of its actions on minority and low-income populations.
- **EJScreen** — An EPA screening and mapping tool that combined environmental and demographic indicators to flag communities with high environmental burdens; removed from EPA's website in early 2025.
- **Facility siting** — The selection of a location for a facility such as a landfill, incinerator, or plant, a decision central to distributive environmental-justice concerns.

## Eli-10

Some neighborhoods end up with the smelly dump, the loud highway, and the smoky factory, while others get parks and clean air. That is usually not random, and studies show it lands hardest on communities that are poorer or mostly people of color. Environmental justice says two things have to be fair: what you get stuck with, and whether anyone asked you first. If a town is going to build something that dirties the air, the people who will breathe that air deserve a real seat at the table, not just a notice after the decision is made.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a group project where one classmate always gets assigned the messiest, worst job, and the teacher never even asks that student what they think. Environmental justice is the rule that says the hard jobs should be shared fairly and that everyone gets a real voice in who does what.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** The analogy shrinks a serious, long-term problem to a single assignment. Real environmental burdens last for decades, affect health and property, and grew out of history like segregation and land prices, not one teacher's choice. And unlike a classroom, there is often no single decision-maker who can simply reassign the burden.

## Worked Example

A county proposes a new waste-transfer station and picks a site in a low-income neighborhood where two-thirds of residents are people of color. Analyzed through environmental justice, ask two questions. Distributive: does this community already host other burdens, a rail yard, a bus depot, a highway, so that the new station adds to a stack of exposures rather than sharing them countywide? An EJScreen-style tool would layer local pollution indicators over demographics to show whether this area is already heavily loaded. Procedural: was the hearing scheduled when working residents could attend, were materials available in the languages people speak, and could public comment actually change the site? If burdens are concentrated and the process shut people out, the proposal raises environmental justice concerns on both axes, even if every individual permit is technically legal.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating environmental justice as only about pollution amounts, ignoring who decides.** The definition has two halves. Meaningful involvement, the procedural side, matters as much as the distribution of harm. A fair outcome reached by shutting people out still fails the standard.
- **Using "environmental justice" and "environmental racism" as synonyms.** Environmental justice is the broad goal of fairness by race and income. Environmental racism names a specific claim, that minority communities are targeted and excluded. One is the standard; the other is a documented problem the standard responds to.
- **Thinking Executive Order 12898 is a law that guarantees outcomes.** It is an executive order directing federal agencies, not a statute. Its practical force rises and falls with administrations, which is part of why the area stays actively contested.
- **Assuming that a documented disparity by itself proves intentional discrimination.** The disparities are well established, but their causes, discriminatory siting versus land markets and settlement history, are debated. Environmental justice takes the disparity seriously without requiring a single agreed cause.
- **Believing a screening tool like EJScreen decides where facilities can go.** Screening tools flag where burdens and vulnerable populations overlap; they inform analysis and public debate. They do not make permitting decisions or prove a legal violation on their own.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Distributive justice vs. Procedural justice** — Distributive justice is about who ends up bearing the burden; procedural justice is about whether affected people had a real voice in the decision. Environmental justice requires both.
- **Environmental justice vs. Environmental racism** — Environmental justice is the fairness standard for all people regardless of race or income; environmental racism is a specific claim that minority communities are disproportionately targeted and excluded.
- **Executive Order 12898 vs. An act of Congress** — The executive order directs agencies within the executive branch and can shift with administrations; a statute is durable law that agencies and courts must apply until Congress changes it.

## Key Takeaway

Environmental justice means a fair share of environmental burdens (distributive) and a real voice in the decisions that create them (procedural), for everyone regardless of race or income. It grew from documented disparities, catalyzed at Warren County and formalized in Executive Order 12898, and remains an active, contested policy field.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **According to the EPA, environmental justice combines two requirements. Which pair correctly names them?**
   - A. Economic growth and environmental protection
   - B. Fair treatment and meaningful involvement
   - C. Emission reduction and habitat conservation
   - D. Federal funding and state enforcement
   - **Answer: B.** EPA defines environmental justice as the fair treatment AND meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income. The other pairs name real environmental goals but are not the EPA's two-part definition.

2. **A city holds a permit hearing for a new incinerator at 10 a.m. on a workday, provides documents only in English in a largely Spanish-speaking neighborhood, and has already finalized the site. Which dimension of environmental justice is most directly violated?**
   - A. Distributive justice, because burdens are shared evenly
   - B. Recognition justice, because the pollutant is identified
   - C. Restorative justice, because cleanup funds are provided
   - D. Procedural justice, because affected residents cannot meaningfully participate
   - **Answer: D.** The scenario is about the fairness of the process: an inaccessible time, untranslated materials, and a predetermined outcome deny meaningful involvement, the procedural dimension. It does not describe the distribution of burden, and the other options misname the issue.

3. **Which 1982 event is most widely cited as a catalyst of the U.S. environmental justice movement?**
   - A. Protests against a PCB landfill in Warren County, North Carolina
   - B. The signing of Executive Order 12898
   - C. The publication of the report Toxic Wastes and Race
   - D. The first People of Color Environmental Leadership Summit
   - **Answer: A.** The 1982 Warren County protests against a PCB-contaminated-soil landfill in a majority-Black community are the widely cited catalyst. The UCC report (1987), the Summit (1991), and Executive Order 12898 (1994) all came later and are distractors dated after 1982.

4. **How does "environmental racism" differ from "environmental justice" as the terms are used in the field?**
   - A. They are interchangeable labels for the same federal program
   - B. Environmental racism applies only to air pollution; environmental justice only to water
   - C. Environmental racism names a specific claim of targeting and exclusion, while environmental justice is the broad fairness standard
   - D. Environmental justice is a legal violation, while environmental racism is only an academic theory
   - **Answer: C.** Environmental justice is the general standard of fairness regardless of race or income; environmental racism, a term credited to Benjamin Chavis, is the specific claim that minority communities are disproportionately targeted for polluting facilities and excluded from decisions. The other options invent false scopes or legal statuses.

5. **A researcher finds that hazardous facilities are concentrated in a set of low-income, majority-minority neighborhoods. Which conclusion is best supported by the environmental justice framework as described in the lesson?**
   - A. The concentration proves that officials intentionally discriminated in every siting decision
   - B. The disparity is real and warrants scrutiny of both outcomes and process, though its causes may be debated
   - C. Because land near industry is cheaper, the disparity raises no fairness concern
   - D. The pattern is meaningful only if a court has already ruled it illegal
   - **Answer: B.** The lesson treats the disparities as well documented while noting that their causes (discriminatory siting versus land markets and settlement history) are debated; the framework calls for scrutinizing both the distribution and the decision process. Options A and D overstate what a disparity proves, and C dismisses a fairness concern the framework takes seriously.
