# History and Role of Policing

## In 30 Seconds

Modern policing is younger than most people assume. London got a full-time uniformed force in 1829; northern American cities built municipal departments between the 1830s and the 1850s, while southern colonies had already created armed slave patrols a century earlier. What police do has been redefined repeatedly since, and research from the 1970s onward showed that random patrol, heavy investigative effort, and fast response each did less than the profession assumed.

## Why This Matters

Nearly every argument about American policing rests on a historical claim: that police were created to fight crime, to manage disorder, or to control enslaved people. Some of those claims are documented, some are contested, and some are folklore. Knowing which is which lets you read the argument instead of choosing a side by instinct. The same holds for the job itself. Staffing, training, and reform proposals all assume a picture of how officers spend their shifts, and measured pictures have looked different from assumed ones since the 1970s. This lesson gives you the dates, the studies, and the places where careful historians genuinely disagree.

## The College Version

### Before there was a police force

English policing grew out of shared obligation rather than paid employment. Households were grouped into tithings of ten families, tithings into hundreds, and hundreds into shires supervised by a shire-reeve, ancestor of the American sheriff. The constable, appointed locally and responsible for the hundred's weapons, is the earliest figure the 1967 President's Commission on Law Enforcement identifies as a police officer. Under Edward I (1272-1307) larger towns acquired a 'watch and ward' that guarded gates and arrested night-time offenders, and in 1326 Edward II created the office of justice of the peace; the constable became the justice's subordinate, a hierarchy that held for roughly five centuries.

By the eighteenth century the arrangement had decayed. Service everyone owed became service nobody performed: constables paid substitutes to take their turns, and the Commission describes those substitutes as badly paid and often too old to be effective. Night watchmen nominally guarded cities against thieves but reportedly did little beyond calling out the hour. Industrialising London improvised: government created public offices, later called police offices, each with three paid justices authorised to employ six paid constables, and civic bodies formed patrols such as the Bow Street Horse and Foot Patrol. By 1800 nine such offices existed in metropolitan London, and the Commission records that they largely refused to communicate with one another for fear of losing credit for an arrest. That is what 1829 was meant to solve: not the absence of law enforcement, but the absence of a coordinated, full-time, publicly accountable one.

### 1829, and the principles Peel probably did not write

Robert Peel became Home Secretary in 1822 arguing that better policing could not eliminate crime but that bad policing was feeding disorder. Seven years later he steered through Parliament an Act for improving the police in and near the metropolis, recorded by legislation.gov.uk as the Metropolitan Police Act 1829, 1829 c. 44 in the tenth year of George IV. The force numbered about a thousand uniformed men under two magistrates, later titled commissioners, who held administrative but deliberately not judicial power, answering to the Home Secretary and through him to Parliament. An 1839 statute let county justices establish forces; an 1856 statute required every borough and county to have one.

The famous nine 'Peelian principles' are a separate matter, and their provenance is genuinely disputed. The Home Office's own published note on policing by consent states that there is no evidence of any link to Robert Peel, that the principles were likely devised by the first Commissioners of Police of the Metropolis, Charles Rowan and Richard Mayne, and that they were set out in the General Instructions issued to every new officer from 1829. Keep the distinction clean: the ideas the list expresses - that the police are the public, that cooperation falls as force is used, that the test of policing is the absence of crime rather than visible action - are real and old. The claim that Peel wrote them is the part that does not survive checking.

### Two American lineages, and the argument between them

Colonists imported constables and sheriffs largely unchanged. Boston had night watchmen by 1636; New York's carried rattles and were known as the Rattlewatch. As cities grew, the watch failed the way the English one had. Boston created a day force in 1838 alongside its watch. The New York legislature passed a law in 1844 authorising the first unified day-and-night police and abolishing the watch; the standard list dates the resulting department to 1845. Boston consolidated in 1854. Hubert Williams and Patrick Murphy, writing in the same NIJ series, reproduce the conventional sequence of 'new police' dates, which begins Boston 1838, New York City 1845 and Chicago 1851 and runs on to Detroit in 1865.

Their central point is that this list is not the whole country. Southern colonial legislatures had enacted slave patrol statutes decades earlier - South Carolina in the 1740s, Georgia in 1757 - creating compulsory patrols empowered to search the dwellings of enslaved people for weapons, punish anyone found off a plantation without a pass, and enter houses suspected of harbouring or trading with them. Charleston, Savannah and Richmond ran combined foot and mounted patrols, and in Charleston policing was the largest single item in the municipal budget. Williams and Murphy report, attributing the argument to the historians Samuel Walker and Philip Reichel, that a strong case exists that the first American modern-style policing occurred in the slave patrols.

Hold that claim's shape precisely, because it is easy to overstate in either direction. It is documented that specific southern jurisdictions built permanent, publicly authorised, continuously patrolling forces for racial control before northern cities built municipal departments, and that there the patrol is a real institutional ancestor. It is an interpretive argument, not a settled finding, that this makes slave patrols the origin of American policing as a whole; Boston's and New York's departments have their own traceable descent from the watch. Williams and Murphy also note that northern departments were created amid anti-abolitionist and anti-Catholic rioting in the 1830s and 1840s, so even the northern story is more about controlling disorder than detecting crime. Historians differ over how much weight each strand carries, and a lesson that resolves that disagreement for you is doing you a disservice.

### Three eras, and the criticism of the periodization

The standard map of American police history is the one George Kelling and Mark Moore published for NIJ in November 1988. Their political era, from the introduction of police into municipalities in the 1840s to the early 1900s, drew authority from ward politicians, defined the function broadly enough to include soup lines and lodging for newly arrived immigrants, ran through semi-autonomous precincts on foot patrol, and measured success as political and citizen satisfaction. Its weaknesses were corruption, curbstone justice against outsiders, and almost no supervisory control.

The reform era took hold in the 1930s, peaked in the 1950s and 1960s, and eroded in the late 1970s. August Vollmer, chief in Berkeley, rallied executives around reform in the 1920s and 1930s, and his protege O.W. Wilson became its principal administrative architect. Reformers replaced political authorization with law and professionalism, used civil service to break patronage hiring, narrowed the function to crime control, and organised departments on Taylorist lines. Wilson supplied the theory that marked cars patrolling randomly would create a sense of police omnipresence, and departments pushed centralized dispatch and 911, marketing rapid response as effective crime control. The 1967 President's Commission ratified the shift by recasting police as part of a criminal justice system rather than an agency of city government. The third era, community problem solving, widens the function again toward order maintenance and problem solving.

Kelling and Moore themselves warn that the boundaries are not clean. The sharper criticism came from Williams and Murphy in 1990, in the same series, who called the analysis 'disturbingly incomplete': it takes little account of slavery, segregation and discrimination, treats departments as autonomous strategists rather than as barometers of the society around them, and generalises from the urban Northeast. Use the three eras as a filing system for changes in authorization, function, organisation and tactics, not as a chronology every department passed through on schedule.

### The studies that dismantled the reform-era assumptions

Three studies did more than any argument to unsettle the professional model, and each is routinely misquoted, so specifics matter. The Kansas City Preventive Patrol Experiment ran from 1 October 1972 to 30 September 1973 in fifteen South Patrol Division beats, matched on crime, calls for service, ethnic composition, median income and transiency, then split into five reactive beats with no preventive patrol, five control beats at the usual one car per beat, and five proactive beats at two to three times that level. Across victimization surveys and reported crime the three conditions showed no significant differences in crime, citizen attitudes, fear, response time, or satisfaction with response time. The report's cautions matter as much: it did not show visible presence can never matter in selected circumstances, it warned against reading it as a case for fewer officers, and it noted officers' noncommitted time ran to sixty percent.

Peter Greenwood and Joan Petersilia's RAND study, report R-1776-DOJ (1975), concluded that traditional investigative approaches do not significantly affect how often cases are solved, that most are solved by routine administrative procedures, that roughly half of investigative effort could be eliminated or redirected without unduly reducing effectiveness, and that apprehension rates are likelier to rise from alert patrol and citizen cooperation than from better detective work. RAND proposed restructuring investigation, including a major offenders unit, not abolishing it.

Rapid response was the third assumption to go. William Spelman and Dale Brown's 1981 study for the Police Executive Research Forum examined more than four thousand victims and witnesses in four cities and found that citizen reporting time, not police response time, most affects the chance of an on-scene arrest: shaving minutes off dispatch pays when a crime is reported as it happens and little otherwise, since most calls arrive after the offender has gone. None of these studies says police do not matter. Each says one tactic delivered less than its reputation promised.

### What the job is actually made of

The most careful early measurement is Eric Scott's 1981 NIJ study, in which observers coded 26,418 citizen calls in departments across Rochester, St. Louis and Tampa-St. Petersburg into twelve categories, recording each as the caller described it. Requests for information were the largest category at twenty-one percent, nonviolent crime second at seventeen, then assistance at twelve, public nuisances at eleven, traffic at nine, interpersonal conflict at seven, and violent crime at two. Fewer than a fifth of calls concerned criminal incidents at all. Two cautions travel with those numbers: departments classify the same event differently, so category boundaries move the percentages, and the familiar claim that about eighty percent of patrol workload is noncriminal inherits that ambiguity even though studies converge on it.

Contemporary data show the same shape without settling on one number. Cynthia Lum, Christopher Koper and Xiaoyun Wu analysed millions of 911 calls across nine U.S. agencies in a 2022 Police Quarterly article and reported that the range and volume of what people call police about is very large and mostly not obviously transferable to other organisations without substantial added resources. The Bureau of Justice Statistics found from its 2022 Police-Public Contact Survey, published in October 2024, that nineteen percent of U.S. residents aged sixteen or older, about 49.2 million people, had police contact in the previous twelve months: 7.6 percent police-initiated, 11.2 percent resident-initiated, 2.5 percent from a traffic accident. Fifty-eight percent of those whose most recent contact was police-initiated were drivers in traffic stops, and forty-six percent of resident-initiated contacts were to report a possible crime. The modal encounter between an American and an officer is a traffic stop; the modal reason an American contacts police is to report something. That is a regulatory and service relationship far more often than an adversarial one - the conclusion the 1967 Commission had already reached when it wrote that officers perform a wide range of complex functions beyond serious crime.

### Legitimacy, procedural justice, and stating the evidence honestly

The dominant contemporary framework for the police role is procedural justice, developed principally by the social psychologist Tom Tyler. Its claim is that people cooperate with legal authority mainly because they regard it as legitimate, and judge legitimacy from how they are treated rather than from whether they liked the outcome. The National Academies committee that reviewed proactive policing in 2018 sets out four elements: voice, the chance to state your side before a decision; neutrality, consistent and explained rule-based decisions; respect, dignified treatment; and trustworthiness, a belief that the officer's motives are benevolent.

Report that evidence at the strength it has. The committee found perceptions of procedurally just treatment strongly associated with judgements of legitimacy and willingness to cooperate, but concluded the research base was insufficient to establish that procedurally just policing causally produces either (Conclusion 6-4). On crime it found the evidence base very small: too small to conclude that procedural justice policing affects crime or disorder, and too small to conclude it does not (Conclusion 4-11). Insufficient evidence is not evidence of absence, the committee stressed, and procedural justice has stronger causal support in courts and workplaces. So: a well-supported association, a promising theory, an open causal question in policing.

Two habits carry this topic: separate event, evidence and interpretation - 1829 is an event, the Home Office note is evidence about authorship, 'policing by consent' is an interpretation - and date every finding while naming its jurisdiction. This lesson is educational material on institutional history, not legal advice, and takes no position on current policy. Police discretion, use of force, community policing and search and seizure law have their own lessons in this unit.

## Key Vocabulary

- **night watch** — A pre-modern arrangement in which townsmen, or paid substitutes, guarded streets and gates between sunset and daybreak and raised the alarm, with no full-time paid organisation behind them.
- **parish constable** — A locally appointed officeholder in English law enforcement, originally unpaid and serving by rotation, who supervised watchmen, executed warrants and answered to the justice of the peace.
- **Metropolitan Police Act 1829** — The statute (1829 c. 44) that established London's first full-time uniformed force of about a thousand men under two commissioners accountable to the Home Secretary.
- **Peelian principles** — A nine-item statement of policing-by-consent philosophy popularly credited to Robert Peel; the Home Office says the link to him is unevidenced and points to the first commissioners instead.
- **slave patrol** — An armed, legally authorised patrol created by southern colonial and state statutes to police enslaved people, with power to search dwellings, stop those travelling without a pass, and inflict punishment.
- **political era** — Kelling and Moore's label for roughly 1840s to early 1900s American policing, in which ward politicians supplied authority, the function was broad, and precincts operated with little central control.
- **reform era** — Kelling and Moore's label for the professional model that took hold in the 1930s, grounding authority in law and expertise, narrowing the mission to crime control, and relying on car patrol and rapid response.
- **preventive patrol** — Driving or walking an assigned area at random when not answering calls, on the theory that visible presence deters offenders; the Kansas City experiment found little crime or fear effect from the marked-car version.
- **calls for service** — Requests the public makes of a police agency, usually by telephone, which together form the main measured picture of what an agency is asked to handle.
- **procedural justice** — The idea that people judge an authority's legitimacy by the fairness of how they are treated, assessed through voice, neutrality, respect and perceived trustworthiness of motive.

## Eli-10

For most of history, keeping order was a chore your town made you take turns doing, like shovelling snow off the sidewalk. England only invented a paid, full-time, uniformed police force in 1829. Northern American cities copied it from the 1830s onward, but southern colonies had already built armed patrols to hunt down escaping enslaved people a century earlier, so the country really has two family trees, not one. Then people started measuring what police actually do all day. It turned out that driving around randomly did not lower crime much, that most cases were solved by routine paperwork and by information victims and witnesses had already supplied rather than by detective work, and that arriving fast usually did not help because the call came in too late anyway. It also turned out that most of the job is not crime at all. It is answering questions, sorting out arguments, traffic, and taking reports.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a hospital emergency department. Its dramatic reputation is built on trauma cases, but most of what walks through the door is sprains, fevers, paperwork and people who are not sure where else to go. Policing is like that: the rare, serious event defines the public image while the ordinary volume defines the actual work.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The analogy fails in the part that matters most. An emergency department cannot arrest you, search you, or use force against you, and nobody founded one to enforce a racial hierarchy. Police carry coercive legal authority in every one of those routine calls, and in some American jurisdictions the institution's ancestor was a slave patrol. Reputation versus workload is a fair comparison; power and origin are not.

## Worked Example

A classmate writes that 'the Kansas City experiment proved police patrol does not work, so cities should cut patrol budgets.' Test it against the report. First check what varied: three matched sets of five beats over twelve months in 1972-73, with routine preventive patrol removed, held constant, or raised to two or three times its usual level. Then check what was measured: reported crime, victimization, fear, attitudes, response time. Then check the finding: no significant differences among the three conditions. Now check the scope. The study tested one tactic, random patrol by marked car, in one city, in one year. It did not test whether police exist, whether officers answer calls, or whether targeted patrol at high-crime places works, and the authors explicitly warned that visible presence may matter in selected circumstances and that the results are not a case for fewer officers. The defensible version of your classmate's sentence is narrower: random preventive patrol showed no measurable payoff, and Kansas City found officers' noncommitted time was sixty percent, so the question worth asking is what that time should be spent on instead.

## Common Mistakes

- **Attributing the nine 'Peelian principles' to Robert Peel as documented fact.**
  - The Home Office's own note says there is no evidence of a link to Peel and credits the first commissioners, Rowan and Mayne, with instructions issued from 1829. Say 'the principles associated with Peel' and note the dispute.
- **Flattening the origins question in either direction: claiming all American policing descends from slave patrols, or leaving slave patrols out of the story entirely.**
  - State the documented part - southern statutes from the 1740s onward created patrols that are direct institutional ancestors in those jurisdictions - and mark the rest as an interpretive dispute among historians, alongside the traceable northern descent from the watch.
- **Reading the Kansas City experiment as proof that police presence never affects crime.**
  - It tested random preventive patrol by marked car in one city over one year and found no significant effect on crime or fear. The report itself says this does not show that visible presence can have no impact in selected circumstances.
- **Treating Kelling and Moore's three eras as a national timetable every department followed.**
  - The authors call the boundaries unclear, and Williams and Murphy argue the scheme is drawn from the urban Northeast and omits how slavery and segregation shaped policing. Use it as a framework for comparing strategies, not as chronology.
- **Reporting procedural justice research as though the causal effect on legitimacy or crime is established.**
  - The 2018 National Academies review found strong associations but insufficient evidence to conclude that procedurally just policing causally raises legitimacy or cooperation, and a very small evidence base on crime effects in either direction.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Metropolitan Police Act 1829 | The nine 'Peelian principles' | The Act is a verifiable statute with a citation (1829 c. 44) that created an institution. The principles are a philosophy statement of disputed authorship, issued as general instructions rather than enacted, and the Home Office denies an evidenced link to Peel. |
| Northern municipal lineage (watch to day force to unified department) | Southern slave patrol lineage | Both are documented. The northern line runs from Boston's watch of 1636 to its day force in 1838 and New York's unification statute of 1844; the southern line runs from statutes such as South Carolina's in the 1740s and Georgia's in 1757. What historians dispute is not their existence but their relative weight in explaining American policing overall. |
| Calls for service data | Police-public contact survey data | Calls data count what the public asks the agency to handle and are recorded by the agency; contact surveys ask a population sample about encounters, including police-initiated ones such as traffic stops that generate no call. They answer different questions and neither alone describes the workload. |
| Association between procedural justice and legitimacy | Causal effect of procedural justice policing | The 2018 National Academies review found the association strong and consistent, and the causal evidence in the policing context insufficient to support a conclusion either way. Reporting the first as if it were the second overstates the research. |

## Key Takeaway

Modern policing is a set of institutional choices with datable origins and at least two American lineages, and the measured job has always been much broader than crime fighting - so state what is documented, name what historians dispute, and date every finding you cite.

## Practice Question Bank

**Q1. According to the UK Home Office's published note on policing by consent, who most likely devised the nine principles commonly called the 'Peelian principles'?**

A. Robert Peel, who drafted them personally before introducing the 1829 Act
B. Charles Rowan and Richard Mayne, the first Commissioners of Police of the Metropolis
C. The Royal Commission on the Police, in its 1962 final report
D. Parliament, which enacted them as a schedule to the Metropolitan Police Act 1829

*Answer: B. The Home Office note states there is no evidence of any link to Robert Peel and that the principles were likely devised by the first commissioners, Rowan and Mayne, and set out in the General Instructions issued to every new officer from 1829. That rules out option A. The 1962 Royal Commission is a much later body, and the principles were instructions to officers rather than statutory text, so they were never a schedule to the Act.*

**Q2. The Kansas City Preventive Patrol Experiment (1972-73) compared reactive, control, and proactive beats. Which statement best describes what the summary report concluded?**

A. Removing routine patrol from a beat produced large increases in reported burglary and robbery
B. Tripling patrol in the proactive beats cut victimization roughly in half
C. Routine preventive patrol by marked car showed little value for preventing crime or reducing fear, though the report warned it had not shown that visible presence can never matter
D. The experiment demonstrated that cities could safely employ substantially fewer officers

*Answer: C. The three conditions produced no significant differences in crime, citizen attitudes, fear, response time, or satisfaction, which rules out the first two options. The report states plainly that it did not show visible police presence can have no impact in selected circumstances, and it warns explicitly that the results should not be read as implying fewer officers are needed, which rules out the last option.*
*Difficulty: understanding · Skill: Stating an experimental finding together with the limits its authors placed on it · Sources: kelling-1974-kansas-city-preventive-patrol-experiment*

**Q3. A student writes: 'American policing began with the slave patrols.' Which revision is best supported by Williams and Murphy's 1990 NIJ paper?**

A. American policing began with Boston's 1838 day force and New York's unified department; slave patrols were a separate militia matter with no policing powers
B. Slave patrols are the single origin of every police department in the United States
C. Southern colonies enacted slave patrol statutes decades before northern cities created municipal forces, and some historians argue those patrols were the earliest American modern-style policing; the conventional northern account omits them
D. Slave patrols existed but were purely private arrangements among plantation owners, so they fall outside the history of public policing

*Answer: C. Williams and Murphy present the slave-patrol origin as a strong argument attributed to Walker and Reichel, not as a settled finding, and their target is the omission of the South from the standard account. The first option denies a documented lineage; the second overstates an interpretive argument as fact; the fourth is wrong because the patrols were created by colonial and state statute with legal powers of search and punishment.*
*Difficulty: analysis · Skill: Distinguishing documented event, interpretive argument, and overgeneralization in contested historiography · Sources: williams-murphy-1990-evolving-strategy-minority-view, presidents-commission-1967-task-force-report-police*

**Q4. A chief cites the 1975 RAND criminal investigation study to justify abolishing the detective bureau entirely. What is the most accurate response?**

A. The study supports it, because RAND found that detectives essentially never solve cases
B. RAND found most cases are solved by routine administrative procedures and that about half of investigative effort could be cut or redirected, but it recommended restructuring investigation, including a major offenders unit, rather than eliminating it
C. The study examined patrol deployment rather than investigations, so it is irrelevant to the detective bureau
D. RAND concluded that apprehension rates rise mainly through more intensive follow-up investigation on every case

*Answer: B. RAND's summary report concluded that traditional investigative approaches do not significantly affect clearance rates, that most cases are solved through routine administrative procedures, and that roughly half of investigative effort could be eliminated or shifted, while proposing reforms such as reduced follow-up on minor cases, a major offenders unit, and prosecutor-supervised post-arrest investigation. It did not find detectives never solve cases, it did study investigations, and it pointed to alert patrol and citizen cooperation rather than more follow-up as the route to higher apprehension.*
*Difficulty: application · Skill: Applying a research finding to a policy claim without exceeding what the study concluded · Sources: greenwood-petersilia-1975-rand-criminal-investigation-process*

**Q5. Scott's 1981 NIJ study coded 26,418 citizen calls to police. Which conclusion states the result with its proper caveat?**

A. Exactly 80 percent of police work in every U.S. department is noncriminal, a figure that holds across agencies
B. Violent-crime calls were the largest single category at 21 percent of the total
C. Requests for information were the largest category at 21 percent and violent-crime calls the smallest at 2 percent, but departments classify the same incident differently, so the percentages should be read as an order of magnitude
D. Because fewer than 20 percent of calls concerned crime, police response time is irrelevant to every offense

*Answer: C. Scott's Table 3-1 puts information requests at 21 percent and violent crime at 2 percent, and the report warns repeatedly that call classification varies by department, which is why it also treats the familiar 80 percent figure as carrying that same ambiguity rather than as an exact constant. Option B inverts the data. Option D confuses this study with the response-time research, which found that fast response still matters for crimes reported while in progress.*
*Difficulty: application · Skill: Reading workload data accurately and carrying the study's stated measurement caveats with the number · Sources: scott-1981-calls-for-service-citizen-demand, spelman-brown-1981-calling-the-police*
