# Blood Pressure

## In 30 Seconds

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries. Every heartbeat sends a fresh surge of blood through them, so the pressure climbs and eases in a rhythm. A reading has two numbers: systolic (top) is the pressure when the heart pumps, and diastolic (bottom) is the pressure between beats. For adults, the normal reference is less than 120 over less than 80. It is measured with a cuff on the upper arm, and high blood pressure usually has no symptoms — so the numbers matter even when you feel fine.

## Why This Matters

Blood pressure is one of the vital signs measured at nearly every healthcare visit, and it is the one that most often surprises people: high blood pressure usually has no warning signs, so it can run quietly for years while doing real work on the heart and arteries. Understanding what the two numbers mean lets a student read a chart instead of just copying it, and lets anyone talk clearly with a provider about what a reading means. The category lines have also shifted between guidelines over the years, so knowing that categories come from guidelines — not verdicts — keeps the numbers in perspective. This lesson builds the foundation that the vital-signs topic and clinical skills courses assume you already have.

## Learning Objectives

- Define blood pressure as the force of blood against the artery walls, following the CDC and MedlinePlus definitions.
- Distinguish systolic pressure (the top number) from diastolic pressure (the bottom number) and state what each one represents.
- Identify the adult normal reference — less than 120/80 mmHg — and explain why blood pressure categories come from guidelines rather than verdicts.
- Describe how blood pressure is measured with a cuff around the upper arm and a stethoscope or machine.
- Name the factors the CDC links to higher blood pressure: salt, inactivity, weight, genetics, and stress.
- Explain why high blood pressure is usually silent and why measuring is the only way to know.

## The College Version

### The pressure story

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of your arteries. The CDC defines it in exactly this way: blood pressure is the pressure of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries, the vessels that carry blood from the heart to the rest of the body. MedlinePlus states the same idea — blood pressure is the force of your blood pushing against the walls of your arteries. Each heartbeat sends a fresh surge of blood into the arteries, so the pressure never sits still: it climbs with every beat and eases between beats. That rise-and-fall rhythm is what a blood pressure reading captures, and it is why the reading comes in two numbers instead of one.

### The two numbers

The top number is the systolic pressure: the pressure in the arteries when the heart beats and pumps blood. MedlinePlus describes it as the moment blood pressure is highest. The bottom number is the diastolic pressure: the pressure when the heart is at rest, between beats, when blood pressure falls. The CDC refers to them as the first number (systolic) and the second number (diastolic), and the reading is written systolic over diastolic — 120/80, for example, means a systolic pressure of 120 and a diastolic pressure of 80. When you hear "120 over 80," the first number is the pump, and the second is the pause.

### The normal category — and the honest note

For adults, the reference for a normal blood pressure is less than 120 over less than 80 mmHg. The CDC states that a normal blood pressure level is less than 120/80 mmHg, and the American Heart Association's category table lists normal as less than 120 systolic and less than 80 diastolic. One honest note belongs right here: these categories come from guidelines, not verdicts. The CDC points out that the threshold used to diagnose high blood pressure has changed over time — some clinicians use 140/90, from a 2003 guideline, while others use 130/80, from a 2017 guideline. A single reading above a line is not a diagnosis either. Blood pressure moves up and down through the day with activity and rest, so providers take two or more readings at separate appointments before making a call.

### How it's measured

The measurement is an everyday skill that takes about a minute. A provider wraps an inflatable cuff around the upper arm, pumps it tight enough to squeeze the artery, then releases it slowly while listening with a stethoscope — or lets a machine listen with an electronic sensor. MedlinePlus describes the equipment plainly: a gauge, a stethoscope or electronic sensor, and a blood pressure cuff. The cuff squeezes the artery; the gauge records the pressure at which blood flow returns, the systolic number, and the pressure at which it flows freely again, the diastolic number. Because a single reading can be a fluke — nerves, a rushed morning, a full bladder — providers take two or more readings at separate appointments before diagnosing high blood pressure.

### What raises it

Several factors are known to raise blood pressure, and the CDC names them plainly. Salt: eating too much sodium, an element in table salt, increases blood pressure. Inactivity: not getting enough regular physical activity is the kind of lifestyle choice the CDC ties to high blood pressure. Weight: carrying excess weight means the heart must work harder to pump blood and oxygen around the body. Genetics: high blood pressure can run in a family, and genes likely play a role. Stress: the CDC and the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute both list managing stress among the steps that help control blood pressure, and a tense moment can push a reading up even when nothing else changes. None of this is a verdict on any individual person — risk factors raise the odds; they do not decide the outcome.

### The quiet number

High blood pressure is usually silent. The CDC states that high blood pressure usually has no warning signs or symptoms, and many people do not know they have it — measuring is the only way to know. MedlinePlus says the same: high blood pressure usually has no symptoms. That is the reality check of this lesson. Blood pressure does not announce itself the way a fever or a pain does. It changes through the day with what you are doing, it can climb slowly over years, and because it feels like nothing, it gets checked only when someone thinks to check it. That is why blood pressure is part of the vital signs taken at nearly every healthcare visit — the vital-signs lesson covers the full set of measurements.

## Key Vocabulary

- **blood pressure** — The force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries as the heart pumps.
- **systolic pressure** — The pressure in the arteries when the heart beats and pumps blood; the top, first number of a reading.
- **diastolic pressure** — The pressure in the arteries when the heart rests between beats; the bottom, second number of a reading.
- **mmHg** — The unit blood pressure is measured in; short for millimeters of mercury.
- **hypertension** — The medical term for blood pressure that stays above normal over time.
- **normal blood pressure** — For adults, a reading below 120 mmHg systolic and below 80 mmHg diastolic, per the AHA and the CDC.
- **blood pressure cuff** — The inflatable band wrapped around the upper arm that squeezes the artery during a measurement.
- **risk factor** — A trait or habit, such as family history or a high-sodium diet, that raises the chance of a condition.

## Eli-10

Blood pressure is how hard your blood pushes against the walls of your arteries — the tubes that carry blood away from your heart. Every time your heart beats, it shoves a fresh wave of blood into those tubes and the pressure climbs; between beats it eases off. The reading has two numbers: the top (systolic) is the pressure when the heart pushes, and the bottom (diastolic) is the pressure between pushes. For adults, normal means less than 120 over less than 80 — and that line comes from guidelines that have shifted over the years, not from a law of nature. The check itself is simple: a cuff squeezes your upper arm while a stethoscope or machine listens. Salt, inactivity, extra weight, family history, and stress can all nudge the numbers up. And the catch: high blood pressure usually has no symptoms at all.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a garden hose. When the faucet is open and the hose is clear, water flows smoothly at a modest pressure — that is a normal reading. Turn the faucet up (the heart pumping harder) or pinch the hose narrower (arteries tightening), and the pressure inside rises; you can feel the hose grow stiffer under your hand. The blood pressure cuff works like your hand on the hose: it squeezes, then eases, and the pressure at which flow resumes tells you how hard the system is pushing.

Where the analogy breaks down: a hose has one steady pressure, but blood pressure has two numbers because the heart works in a beat-and-rest rhythm, not a steady stream. A hose's pressure changes only when someone touches the faucet; blood pressure shifts minute to minute with activity, stress, and even the time of day, and it can stay quietly high for years with no visible change to the person. And unlike a hose that bursts loudly, high blood pressure gives no dramatic warning — which is why the numbers have to be checked.

## Worked Example

Priya, a 45-year-old teacher, stops at a pharmacy blood pressure station. The machine wraps a cuff around her upper arm, inflates it, and displays 138/86. She is surprised — she feels fine. Two weeks later, her clinic measures her twice: 136/84 and 134/82. Her provider explains that the top number (systolic, 134-138) is the pressure when the heart pumps and the bottom number (diastolic, 82-86) is the pressure between beats; both sit above the normal reference of less than 120/80, in the range the 2017 guideline calls stage 1 hypertension. Priya has no symptoms — the numbers are the only sign. The provider reviews the factors the CDC links to blood pressure — sodium, physical activity, weight, stress — and schedules a follow-up. Nothing hurt and nothing felt wrong, and the reading still did its quiet work.

## Common Mistakes

- **"The top number is the pressure between heartbeats."** It is the other way around. Systolic (top) is the pressure when the heart beats and pumps blood; diastolic (bottom) is the pressure between beats, when the heart rests.
- **"One high reading means I have high blood pressure."** Readings move with activity, stress, and the time of day. Providers take two or more readings at separate appointments before diagnosing high blood pressure.
- **"I feel fine, so my blood pressure must be fine."** High blood pressure usually has no warning signs or symptoms. The CDC is blunt about it: measuring your blood pressure is the only way to know.
- **"The normal line is 140/90 — that is the standard."** The normal reference is less than 120/80 mmHg. The diagnostic thresholds come from guidelines that have shifted — 140/90 from a 2003 guideline, 130/80 from a 2017 guideline — which is why categories are guideline-based, not verdicts.
- **"High blood pressure runs in my family, so nothing I do matters."** Family history raises risk, but it does not decide the outcome. The CDC also names lifestyle factors — sodium, activity, weight, stress — and notes that risk factors raise the odds without sealing them.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Systolic pressure vs. Diastolic pressure:** Systolic is the pressure in the arteries when the heart beats and pumps — the top, first number. Diastolic is the pressure between beats, when the heart rests — the bottom, second number.
- **Normal blood pressure vs. Elevated blood pressure:** Normal is less than 120 systolic and less than 80 diastolic. Elevated is 120-129 systolic with diastolic still below 80 — a heads-up zone in the AHA categories, not yet high blood pressure.
- **Cuff measurement vs. Pulse check:** A cuff measures the force of blood against the artery walls and produces the two-number reading. Feeling a pulse only confirms the heart is beating and roughly how fast — it cannot report blood pressure.

## Key Takeaway

Blood pressure is the force of blood against the artery walls, written as two numbers — systolic over diastolic — with a normal adult reference of less than 120 over less than 80. Because high blood pressure usually has no symptoms, the quiet number only does its work when someone measures it.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **A nursing student reads a patient's chart and sees a blood pressure recorded as 128/84. Which statement correctly explains what the two numbers represent?**
   - The top number is the pressure when the heart rests between beats; the bottom number is the pressure when the heart pumps.
   - The top number is the pressure when the heart pumps blood; the bottom number is the pressure between beats.
   - The top number shows how fast the heart beats; the bottom number shows the strength of the pulse.
   - The top number is the pressure in the veins; the bottom number is the pressure in the arteries.
   - **Correct:** B — The top (first) number is the systolic pressure, the pressure in the arteries when the heart beats and pumps blood; the bottom (second) number is the diastolic pressure, the pressure between beats. The other options confuse pressure with pulse rate or vein pressure, which a blood pressure reading does not report.

2. **At a community health fair, a volunteer measures a 40-year-old adult's blood pressure three times over the afternoon: 116/74, 118/76, and 115/75. Which label best fits these readings?**
   - High blood pressure, stage 2.
   - Elevated — watch the numbers.
   - Normal — below 120 systolic and below 80 diastolic.
   - Hypertensive crisis.
   - **Correct:** C — Each reading is below 120 systolic and below 80 diastolic, matching the normal category in the AHA chart and the CDC's statement that a normal blood pressure level is less than 120/80 mmHg. Elevated, stage 2, and crisis all require higher numbers.

3. **A patient asks why the nurse uses an inflatable cuff around the upper arm instead of just checking the wrist pulse. Which explanation is accurate?**
   - The cuff measures how many times the heart beats each minute.
   - The cuff measures the oxygen level in the blood.
   - The cuff warms the arm so the blood flows faster.
   - The cuff squeezes the artery so the force of blood against the artery wall can be read as two numbers.
   - **Correct:** D — The cuff squeezes the artery while a stethoscope or electronic sensor listens, letting the gauge record systolic and diastolic pressure. A pulse check tells you the heart is beating and roughly how fast, but it cannot produce a pressure reading.

4. **A 55-year-old man feels perfectly well, exercises regularly, and has no symptoms of any kind. A routine clinic visit shows his blood pressure is consistently high. Why is this possible?**
   - High blood pressure usually has no warning signs or symptoms, so a person can have it while feeling fine; measuring is the only way to know.
   - High blood pressure always causes headaches and dizziness first, so this reading must be an error.
   - Blood pressure rises only in people who feel stressed, and he reports no stress.
   - Symptoms of high blood pressure appear only after age 60, so he is too young to have it.
   - **Correct:** A — The CDC states that high blood pressure usually has no warning signs or symptoms and that many people do not know they have it — measuring is the only way to know. Headaches and dizziness are not reliable signals, stress is not the only factor, and high blood pressure is not limited to people over 60.

5. **A student nurse is making a poster about factors that can raise blood pressure. Which item belongs on the list, according to the CDC?**
   - Eating too much sodium, which is an element in table salt.
   - Drinking extra water every day.
   - Getting regular physical activity.
   - Keeping a healthy weight.
   - **Correct:** A — The CDC identifies eating too much sodium as a factor that increases blood pressure, along with physical inactivity, overweight and obesity, family history, and stress. The other options are protective habits, not risk factors.
