# Interfaith Relations

## In 30 Seconds

Interfaith relations are the dialogue and cooperation that develop across religious boundaries. Interfaith dialogue is structured conversation in which people of different traditions listen to and learn from one another while also sharing their own faith. Its goals include mutual understanding, reducing prejudice, cooperating on shared concerns, and peacebuilding. Interfaith work takes many forms, from dialogue groups and shared service projects to councils and education. It is not conversion, syncretism, or relativism: participants keep their own commitments. A landmark moment was the 1893 World's Parliament of Religions in Chicago.

## Why This Matters

Interfaith relations are one of the most visible responses to religious diversity, and studying them is a way to think clearly about how religious communities actually interact. The vocabulary matters: dialogue, cooperation, and pluralism are often used loosely, and confusing dialogue with conversion, syncretism, or relativism misdescribes what participants are doing. Knowing the history — from the 1893 Parliament to the growth of interfaith organizations after September 11, 2001 — also helps explain why these movements look the way they do today. And scholarly critiques remind students that interfaith engagement is contested rather than settled: its promise and its limits are both subjects of research.

## Learning Objectives

- Define interfaith relations and interfaith dialogue as structured conversation and cooperation across religious boundaries.

- Distinguish interfaith dialogue from conversion, syncretism, and relativism.

## The College Version

### What interfaith relations are

Interfaith relations is the scholarly name for dialogue and cooperation across religious boundaries. The words "interfaith" and "interreligious" are themselves relatively recent: the Pluralism Project at Harvard notes that they came into common use in the late twentieth century, as growing religious diversity made conversation between traditions a practical question rather than a distant ideal. Interfaith movements, on this account, are premised on respecting each tradition's distinctiveness — they do not aim to create a new faith, and they assume participants will remain members of their own communities.

Interfaith dialogue, the Pluralism Project explains, is a relationship in which people bring their own faith into genuine encounter with the faith of a neighbor: listening to and learning from people of other traditions as well as sharing one's own. Dialogue is not a monologue and not a debate; it depends on exchange in both directions.

### Goals and forms of interfaith work

Interfaith work is usually described as serving several goals. The first is mutual understanding: participants learn what other traditions actually teach and practice rather than relying on stereotypes. A second is reducing prejudice, on the premise that direct encounter can soften distrust between communities. A third is cooperation on shared concerns — the Pluralism Project observes that Christians, Muslims, Buddhists, Jews, Hindus, and Humanists may explain problems such as growing inequality, rising violence in cities, or climate change in different ways, citing different scriptures and teachings, yet increasingly recognize the need to work together on them. Peacebuilding is a fourth goal: interfaith organizations often present themselves as building relationships that make conflict less likely.

The forms of interfaith work are correspondingly varied. Dialogue groups bring people of different traditions together for structured conversation. Scriptural reasoning is a specific practice in which participants read and discuss passages from their sacred scriptures together; it began among theologians and religious philosophers and has since spread beyond academic settings. Shared service projects unite communities around practical needs such as homelessness relief or disaster response. Interfaith councils are standing bodies — often city-wide or campus-wide — that coordinate ongoing relationships among congregations and traditions. Interfaith education introduces students and the public to the beliefs and practices of traditions other than their own. The Pluralism Project documents dozens of such organizations and programs across the United States.

### A brief history

The 1893 World's Parliament of Religions is the usual starting point. Held in Chicago as part of the World's Columbian Exposition, it ran for seventeen days in September 1893 and brought together some four hundred men and women representing forty-one denominations and religious traditions. The Parliament of the World's Religions describes it as the first formal gathering of representatives of Eastern and Western spiritual traditions, and the organization calls it the birthplace of the worldwide interfaith movement. The organizers' stated aims are revealing: to promote friendly conference and mutual good understanding among people of diverse faiths, while explicitly not seeking to foster the temper of indifferentism and not striving to achieve any formal and outward unity.

The twentieth century saw the movement develop unevenly. The vocabulary of interfaith and interreligious work took shape only in its later decades, alongside growing religious diversity in Western societies. A decisive turning point came after September 11, 2001: the Pluralism Project reports that conversations about interfaith dialogue and the need to understand the faith of others gained new urgency, and several interfaith organizations were founded in the years that followed.

### What dialogue is not

Practitioners and scholars alike are careful about what interfaith dialogue is not. It is not conversion: the Pluralism Project's account of dialogue contrasts it with evangelization, since dialogue does not aim to persuade anyone to change religion. It is not syncretism: interfaith movements are premised on respecting each tradition's distinctiveness rather than blending traditions into a new religion, and the 1893 Parliament explicitly disavowed any goal of formal outward unity. And it is not relativism: participants are not asked to treat their traditions' claims as interchangeable or to suspend their commitments. The Pluralism Project makes the same point about pluralism more broadly — pluralism is not relativism: it does not ask people to set their commitments aside. Dialogue assumes real difference and real commitment; it is the conversation that happens across them.

### Scholarly perspectives, and dialogue's relation to pluralism

Scholarship on interfaith relations combines promise and critique. The promise is the one practitioners claim: dialogue produces mutual understanding and may reduce prejudice between communities. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes, however, that the question whether acknowledging religious diversity actually leads to tolerance is itself disputed — some scholars are skeptical that awareness of diversity reliably reduces prejudice. Critics have also raised structural concerns. Mikel Burley, cited in the same entry, worries that approaches focused on general abstract concepts downplay "the particularities of historically and geographically distinct conceptions of God" in actual lived religions — a version of the worry that comparative or dialogical framings can flatten difference. Others, such as John Schellenberg, point to the field's historical imbalance toward theistic and especially Christian sources. These are descriptions of scholarly debate, not verdicts.

Finally, interfaith relations is the practical face of pluralism. The Pluralism Project defines pluralism as "the energetic engagement with diversity" — not diversity alone and not mere tolerance — with dialogue as the language of that engagement. The sibling lesson on religious diversity develops that distinction in depth; this lesson focuses on what the engagement looks like in practice.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Interfaith dialogue:** Structured conversation between people of different religious traditions in which participants listen to and learn from one another as well as share their own faith.
- **Interreligious:** An adjective used alongside "interfaith" to describe dialogue and cooperation that cross religious boundaries.
- **Syncretism:** The blending of elements from different religions into a new combination; interfaith dialogue does not aim at this.
- **Relativism:** The view that no tradition's claims are better grounded than another's; dialogue does not require participants to adopt this view.
- **Conversion:** A change from one religious commitment to another; interfaith dialogue is not an attempt to bring this about.
- **Scriptural reasoning:** An interfaith practice in which members of different traditions meet in groups to read and discuss their sacred scriptures together.
- **Pluralism:** The positive engagement with religious difference, in the Pluralism Project's framing "the energetic engagement with diversity."

## Eli-10

Imagine two neighbors, one Jewish and one Muslim, who have never really talked. Interfaith relations is the name scholars give to what happens when they start: real conversation across the boundary between their traditions. In that conversation each person listens and learns — what does the other's holiday actually celebrate? — and each also shares what their own faith means to them. Nobody is trying to get the other to switch religions, and nobody is trying to melt the two traditions into one. They are trying to understand each other and, often, to find things they can do together, like a neighborhood food drive or a response to local violence. After September 11, 2001, this kind of organized conversation grew quickly, as communities decided that not knowing about one another's faiths had become too costly.

## Eli's Analogy

Interfaith dialogue is like musicians from different traditions playing in one ensemble. A sitar player, a cellist, and a drummer each keep their own instrument, tuning, and style; the music works when each listens to the others and plays with them, not when everyone tries to sound the same.

This analogy has limits: music blends naturally, while religious commitments can genuinely conflict. An ensemble aims at harmony by design; interfaith dialogue has no guarantee of agreement, and participants may leave disagreeing as much as they arrived — the point is understanding, not blend.

## Worked Example

A student studies interfaith life in a mid-sized city after 2001. She finds an interfaith council founded in 2003, part of the surge of interfaith organizing that followed September 11, 2001. She attends a council meeting and a dinner-dialogue series, where participants take turns explaining their traditions and answering questions. She also sits in on a scriptural reasoning group: a rabbi, a minister, and an imam read passages from their scriptures aloud and discuss them. In her report she classifies each activity by form — dialogue group, council, scriptural reasoning — and she is careful to note that no participant tried to convert anyone or to merge the traditions. She concludes that the city's interfaith work is best described as cooperation across difference, not unity of belief.

## Common Mistakes

- **Thinking dialogue requires giving up your own faith.** The Pluralism Project describes dialogue as listening and learning as well as sharing one's own faith; interfaith movements are premised on respecting each tradition's distinctiveness.
- **Treating interfaith dialogue as a step toward one world religion.** Interfaith work is not syncretism; the 1893 Parliament explicitly disavowed any goal of formal and outward unity, and participants keep their own commitments.
- **Equating dialogue with tolerance or relativism.** Tolerance can require no knowledge of the other at all, and relativism asks people to set commitments aside; dialogue assumes real difference and real commitment.
- **Assuming interfaith engagement reliably reduces prejudice.** Scholars dispute whether acknowledging religious diversity actually leads to tolerance, so the honest claim is that reducing prejudice is a goal, not a guaranteed outcome.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dialogue | Debate | Dialogue aims at mutual understanding through listening and learning; debate aims at winning an argument. |
| Interfaith dialogue | Syncretism | Dialogue preserves each tradition's distinctiveness; syncretism blends traditions into a new combination. |
| Tolerance | Pluralism and dialogue | Tolerance requires putting up with difference; pluralism and dialogue require active engagement with it (Pluralism Project). |

## Key Takeaway

Interfaith relations are dialogue and cooperation across religious boundaries — aimed at understanding, not conversion, syncretism, or relativism — and they have grown from the 1893 Parliament into a field scholars both study and critique.
