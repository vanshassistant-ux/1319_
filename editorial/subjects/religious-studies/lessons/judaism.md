# Judaism

## In 30 Seconds

Judaism is a monotheistic religious and cultural tradition that emerged in the ancient Middle East. Its foundational story centers on a covenant, a special relationship between God and the Jewish people, marked by the giving of the Torah. Practice revolves around mitzvot (commandments), prayer, synagogue and home rituals, and a cycle of holidays. Jews today are diverse: Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist movements, secular and cultural Jews, and communities shaped by Ashkenazi, Sephardi, and Mizrahi histories. Judaism is one of the Abrahamic religions, alongside Christianity and Islam.

## Why This Matters

Judaism is a major world tradition with deep roots in the Middle East and a global presence shaped by diaspora, modern Israel, and centuries of migration. Because Jewish identity can be religious, cultural, ethnic, or several of these at once, students who treat the tradition as one uniform creed will misread the evidence. Describing it accurately means naming its internal diversity, from denominational movements to secular Jews, and understanding concepts such as covenant and Torah that also matter for studying Christianity and Islam, which share Judaism's Abrahamic heritage. That skill transfers directly to the study of any religious tradition.

## Learning Objectives

- Define Judaism as a diverse religious and cultural tradition rather than a single uniform creed.
- Explain the covenant, the written and oral Torah, and the idea of a chosen people as tradition and scholars describe them.
- Describe core concepts and practices, including mitzvot, tzedakah, tikkun olam, the Sabbath, and the major holidays.
- Distinguish the general orientations of Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist Judaism.
- Describe the general character of the Tanakh and the Talmud without quoting from them.
- Analyze how diaspora, modern Israel, and Holocaust memory shape Jewish life and identity today.

## The College Version

### A covenant people: the foundational story

Judaism is one of the world's major monotheistic traditions. Its foundational story, as tradition tells it and scholars describe it, centers on a covenant: a binding relationship between God and the Jewish people. The story begins with Abraham, whom the tradition presents as the ancestor of the Jewish people and whom Christianity and Islam also recognize, which is why scholars group the three as the Abrahamic religions. The covenant was renewed, in the tradition's telling, at Mount Sinai through Moses, when the people received the Torah and agreed to keep God's laws. Jews believe God appointed them to be a chosen people in order to set an example of holiness and ethical behaviour to the world. Scholars describe this idea of chosenness as an internal theological claim about responsibility, not a ranking of peoples, and not all Jewish movements affirm it. OpenStax's sociology textbook summarizes the same elements: monotheism, the covenant as a promise of special relationship with God, and the Torah as the sacred Jewish text.

### Core concepts: Torah, mitzvot, tzedakah, and tikkun olam

The word Torah means teaching, instruction, or law. In its narrow sense it names the first five books of Jewish scripture; in a broader sense it covers the whole body of Jewish teaching, including the oral law. Tradition describes both a written Torah and an oral Torah, the latter transmitted by teachers for generations before it was written down. Mitzvot are the commandments or obligations that guide Jewish practice; tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah. Tzedakah is charitable giving, understood in Jewish tradition not as optional generosity but as a commanded duty of righteousness and justice. Tikkun olam, often translated as repairing or healing the world, has a long interpretive history in rabbinic texts and has become, in modern Jewish life, a common framing for social justice and efforts to improve society. Jews emphasize moral behavior and action in this world, a point OpenStax highlights in its overview of the tradition.

### The Sabbath and the rhythm of the year

Shabbat, the Sabbath, is the weekly day of rest, observed from Friday evening to Saturday evening. It begins with candle lighting and is marked by festive meals with challah bread, wine, rest from work, and synagogue attendance. The year is structured by holidays, each with its own character: Rosh Hashanah, the Jewish New Year, a two-day autumn festival of reflection and repentance marked by the blowing of the shofar; Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, the most solemn day, observed with fasting and prayer; Passover, which commemorates the liberation of the Israelites from slavery in Egypt with the ritual seder meal and unleavened bread; and Hanukkah, the eight-day festival of lights recalling the rededication of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem. These holidays are widespread, but observance varies across denominations, communities, and families.

### Practice and lifecycle: prayer, synagogue, and home

Jewish religious life is built around prayer, the synagogue, and the home. The synagogue serves as a house of worship, study, and community gathering; it houses the Torah scrolls in the Ark, and communal prayer requires a minyan, a quorum of worshippers. Services may be led by a rabbi, a cantor, or a member of the congregation. Much of Jewish practice centers on the home and family: the Sabbath meals, holiday celebrations, and daily blessings. Lifecycle events mark the passage of life: brit milah, the ritual circumcision of boys traditionally on the eighth day, recalling the covenant with Abraham; bar and bat mitzvah, the coming-of-age ceremonies at which young people, at thirteen for boys and twelve for girls in some congregations, take on responsibility for the commandments; and Jewish marriage ceremonies, which traditionally include rituals such as the breaking of a glass. Rabbis are the tradition's spiritual leaders and interpreters of Jewish law; as Britannica notes, they do not hold priestly status in the way priests do in some other traditions.

### Texts: the Tanakh and the Talmud

The Tanakh is the whole of the Hebrew scriptures, an acronym of its three parts: Torah (Teaching), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings). The Talmud is a different kind of work: the written record of the Jewish oral law and of the rabbinic discussions around it, composed of the Mishnah and the Gemara, with Jerusalem and Babylonian versions. It is the source from which the code of Jewish law, halakhah, is derived. Like any tradition's scriptures, these texts are studied, debated, and interpreted; this lesson describes their general character and never quotes them.

### Diversity and the modern world

Judaism today is internally diverse. The major denominational movements have different general orientations: Orthodox Judaism upholds the Torah and Jewish law as binding divine revelation; Conservative Judaism (Masorti) keeps traditional practice while interpreting the tradition in light of modern scholarship; Reform Judaism, which emerged in early nineteenth-century Germany, treats the tradition as evolving and adapts practice to modern life; and Reconstructionist Judaism, an American movement of the twentieth century, describes Judaism as an evolving religious civilization created by the Jewish people. Beyond the denominations, many people identify as Jewish culturally or secularly, without religious observance. Jewish communities also differ by regional heritage: Ashkenazi communities developed in Central and Eastern Europe, Sephardi communities trace their heritage to the Iberian Peninsula and the Mediterranean world, and Mizrahi communities to the Middle East and North Africa. An estimated 13 to 15 million Jewish people live worldwide, most of them in Israel and the United States, a distribution shaped by diaspora and migration. The Holocaust, the systematic murder of about six million Jews, is commemorated in Jewish communities with observances such as Yom Ha-Shoah, and scholars and community leaders describe Holocaust memory as central to modern Jewish identity and religious life.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Covenant:** A binding agreement between God and the Jewish people that tradition describes as the foundation of the relationship, expressed through keeping God's laws.
- **Torah:** Teaching, instruction, or law; in the narrow sense the first five books of Jewish scripture, and in a broader sense the whole body of Jewish teaching including the oral law.
- **Tanakh:** The Hebrew scriptures as a whole, an acronym of Torah (Teaching), Nevi'im (Prophets), and Ketuvim (Writings).
- **Talmud:** The written record of the Jewish oral law and of rabbinic discussion around it, composed of the Mishnah and the Gemara, with Jerusalem and Babylonian versions.
- **Mitzvot:** Commandments or obligations that guide Jewish practice; tradition counts 613 commandments in the Torah.
- **Tzedakah:** Charitable giving understood in Jewish tradition as a commanded duty of righteousness and justice, not optional generosity.
- **Tikkun Olam:** A Hebrew phrase meaning repairing or healing the world, used in modern Jewish life as a framing for social justice and improving society.
- **Shabbat:** The weekly Jewish day of rest observed from Friday evening to Saturday evening, marked by candle lighting, festive meals, and rest.
- **Synagogue:** A Jewish house of worship, study, and community gathering, where the Torah is read and communal prayer takes place.
- **Diaspora:** Jewish communities living outside the land of Israel, and the dispersion that created them; most Jews today live in diaspora communities.

## Eli-10

Judaism is a tradition built on a story, a book, a way of life, and a people. The foundational story says that God made a covenant with the Jewish people, choosing them to set an example of holiness and ethical behaviour, and gave them the Torah as a guide. That story is told as history by the tradition and studied critically by scholars, and both descriptions can be learned side by side. Around that core, Jews built a way of life: commandments to follow, prayers to say, a day of rest each week, holidays through the year, and ceremonies for birth, coming of age, and marriage. There is no single Jewish way. Orthodox, Conservative, Reform, and Reconstructionist movements differ in how they understand revelation and law; many Jews are secular or cultural Jews; and communities in Europe, the Middle East, the Americas, and elsewhere each carry their own customs.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a family house that many generations have added to. The original rooms, the covenant and the Torah, are still the core, but each generation built new wings, rearranged rooms, and furnished them differently. Some family members keep the house's old rules strictly; others gather for the family meals and keep the stories without following every rule; still others identify with the family name and history while living mostly outside the house. They all call it the same house.

The analogy has limits. The analogy has limits. A house's rooms never argue with one another, while Jewish movements debate the meaning of the law and disagree about it. A house also sits in one place, while Jewish communities span the globe with very different customs and histories. And a house is a thing people own; a tradition is something people continually remake, interpret, and argue about.

## Worked Example

A student is writing a short essay on the Sabbath. She reads BBC Religions' description: Shabbat begins at nightfall on Friday and lasts until nightfall on Saturday, marked by candle lighting, festive meals with challah, rest from work, and synagogue attendance. She drafts: 'Jewish families observe Shabbat from Friday evening to Saturday evening with candles, meals, and rest.' Before submitting, she checks her claim against a second source and adds a key qualification: observance varies across denominations and families, so no single description covers every Jewish household. Her final sentence describes the general pattern without asserting uniformity, which keeps the claim accurate.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating Judaism as one uniform belief system with a single set of practices.** Judaism spans denominational movements with different orientations, plus secular and cultural Jews; the first move in studying it is to name that diversity.
- **Confusing the Torah with the whole of Jewish scripture.** The Torah is the first five books and, more broadly, all Jewish teaching; the whole Hebrew scriptures are the Tanakh, and the Talmud is the written record of rabbinic discussion of the oral law.
- **Reading 'chosen people' as a claim of superiority.** Tradition describes chosenness as a responsibility to set an example of holiness and ethical behaviour; scholars describe it as an internal theological claim, and not all Jewish movements affirm it.
- **Assuming every Jew keeps the same holidays, dietary rules, and rituals.** Observance varies widely by denomination, community, and family, and some Jews identify as Jewish culturally or secularly without religious observance.
- **Treating tikkun olam as an ancient, fixed doctrine.** The phrase appears in rabbinic texts but its modern meaning, repairing the world through social justice, is a distinctly contemporary framing.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Torah / Talmud:** The Torah is the foundational teaching (the five books and, broadly, all Jewish teaching); the Talmud is the written record of rabbinic discussion of the oral law, compiled centuries later.
- **Orthodox Judaism / Reform Judaism:** Orthodox movements uphold the Torah and Jewish law as binding divine revelation; Reform Judaism, emerging in early nineteenth-century Germany, treats the tradition as evolving and adapts practice to modern life.
- **Ashkenazi communities / Sephardi communities:** Ashkenazi communities developed in Central and Eastern Europe; Sephardi communities trace their heritage to the Iberian Peninsula and the Mediterranean world, and each carries its own customs and liturgical traditions.

## Key Takeaway

Judaism is a diverse religious and cultural tradition rooted in covenant and Torah, best studied by describing what adherents believe and do across its movements, communities, and interpretations.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **In Jewish tradition, what does the Hebrew term mitzvot most commonly refer to?**  
   A. Commandments or obligations that guide Jewish practice
   B. The weekly portions read aloud from the Torah scroll
   C. The rabbinic discussions recorded in the Talmud
   D. The quorum of worshippers required for communal prayer
   **Answer:** A. Commandments or obligations that guide Jewish practice

2. **A student observes a Jewish family lighting candles at sunset on Friday, sharing a meal with challah bread, and resting until Saturday evening. Which observance is this?**  
   A. Passover
   B. Yom Kippur
   C. Shabbat
   D. Hanukkah
   **Answer:** C. Shabbat

3. **Why do scholars group Judaism with Christianity and Islam as 'Abrahamic' religions?**  
   A. All three were founded in the same century
   B. All three recognize the Talmud as scripture
   C. All three require pilgrimage to Jerusalem
   D. All three trace their origins to the figure of Abraham
   **Answer:** D. All three trace their origins to the figure of Abraham

4. **A textbook describes Reconstructionist Judaism as viewing Judaism as 'an evolving religious civilization.' Which statement best reflects that orientation?**  
   A. Jewish law was revealed once and cannot change
   B. Jewish tradition and culture are created and reshaped by the Jewish people over time
   C. Only the written Torah, not later rabbinic discussion, carries authority
   D. Jewish identity should be limited to religious belief alone
   **Answer:** B. Jewish tradition and culture are created and reshaped by the Jewish people over time

5. **A student writing about Jewish ethics wants to describe tzedakah accurately. Which sentence is correct?**  
   A. Tzedakah is the weekly day of rest observed from Friday evening to Saturday evening
   B. Tzedakah is charitable giving understood as a commanded duty of righteousness and justice
   C. Tzedakah is the festival commemorating the rededication of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem
   D. Tzedakah is the quorum of adults required for communal prayer
   **Answer:** B. Tzedakah is charitable giving understood as a commanded duty of righteousness and justice
