# Shinto

## In 30 Seconds

Shinto is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan, centered on kami — sacred or divine presences that may be spirits, forces of nature, or elements of the landscape, and that scholars stress are not gods in the Western sense. Shinto has no founder and no canonical scriptures; it is built around ritual: visiting shrines, purification, offerings, and festivals. Scholars distinguish shrine, sect, and folk forms, and in Japanese life Shinto has long coexisted and blended with Buddhism.

## Why This Matters

Studying Shinto opens a window onto how a major tradition can be built on practice rather than doctrine: no founder, no creed, no canonical scripture, yet a religion deeply woven into national and daily life. It also challenges a common Western assumption that every religion centers on a god or gods: kami resist that category, and scholars describe them as sacred or divine presences instead. Because most Japanese people participate in both Shinto and Buddhist practices, Shinto is a case study in religious coexistence and blending. Finally, its forms — shrine, sect, folk, and the historical state Shinto — show how scholars organize a tradition and how religious life and politics can become entangled, a theme this lesson only touches.

## The College Version

### Kami: sacred presences, not gods

Shinto is the indigenous religious tradition of Japan, and its heart is devotion to kami — invisible spiritual beings and powers — together with the shrines and rituals that honor them. Britannica describes kami as sacred or divine powers that are closer to human beings than the transcendent deities of monotheistic religions. Kami can be spirits, forces of nature, or elements of the landscape: a mountain, a waterfall, or a remarkable tree may be the site of a kami. Scholars stress that kami are not gods in the Western sense; BBC Religions notes that even the common translation "spirits" oversimplifies the concept. Shinto has no founder, no canonical scriptures, and no commandments; it is ritual-centered rather than creed-centered, and what matters is the practice that lets people communicate with kami.

### The forms of Shinto

Scholars commonly organize Shinto into several forms. Shrine Shinto (jinja Shinto) is the mainstream institutional form, organized around local shrines — jinja — where kami are venerated through regular ritual. Folk Shinto is the informal layer of practice rooted in households and local communities: small wayside shrines, seasonal customs, and festivals that run alongside the institutional shrines. Sect Shinto is the collective term for Shinto-derived religious movements that organized themselves as distinct groups in the modern era. State Shinto is different in kind: it was a historical development of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries in which the state promoted shrine rituals as national ceremonies, and it came to an end after World War II. Scholars treat these categories as descriptive tools rather than boundaries practitioners themselves drew.

### Practice: purification, offerings, festivals

Shinto practice centers on the shrine and its rituals. The torii, a distinctive gate, marks the boundary between the shrine precinct and the everyday world. Visitors typically purify themselves before approaching the kami: harae, purification rites, remove impurity or pollution, and washing hands and mouth at a basin is a familiar form. Worship involves offerings, prayer, and gestures of respect before the shrine hall. Festivals, matsuri, honor the kami and bind communities together through processions, music, and celebration. Many shrines also offer omikuji, paper fortunes that visitors draw for predictions about the year ahead. Purification, offering, and festival recur across shrines, though the details vary from place to place.

### Shinto in Japanese life

Shinto is woven into ordinary Japanese life. The most popular shared practice is hatsumode, the first shrine visit of the New Year, which BBC Religions describes as the most popular shared national event in Japan. Shrines are also settings for weddings and for rites marking children's growth, and families visit local shrines for seasonal festivals and personal petitions throughout the year. Because Shinto does not require exclusive adherence, many Japanese people take part in Shinto rites at shrines and Buddhist rites at temples without conflict; the two traditions mark different moments of life. This pattern makes Shinto a striking example of a religion embedded in everyday culture rather than separated from it.

### Texts: the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki

Shinto has no canonical scripture, but it does have early texts that scholars study for the tradition's history. The Kojiki, compiled in the early eighth century, and the Nihon Shoki, completed shortly after, are the two oldest surviving myth-histories of Japan: they weave together accounts of kami, legendary rulers, and the early events of the Japanese islands. These works are described here in general terms and never quoted; interpreting their stories belongs to the study of myth and of early Japanese history, topics treated in their own lessons.

### Shinto and Buddhism: coexistence and blending

For more than a millennium, Shinto and Buddhism have coexisted in Japan, and their histories are deeply intertwined. When Buddhism arrived from the Asian mainland, it did not displace kami worship; the two traditions developed side by side, and at various periods kami came to be understood in Buddhist terms — for example, as protectors of Buddhist teaching or as manifestations of buddhas. This blending shaped shrines, temples, and festivals alike. Buddhism has its own lesson; here it matters as the partner tradition that Japanese religious life has long shared. Today Shinto remains an active tradition: shrines are maintained across Japan, festivals continue, and Shinto rites mark national and personal occasions, even as most Japanese also take part in Buddhist practice.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Kami:** Sacred or divine presences in Shinto: spirits, forces of nature, or elements of the landscape, closer to humans than the transcendent deities of monotheistic religions.
- **Jinja:** A Shinto shrine: the precinct and buildings where a kami is venerated through ritual.
- **Harae:** Purification rites in Shinto that remove impurity or pollution, often performed before approaching a kami.
- **Matsuri:** Shinto festivals that honor kami, typically involving offerings, processions, and community celebration.
- **Torii:** The gate that marks the entrance to a Shinto shrine, separating the sacred precinct from the everyday world.
- **Omikuji:** Paper fortunes drawn at many Shinto shrines that offer predictions about a visitor's fortunes.
- **Hatsumode:** The first shrine visit of the new year, described as the most popular shared Shinto practice in Japan.
- **Shrine Shinto:** The mainstream, institutionally organized form of Shinto centered on local shrines (jinja) and their rituals.
- **Sect Shinto:** A collective term for Shinto-derived religious movements organized as distinct groups, mainly from the modern era.
- **Folk Shinto:** The informal layer of Shinto practice rooted in households, local communities, and seasonal customs.

## Eli-10

Shinto is the oldest religious layer of Japanese life — a tradition of spirits and sacred presences called kami. Kami are not like the all-powerful gods of some other religions; they are closer to us. A river, a mountain, or a striking tree might be honored as kami. Shinto has no founder, no holy book, and no list of commandments. What it has is practice: wash, offer, pray, celebrate. People visit shrines, purify themselves, make offerings, and join festivals through the year. Because Shinto never demanded exclusive loyalty, it has sat comfortably beside Buddhism for over a thousand years, and most Japanese take part in both.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a neighborhood shrine as a house shared by the community and its kami. You do not move in or sign a contract; you visit. You wash your hands at the gate, offer something small, say what is on your mind, and join the festival when it comes around. The kami are like respected residents — present, powerful in their own way, and honored through attention and care.

The house analogy suggests each kami lives in one fixed place, but kami are tied to particular sites yet also understood as present more widely, and shrines are rebuilt in cycles. A household also has a single clear purpose, while Shinto has many forms — institutional shrines, local folk customs, and modern groups — that the analogy does not capture.

## Common Mistakes

- **Translating kami as "gods" and picturing them like the deities of Greek or monotheistic religion.** Scholars describe kami as sacred or divine presences — spirits, forces of nature, or landscape elements — not transcendent, all-powerful gods; even the common translation "spirits" oversimplifies the concept.
- **Assuming Shinto is exclusive — that a Japanese person is either Shinto or Buddhist.** Shinto does not require adherents to treat it as their only religion; most Japanese participate in both Shinto and Buddhist rites, and the two traditions have blended for over a millennium.
- **Treating state Shinto as an ancient or still-official form of Shinto.** State Shinto was a historical development of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, tied to the Japanese state, and it came to an end after World War II; Japan has no official state religion today.
- **Expecting Shinto to have a founder, a creed, or a canonical scripture.** Shinto has no founder and no canonical scriptures; it is a ritual-centered tradition, and early texts such as the Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki are myth-histories studied as historical sources.

## Key Takeaway

Shinto is Japan's indigenous, ritual-centered tradition of kami — sacred presences rather than gods — whose shrines, festivals, and life-cycle rites coexist with Buddhism in everyday Japanese life.

## Practice Question Bank

1. In the study of Shinto, how do scholars most accurately describe kami?
   A. Sacred or divine presences — spirits, forces of nature, or elements of the landscape
   B. A fixed pantheon of anthropomorphic gods equivalent to the deities of Greek religion
   C. Ghosts of the dead who must be appeased to prevent misfortune
   D. Abstract philosophical principles with no role in ritual practice
   **Answer: A.** Scholars describe kami as sacred or divine powers that are not gods in the Western sense: they can be spirits, forces of nature, or features of the landscape, and they are closer to human beings than transcendent deities.

2. A family visits a shrine at New Year: they purify themselves at a basin, draw paper fortunes, and later that year join the shrine's festival procession. Which set of Shinto terms describes these activities?
   A. Kojiki, Nihon Shoki, and kamidana
   B. Harae, omikuji, and matsuri
   C. Jinja, shimenawa, and shichigosan
   D. Sect, folk, and shrine forms
   **Answer: B.** The activities described are purification (harae), paper fortunes (omikuji), and a festival (matsuri); the other options name early texts, shrine objects, or scholarly categories rather than the practices shown.

3. A student writes: "Shinto is the traditional religion of Japan, so Japanese people are exclusively Shinto." Which response best reflects what scholars say?
   A. Correct — Shinto's long history means it has always required exclusive membership.
   B. Correct — because Shinto has no founder, anyone may join and must leave other religions.
   C. Mistaken — Shinto does not require adherents to treat it as their only religion, and it has long coexisted and blended with Buddhism in Japan.
   D. Mistaken — Shinto is a set of beliefs only, and Japanese people follow it privately.
   **Answer: C.** Scholars note that Shinto does not require exclusive adherence and that most Japanese participate in Shinto and Buddhist practices together, so the student's inference about exclusivity is not supported.

4. Which pair of early works are most commonly named as the early Japanese myth-histories in accounts of Shinto's textual history?
   A. The Analects and the Daodejing
   B. The Torah and the Talmud
   C. The Vedas and the Upanishads
   D. The Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki
   **Answer: D.** The Kojiki and the Nihon Shoki are the two earliest surviving myth-histories of Japan, compiled in the early eighth century; the other pairs belong to Chinese, Jewish, and Indian traditions.

5. Which statement about state Shinto matches what scholars describe?
   A. It was a historical development of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, tied to the Japanese state, that came to an end after World War II.
   B. It is the oldest form of Shinto, dating from the founding of the first shrines.
   C. It is the official religion of Japan today, maintained by the national government.
   D. It is a branch of sect Shinto focused on new religious movements.
   **Answer: A.** Scholars describe state Shinto as a now-defunct development within shrine Shinto of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, ended after World War II — not an ancient form, not the official religion today, and not a sect-Shinto branch.
