# Religion and Violence

## In 30 Seconds

Religion and violence is a scholarly problem, not a settled label. Scholars debate whether "religious violence" names a distinct kind of violence at all, since conflicts usually mix religious, political, and economic causes. Sacred texts and religious histories contain both peaceful and violent elements, and communities interpret them differently. Two broad traditions of moral thinking about violence — just war reasoning and pacifism — appear across cultures. Religious actors also build peace: they mediate conflicts, lead nonviolence movements, and cooperate across traditions. Careful study describes specifics without sensationalism or stereotyping.

## Why This Matters

Headlines often reduce complex conflicts to religion, and religious traditions are sometimes caricatured as essentially violent or essentially peaceful. Learning how scholars actually study religion and violence — the definitional debate, textual complexity, just war and pacifist traditions, chaplaincy, and peacebuilding — lets you read claims more carefully and describe cases accurately. These analytical habits transfer beyond this topic: they train you to weigh multiple causes, respect internal diversity within traditions, and separate description from judgment in any contested field.

## Learning Objectives

- Define the scholarly debate over whether "religious violence" is a coherent analytical category.
- Distinguish just war reasoning from pacifism as traditions of moral thinking about violence.
- Explain how sacred texts and religious histories contain both peaceful and violent elements.
- Apply the descriptive, comparative study of religion to a case without stereotyping any tradition.
- Analyze why scholars typically identify mixed religious, political, and economic factors in conflicts.

## The College Version

### The definitional question

The phrase "religious violence" sounds like a simple description, but scholars treat it as a contested analytical category. They ask what it would mean for violence to be religious rather than political, economic, or social — and whether those causes can be separated at all. Britannica's overview of war notes that the causes of war are debated, with theories pointing to psychological factors or to social relations and institutions, and that ideologies, whether religious or political, can intensify conflicts. The United States Institute of Peace, by contrast, emphasizes that religion can be a powerful driver of violent conflict, stoking prejudice, legitimating violence, and mobilizing communities into war. Because the definition of religion itself is unsettled in Religious Studies, the category "religious violence" inherits that contest. The scholarly task is therefore not to decide once and for all whether religion causes violence; it is to examine a specific case and describe how religious elements interact with other factors.

### Violence in sacred texts and religious histories

Sacred texts are rarely single-voiced. Scholars observe that the same scripture can carry passages associated with peace and passages associated with war, and that communities have interpreted those passages very differently over time. The Qur'an, for example, presents multiple interpretations of jihad, including an internal spiritual struggle and armed combat, and scholars note that the common Western rendering "holy war" is misleading. Peace teachings have deep roots as well: the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy points to pacifist sentiment in early Christianity, including the Sermon on the Mount's blessing of peacemakers. Religious histories are equally complex — traditions contain both peacebuilding movements and people who invoked religion to justify violence, and no tradition is internally uniform. Responsible scholarship describes these elements in context rather than reducing a tradition to any one of them.

### Just war and pacifism

Two broad traditions of moral thinking about violence appear across cultures and religious communities: just war reasoning and pacifism. Just war reasoning holds that resort to armed force can be justified under certain conditions — jus ad bellum — and that force, once used, should be limited in certain ways — jus in bello. Britannica's article notes that the concept is rooted in Classical Roman and biblical Hebraic culture, was developed by Christian theologians such as Augustine and Aquinas, took secular form in Grotius's work on the law of war and peace, and later shaped international law, including the Geneva Conventions. Pacifism, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy defines it, is a commitment to peace and opposition to war; pacifist traditions include early Christianity and communities such as the Mennonites and Quakers. The philosophical literature often presents just war theory as a middle path between realism and pacifism. How religious communities weigh these traditions is a question for religious ethics, which is a separate topic.

### Religion and war

Wars frequently carry religious dimensions without being reducible to them. Religious institutions and leaders have legitimated conflicts, blessed armies, and mobilized communities; the historical phrase "wars of religion" refers to the European conflicts of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, such as the Thirty Years' War, which ended with the Peace of Westphalia in 1648. Concepts of holy war appear in several traditions; in Muslim legal theory, Britannica notes, jihad is described as the only type of just war, even as scholars emphasize that the term's meanings are debated. Many national militaries also employ chaplains. The U.S. Army Chaplain Corps, for instance, describes its mission as providing religious support and advising on matters of religion, ethics, and morale, including worship services and counseling for service members. Describing these roles factually — without treating any tradition as essentially warlike or essentially peaceful — is the scholar's task.

### Peacebuilding, nonviolence, and interfaith work

Religious actors appear prominently in peace work as well as in conflict. The United States Institute of Peace describes religious ideas, actors, and institutions as resources for peacebuilding that contribute to the prevention and resolution of conflict, mediation, and reconciliation between adversaries. Nonviolence movements with religious dimensions illustrate the same point: Gandhi's satyagraha drew on the concept of ahimsa, a principle of nonviolence central to Jain teaching and influential more broadly in India. Interfaith and interreligious cooperation — dialogue, understanding, and joint action across traditions — has grown since the late twentieth century; the Harvard Pluralism Project notes that such movements respect each tradition's distinctiveness rather than creating a new faith. The study of interfaith relations is its own topic; here it matters as one strand of religious peacebuilding.

### Martyrdom, sacrifice, and studying responsibly

Martyrdom is a religious concept with a long and complex history. The Greek word martys originally meant "witness," and the term came to mean someone who dies rather than renounce their faith. Most major religions give special recognition to martyrdom — from the honored dead of early Christian communities and the kedoshim of Jewish tradition to the shahid in Islamic tradition — each with its own history and meaning. Scholars describe these concepts neutrally, attending to how communities themselves understand sacrifice. The same discipline governs the study of religion and violence as a whole: the American Academy of Religion's literacy guidelines distinguish descriptive from prescriptive statements, and careful scholars avoid sensationalism, proof-texting, and stereotyping. A single passage, group, or event should never be made to stand for an entire tradition.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Just war theory:** The tradition of moral thinking that some wars can be justified under certain conditions and that the conduct of war should be limited.
- **Jus ad bellum:** The justice of the decision to resort to war, including conditions such as proper authority, just cause, and last resort.
- **Jus in bello:** The justice of conduct within war, including limits on targets, methods, and the proportionality of force.
- **Pacifism:** A commitment to peace and opposition to war, held in various forms across religious and secular traditions.
- **Religious legitimation:** The use of religious authority, texts, or ideas to justify or lend moral weight to a course of action such as war.
- **Peacebuilding:** Work that prevents, resolves, or repairs violent conflict, including mediation, reconciliation, and rebuilding relationships.
- **Martyrdom:** Death or suffering endured rather than renounce one's faith, recognized and interpreted differently across traditions.
- **Interfaith dialogue:** Late-20th-century term for efforts at understanding and cooperation among people of different religious traditions.

## Eli-10

When you hear "religious violence" in the news, it sounds like one thing. Scholars treat it as a question. They ask: did religion cause this, or did it give words to people fighting about land, money, or power? Texts and traditions contain peaceful and violent elements, and communities choose which to lift up. So a careful study looks at one conflict at a time, describes the religious pieces — leaders, texts, identities, sacred places — and the other pieces too, and reports what it finds without declaring a whole tradition violent or peaceful.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a river. The same water can irrigate a valley one year and flood a town the next. The water's behavior depends on the terrain and the season as much as on the water itself. Religion is like the river: its role in a conflict depends on the political and economic landscape it flows through.

This analogy has limits: it can make religion look passive, as if it only carries whatever the terrain dictates. Religions are not passive — they have their own texts, leaders, and commitments that shape how people act, and members of the same tradition often disagree about what their tradition requires. A careful study keeps both the religion and the landscape in view.

## Worked Example

A fictional region has two farming communities with different religious traditions. Tensions rise over a river both communities use; religious leaders on each side call the dispute sacred and cite passages from their scriptures at public gatherings. A student writing a Religious Studies analysis begins by naming the elements: economic competition over water, political rivalries among regional officials, and religious identity and leadership. She then checks the category. Is this "religious violence"? She concludes that the conflict has intertwined religious, political, and economic causes, and that a responsible paper describes how each side's leaders interpret scripture and how those interpretations interact with land and power — without declaring either tradition inherently violent.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating "religious violence" as a settled category that explains a conflict.** Scholars debate the term itself; analyze the specific mix of religious, political, and economic factors in each case.
- **Proof-texting: treating a single passage as the whole message of a scripture.** Sacred texts carry multiple, sometimes conflicting passages and interpretations; describe which passages are invoked and by whom.
- **Generalizing about an entire tradition from one group or event.** Name the specific actors and note the internal diversity of every tradition.
- **Confusing academic description with endorsement or condemnation.** Descriptive study reports how communities understand violence and its limits without judging the truth of their claims.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Just war reasoning | Pacifism | Just war reasoning allows that some wars can be justified under conditions and should be limited in conduct; pacifism opposes war as a matter of principle. |
| Jus ad bellum | Jus in bello | Concerns the justice of the decision to go to war versus the justice of conduct once war is under way. |
| Description | Prescription | Reporting how a community understands violence and its limits versus declaring what its members should believe or do. |

## Key Takeaway

Religion and violence is a scholarly question, not a settled label: traditions contain both peaceful and violent elements, conflicts mix religious and other causes, and careful study describes specifics without stereotyping.
