# What Is Religion?

## In 30 Seconds

In Religious Studies, religion is a category for investigating human beliefs, practices, communities, values, and orientations to what people regard as sacred or ultimate. Scholars do not use one universally accepted definition. Instead, a definition makes its focus and boundaries visible. Some definitions ask what a tradition is about; others ask what roles it plays in personal or social life.

## Why This Matters

A definition determines what a question can notice. It can affect which practices, communities, and historical cases a researcher includes, compares, or leaves at the edge of a study. Learning to name a definition’s purpose and limits helps students read claims about religion more carefully. It also supports accurate comparison: traditions should not be judged by whether they resemble one familiar example.

## Learning Objectives

- Define religion as a contested analytical category in Religious Studies.
- Distinguish substantive and functional approaches to defining religion.
- Explain how a definition’s boundaries shape a comparison.
- Apply a stated definition cautiously to a new example.
- Analyze why a single checklist can produce difficult boundary cases.

## The College Version

### A category, not a final answer

The ordinary question “What is religion?” sounds as if it should have one short, final answer. In Religious Studies, it is more productive to begin with a different question: what work is a particular definition meant to do? A definition is a rule for grouping cases together. It highlights some features and makes other features less central. That is useful, but it means a definition is never neutral in its consequences. A definition centered on devotion to gods, for example, will sort cases differently from one centered on ritual, community, moral order, or a relationship to what participants regard as sacred.

Scholars therefore treat religion as a contested category rather than assuming that every tradition shares one necessary feature. The contest is not merely a vocabulary dispute. It concerns the evidence a scholar will examine and the comparisons a scholar can responsibly make. A narrow definition may make a study precise, while also creating exclusions that must be acknowledged. A broad definition may notice more similarities, while also risking that many unrelated activities appear to belong together. Good academic work names its working definition and remains alert to cases that strain it.

This approach does not ask students to decide whether a religion is true, false, valuable, or invalid. It asks them to describe how people, communities, texts, practices, and institutions are organized and interpreted. A student can recognize that a participant calls a place sacred without making a personal claim about the place. That distinction makes comparative study possible while respecting that traditions are lived in diverse ways.

### Substantive and functional approaches

One major family of definitions is substantive. A substantive approach identifies the kind of reality, being, or domain to which religion is oriented. Depending on the scholar, that focus may be gods, spirits, the sacred, transcendence, or an unseen order. These definitions can help distinguish religious claims from other kinds of claims, because they specify an object or orientation. They may, however, fit some traditions better than others. If a definition requires belief in a deity, it can make non-theistic traditions hard to describe even when those traditions contain disciplined practices, ethical teachings, institutions, and inherited ways of interpreting life.

A functional approach instead emphasizes what religious ideas and practices do. Researchers using this approach may examine how a tradition creates solidarity, provides meaning, marks life transitions, organizes moral commitments, or shapes identity. OpenStax’s sociological overview presents religion as both personal and social, drawing attention to beliefs, practices, norms, and group life. Functional approaches make those observable roles easier to study. Yet they can become so broad that activities not ordinarily identified as religious are included because they also create identity or cohesion.

Neither approach automatically wins. They answer different questions and create different boundary lines. Some scholars combine substantive and functional criteria; others use a family-resemblance approach, looking for overlapping features rather than a single required trait. The key skill is not memorizing a winner. It is asking what a definition includes, what it excludes, and whether those results fit the purpose of the inquiry.

### Using definitions carefully in comparison

A careful comparison begins by stating the lens. Suppose a researcher is comparing a weekly gathering, a memorial practice, and a text-centered study group. A functional lens might ask whether each activity builds community, offers a shared account of suffering, or marks a transition. A substantive lens might ask whether participants relate the activity to a sacred or transcendent reality. Neither question alone settles whether each case is religion. Instead, the answers show how the classification depends on the stated rule.

The evidence should be more specific than a label. Researchers can attend to participants’ own descriptions, rituals, narratives, material objects, ethical commitments, organizations, and historical setting. They should also avoid treating a named tradition as internally uniform. Members may disagree about beliefs, change practices over time, or describe the same activity differently. A classification can be useful while still being provisional: “under this definition, these features support comparison; under another definition, the boundary may move.”

This discipline prevents two common errors. One is essentialism: reducing a large, changing tradition to one supposed core feature. The other is overextension: calling any intense commitment a religion without explaining why. In introductory study, the most defensible conclusion is often conditional rather than absolute. State the definition, show the relevant evidence, identify a limit, and leave room for a revised account when better evidence or a different research question calls for one.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Religion:** A contested category used to study certain systems of meaning, practice, community, and orientation.
- **Definition:** A stated rule that identifies what a concept includes and excludes for a purpose.
- **Substantive approach:** An approach that emphasizes the kind of reality or object toward which a tradition is oriented.
- **Functional approach:** An approach that emphasizes the social or personal roles beliefs and practices may perform.
- **Sacred:** Set apart as specially meaningful, worthy of reverence, or connected to a religious orientation.
- **Ritual:** A patterned action performed in a meaningful setting, often with shared forms or timing.
- **Boundary case:** An example that makes a category difficult to apply because it fits some criteria but not others.
- **Family resemblance:** A classification approach that looks for overlapping features instead of one required feature.

## Eli-10

Imagine several boxes of things from different neighborhoods: a songbook, a special meal, a quiet meditation practice, a building where people gather, and a story about where the world came from. You might notice that some belong together, but no single item is in every box. Scholars face a similar problem when they use the word religion. They make a rule for a question, explain the rule, and check whether it treats different communities fairly. The rule is a tool for studying; it is not a test that tells people what they must believe.

## Eli's Analogy

A definition is like a map key. A map key tells you what the symbols mean so you can follow a route. A substantive key might mark places connected to the sacred. A functional key might mark places or practices that help a group make meaning or hold together. Different keys reveal different parts of the same landscape.

This analogy has limits: unlike map symbols, human traditions change, overlap, and are described differently by participants. A definition cannot capture every lived detail, so a researcher must explain its limits.

## Worked Example

A class compares two memorial gatherings. In the first, participants recite inherited words, light candles, and describe the gathering as an encounter with the sacred. In the second, participants share stories and support one another but do not connect the event to a sacred reality. A substantive definition may include the first more readily. A functional definition may find that both help participants make meaning and strengthen relationships. The responsible conclusion is not that one label is permanently correct. The student should state which definition is being used, identify the evidence, and note how a different definition changes the result.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating one definition as the final essence of every tradition:** State the definition’s purpose and test its boundaries against varied cases.
- **Assuming religion always means belief in a deity:** Recognize that definitions centered on deities may exclude cases that other approaches study.
- **Calling every strong group commitment a religion:** Explain the criteria and evidence instead of applying the label by intensity alone.
- **Confusing academic description with personal endorsement or criticism:** Describe participants’ claims and practices without requiring agreement with those claims.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Substantive approach | Functional approach | Focuses on what a tradition is oriented toward versus what beliefs and practices do in personal or social life. |
| Working definition | Claim of final essence | A transparent tool for a stated inquiry versus an assertion that one feature exhausts all religion. |
| Description | Endorsement | Reporting how a practice or claim is understood versus declaring that it is true or worthy. |

## Key Takeaway

Religion is a contested category, so a careful study states its definition, follows the evidence, and acknowledges what that definition includes and leaves uncertain. Substantive and functional approaches are useful lenses, not automatic verdicts.
