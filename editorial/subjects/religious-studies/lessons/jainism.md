# Jainism

## In 30 Seconds

Jainism is one of the most ancient Indian religious traditions, still practiced by millions today. Its central principle is ahimsa, nonviolence toward all living beings, because Jains hold that every creature carries a living soul. Liberation means shedding all karma by following the three jewels of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct. Ascetics and laypeople observe vows of nonviolence, truthfulness, not stealing, chastity, and non-attachment. The tradition's two main orders are the Digambara and the Svetambara.

## Why This Matters

Jainism offers a distinctive case for studying religion: an ancient tradition with no creator god at its center, built around one of the most thorough practices of nonviolence in any religious tradition. Understanding it sharpens comparative skills, because Jain karma is not identical to the karma of neighboring traditions, and the tradition's internal divisions show how one community can disagree over practice and scripture. Jain ideas of ahimsa also reached far beyond the community: Mahatma Gandhi, whose satyagraha campaigns shaped the Indian independence movement, was a famous advocate of ahimsa. With communities across India and a global diaspora, Jainism remains a living tradition whose questions about harm, restraint, and interdependence speak directly to contemporary debates about animals, the environment, and peace.

## The College Version

### Origins: an ancient Indian tradition

Jainism is one of the three most ancient Indian religious traditions still in existence, alongside Hinduism and Buddhism, and scholars treat it as an independent tradition rather than a Hindu sect or a Buddhist heresy. The name comes from the Sanskrit verb ji, "to conquer": it refers to the ascetic battle that Jain renunciants must fight against the passions to reach enlightenment. Those who achieve it are called Jinas, "Conquerors," and the tradition's followers are Jains. Scholars of religion generally hold that Jainism originated in the 7th-5th century BCE in the Ganges basin of eastern India, a region of intense religious activity at the time. Jains hold that their tradition has no historical founder. Tradition instead names a series of great teachers, the tirthankaras ("ford-makers"), who lead the way across the stream of rebirths. Parshvanatha, traditionally the 23rd tirthankara, is the first figure for whom scholars see reasonable historical evidence. The 24th and last tirthankara of the current age is Vardhamana, known as Mahavira ("Great Hero"). Tradition dates him to 599-527 BCE; scholars regard him as a close contemporary of the Buddha, and some prefer later dates. Mahavira renounced his princely life at thirty and, according to tradition, spent twelve and a half years in intense ascetic practice before teaching.

### Core teachings: ahimsa and the three jewels

Ahimsa, nonviolence or non-injury, is the most central Jain teaching and the heart of Jain ethics. Jains hold that animals, plants, and human beings all contain living souls, each of equal value, so ahimsa extends to every living being. It is more than refraining from violence: violence of thought and speech counts as much as physical harm, and so does asking, encouraging, or condoning harm done by others. The positive goal is expressed in the three jewels: right faith (samyak darshana), right knowledge (samyak jnana), and right conduct (samyak charitra). Right faith means seeing things properly, free of preconceptions; right knowledge means accurate understanding of the nature of the universe; right conduct means living according to the ethical rules so as to avoid harm and loosen attachment. Jains hold that right faith and right knowledge naturally support right conduct. Together the three jewels are the path to liberation of the soul.

### Karma, the soul, and a universe without a creator

Jain ideas of karma and the soul differ from those of neighboring traditions. The soul, jiva, is a conscious living being; the body is its temporary container. Each jiva is eternal, independent, and responsible for what it does. Karma, in Jain teaching, is not only a moral law: it is a subtle physical substance that fills the universe and is attracted to the soul through actions, words, and thoughts. Passions such as anger, pride, and greed make the soul "sticky," so karma clings to it and shapes the quality of present and future lives. Liberation, moksha, means removing all karma from the soul so that it is no longer reborn; living according to the Jain vows is how karma is burned away. Jainism does not teach that the universe was created by a god. The universe is eternal and uncreated, and karma operates without any divine being dispensing rewards or punishments. The perfected beings Jains venerate, the jinas and tirthankaras, are models to aspire to, not creators, judges, or helpers. Scholars debate labels for this framing, and some have proposed "transtheistic," since Jains do venerate perfected beings.

### Practice: the five great vows, ascetic and lay life

Jain practice centers on five great vows, the mahavratas: nonviolence (ahimsa), truthfulness (satya), not stealing (asteya), chastity (brahmacharya, sexual restraint with celibacy as the ideal), and non-attachment to possessions (aparigraha). Monks and nuns, the tradition's professional religious practitioners, follow these vows fully, living as wandering ascetics who sweep the ground before them and sometimes wear cloths over their mouths to avoid harming insects. Lay Jains follow the same principles in a limited form, the anuvratas, adapted to household life; the fuller lay code has twelve vows in all. Laypeople are expected to be vegetarian, to avoid occupations that cause harm, and to give to charity. Fasting is very common in Jain spirituality, practiced at festivals and holy days and as penance, recalling Mahavira's own asceticism. Vegetarianism is the largest practical expression of ahimsa in lay life: since all creatures carry souls, Jains do not intentionally injure any form of life above the class of one-sensed beings.

### Two orders, the Agamas, and Jainism today

Jains are divided into two major orders. The Digambara ("sky-clad") order holds that a true monk should go without clothes and without possessions; the Svetambara ("white-clad") order holds that monks and nuns should wear simple white robes. The two orders agree on the basics of the tradition but differ on details: monastic dress, the spiritual status of women (Digambaras hold that a soul cannot attain liberation from a female body), rituals, and which texts count as scripture. The Agamas, the texts containing Mahavira's teachings, compiled by his disciples and long transmitted orally, are the canonical scriptures of Svetambara Jainism; the Digambara order holds that the original scriptures were lost. Today most of the more than six million Jains live in India, and diaspora communities practice in Britain, the United States, and other English-speaking countries, where temples such as the Jain Center of Greater Boston (established in 1973) serve growing communities. Jain ideas of ahimsa have reached well beyond the community: Mahatma Gandhi was a famous advocate of ahimsa, and his satyagraha ("truth-force") policy of passive resistance drew on it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Ahimsa:** Nonviolence or non-injury toward all living beings; the most central principle of Jain ethics.
- **Tirthankara:** A "ford-maker": in Jain tradition, a great teacher who leads the way across the stream of rebirths to liberation.
- **Mahavira:** The name given to Vardhamana, traditionally the 24th and last tirthankara of the current age, meaning "Great Hero."
- **Jiva:** The conscious living soul in Jain teaching, eternal and independent, which is reborn until it achieves liberation.
- **Karma:** In Jain teaching, a subtle physical substance attracted to the soul by actions, words, and thoughts, which must be eliminated for liberation.
- **Three Jewels:** The guiding principles of Jain ethics: right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, together the path to liberation.
- **Mahavratas:** The five great vows of Jain practice: nonviolence, truthfulness, not stealing, chastity, and non-attachment.
- **Digambara:** One of the two main Jain orders, meaning "sky-clad"; its monks go without clothes and without possessions.
- **Svetambara:** One of the two main Jain orders, meaning "white-clad"; its monks and nuns wear simple white robes.
- **Agamas:** The texts containing the teachings of Mahavira, compiled by his disciples and long transmitted orally; the canonical scriptures of Svetambara Jainism.

## Eli-10

Jainism is a tradition built around one question: how can a conscious being live without causing harm? Jains answer that every living thing, human, animal, insect, even plant, is a soul in a body, and every soul deserves the same care. So the central rule is ahimsa: do not injure, and do not even want to injure. Alongside it, Jains hold that the universe was not made by a god; it simply is, and it runs by karma, the results of actions. Your soul carries the weight of everything you have done, said, and thought. The goal of Jain life is to stop adding to that weight, and to live so carefully that the soul becomes light enough to be free.

## Eli's Analogy

Imagine a clear mirror that is your soul. Every careless word, sharp thought, or harmful act is like dust settling on it. Day by day the dust builds up, and the mirror shows less and less of what it really is. Jain teaching calls that dust karma. The whole of Jain practice, the vows, the fasting, the careful way of walking, is about two things: stop stirring up new dust, and gently clean away what has already settled. When the mirror is finally clear, the soul sees itself as it always was: pure, knowing, and free.

The mirror has limits. Karma is not ordinary dust: Jains describe it as a subtle kind of matter with its own laws, attracted by actions, words, and thoughts. And cleaning a mirror is passive, while Jain practice is demanding, requiring vows, discipline, and constant attention to other beings. Most importantly, dust is only a problem for the mirror itself, but in Jain teaching every harmful act also touches other souls, which is why nonviolence sits at the center of the whole path.

## Worked Example

A student writing about Jainism drafts: "Jainism was founded by Mahavira in the sixth century BCE." Checking her sources, she finds that Britannica reports scholars generally date the tradition's origin to the 7th-5th century BCE in the Ganges basin of eastern India, and that Jains hold their tradition has no historical founder. BBC Religions adds that Mahavira is regarded as the man who gave Jainism its present-day form, while tradition names him the 24th tirthankara, with scholars treating him as a close contemporary of the Buddha. She revises her sentence: "Scholars date Jainism's origins to the 7th-5th century BCE in eastern India. Tradition names Mahavira as the 24th and last tirthankara of the current age, though the tradition itself has no historical founder."

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling Mahavira "the founder of Jainism," as if the tradition began with him.** Tradition holds that Jainism has no historical founder; Mahavira is the most recent (24th) tirthankara, who gave the tradition its present-day form.
- **Treating Jainism as a sect of Hinduism or an offshoot of Buddhism.** Scholars describe Jainism as an independent ancient Indian tradition that shares a common cultural background and some vocabulary with its neighbors.
- **Calling Jainism "atheistic" without qualification.** Jains do not affirm a creator god, but they venerate perfected beings, the jinas and tirthankaras, as models; scholars describe this framing in different ways, and some propose "transtheistic."
- **Assuming all Jains practice identically.** The Digambara and Svetambara orders differ on monastic dress, the spiritual status of women, and which texts are scripture, and practice differs between monks, nuns, and laypeople.
- **Reducing ahimsa to vegetarianism.** Vegetarianism is one practical expression of ahimsa; the principle also extends to thought and speech, occupations, and the positive work of compassion and charity.

## Key Takeaway

Jainism is an ancient Indian tradition centered on ahimsa, nonviolence toward all living beings, in which each soul frees itself from karma through right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, on a path with no creator god at its center.
