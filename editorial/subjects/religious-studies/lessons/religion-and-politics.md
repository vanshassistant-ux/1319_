# Religion and Politics

## In 30 Seconds

Religion and politics meet in every country, but they meet differently. Some states keep religious and governmental institutions separate; others recognize an official religion; a few are governed by religious authorities or religious law; and many nations weave religious symbols into public ceremonies, a pattern scholars call civil religion. Religious freedom is a legal right with recognized limits, and religious actors — movements, lobbies, parties — take part in public life. Whether modernization shrinks religion’s public role is an open scholarly debate.

## Why This Matters

Relations between religion and politics shape law, institutions, and everyday life: what a state may display, fund, teach, or prohibit is often decided at this intersection. Because arrangements vary widely across countries, no single model explains them all. Knowing the range — separation, establishment, theocracy, civil religion — helps students read claims about church and state, religious freedom, and religious parties accurately, and recognize that questions such as the secularization debate are contested rather than settled. This foundation also prepares later work on secularism, religious authority, and religious diversity.

## Learning Objectives

- Define and distinguish separation of religion and state, established religion, theocracy, and civil religion.
- Explain religious freedom as a legal right and describe how states protect and limit it.
- Describe ways religious organizations, leaders, and movements participate in public life.
- Explain how religion can shape national identity, using general comparative examples.
- Analyze the secularization debate by attributing positions to named scholars and weighing evidence on both sides.
- Evaluate contested cases neutrally, identifying the question at issue without advocacy.

## The College Version

### A range of arrangements

Societies arrange the relationship between religion and political power in different ways, and a single country can combine them. Separation of religion and state (often called separation of church and state) is the arrangement in which the state does not establish or endorse a religion and religious bodies do not hold governmental authority. Britannica describes the underlying concept — that the religious and political powers in society are clearly distinct — and notes its legal expression in the First Amendment of the U.S. Constitution. The boundary is constantly tested: courts and publics have disagreed about religious displays in public buildings, prayer in public schools, and state support for religious organizations, and the same kind of expression can be judged acceptable or not depending on who organized it and how official it was.

At the other end of the range, an established religion is a tradition a state formally recognizes or supports: historically, several Lutheran churches in Europe became, in effect, arms of the state, and Henry VIII made the Church of England the state church of England. A theocracy goes further: government by divine guidance or by officials regarded as divinely guided, often with clergy in office and religious law as state law; examples commonly cited include Saudi Arabia, Iran, and the Vatican. Civil religion is neither establishment nor theocracy: it is the public use of religious language, symbols, and rituals in national life, which Robert Bellah in 1967 described as visible in presidential inaugurations, flag rituals, and national cemeteries.

### Religious freedom and the law

Religious freedom is not only a philosophical idea; it is a legal right. Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, a United Nations treaty, guarantees everyone freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including the freedom to adopt a religion or belief of one’s choice and to manifest it in worship, observance, practice, and teaching, alone or in community. The same article states the accepted limits: states may restrict the manifestation of religion only when the restriction is prescribed by law and necessary to protect public safety, order, health, or morals, or the fundamental rights and freedoms of others. This two-sided structure — a broad right plus a narrow, law-based limit — is typical of how modern states protect religious freedom while also regulating it. National constitutions and courts add their own rules: some states register religious organizations, some fund religious schools, some restrict practices they judge harmful — each measure raising the same question of where protection ends and limitation begins.

### Religious actors in public life

Religious communities do not appear in politics only inside constitutions; they act. Religious organizations, leaders, and movements engage in public life through social movements, advocacy, lobbying, education, and social services. OpenStax describes liberation theology, a movement that began within Latin American Catholicism in the 1950s and 1960s, as combining Christian principles with political activism aimed at reducing injustice and poverty. In the United States, religious groups have long been active in policy controversies, and courts have repeatedly ruled on how far that activity may go inside public institutions. In some democracies, parties form in association with religious communities: Christian democratic parties emerged across Europe after World War II — the German Christian Democratic Union and the Italian Christian Democratic Party among them — associated with Catholic social thought yet autonomous from church organizations, and they became major governing forces. Religious participation in politics takes many organizational forms, and none of them by itself reveals a country’s constitutional arrangement.

### Religion and national identity

Religion can also be woven into a people’s sense of nationhood. In pre-Christian civilizations, Britannica notes, people typically worshipped the gods of the state, religion being in such cases a department of the state; among the ancient Israelites, revealed law constituted the law of the people. Later, established churches tied one tradition to a country’s public identity, as with the Church of England. Modern national life continues the pattern in softer forms: civil religion research shows nations drawing on religious language and ritual in ceremonies that mark the state — inaugurations, holidays, memorials. None of this means that every member of a nation shares one religion; it means that national identity and religious identity are frequently intertwined, with the strength of the tie varying by country and over time.

### The secularization debate

One of the most contested questions in the study of religion and politics is whether modernization reduces religion’s public role. The secularization thesis holds that it does: as societies industrialize, rationalize, and diversify, religion loses social and political influence. The claim has a long pedigree — OpenStax notes that Durkheim, Weber, Marx, and Freud anticipated this decline, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy attributes predictions of religion’s disappearance or privatization to Marx, Weber, and, at one time, Peter Berger. The evidence cuts both ways. Religion remains publicly influential, shaping political culture in liberal democracies; the United States is comparatively religious among wealthy democracies; and yet the share of American adults with no religious affiliation has grown — 26 percent in a 2019 Pew survey, up from 17 percent in 2009 — while several European countries report lower observance. Scholars disagree about how to weigh these trends, and the debate remains open.

### Contested cases, studied neutrally

Several live disputes belong to this topic, and the academic task is to describe them accurately rather than to take sides. Political theorists, for example, disagree about what role religious reasons may play in justifying coercive law in pluralistic democracies: some hold that citizens should be able to offer a plausible secular rationale for the laws they support, others deny that such restraint is justified, and still others argue that religious orthodoxy and liberal democracy are fundamentally incompatible. The boundaries of separation in the United States are similarly contested, with courts and legislatures resolving particular cases. And scholars disagree about how to interpret data on religious decline and persistence. A neutral study names the positions, attributes them, and examines the evidence; it does not adjudicate which side is correct.

## Key Vocabulary

- **separation of religion and state:** An arrangement in which a state does not establish or endorse a religion and religious bodies do not hold governmental authority.
- **established religion:** A religion that a state formally recognizes or supports as its official or preferred tradition.
- **theocracy:** A form of government by divine guidance or by officials regarded as divinely guided, often with clergy in office and religious law as state law.
- **civil religion:** A concept for the public use of religious language, symbols, and rituals in national life; Robert Bellah described an American example in 1967.
- **religious freedom:** A legal right to hold, adopt, or change a religion or belief and to manifest it in practice, subject to limits prescribed by law.
- **secularization thesis:** The contested scholarly claim that modernization reduces religion’s social and political influence.
- **religious party:** A political party formed in association with a religious community or a religiously informed program.
- **liberation theology:** A movement, beginning in Latin American Catholicism, that combines religious principles with political activism against injustice and poverty.

## Eli-10

Think of religion and politics as two rooms in a house. In some houses the rooms are separated by a wall: the state stays out of religion and religion stays out of government. In others, one room is officially the most important — an established religion. In a few houses, the religious room runs the whole building: that is a theocracy. And in many houses, people decorate shared spaces with religious symbols — a prayer at an inauguration, a blessing at a national ceremony — without anyone officially owning the house. That shared decoration is what scholars call civil religion. Around the world, laws protect people’s freedom to practice religion, but with limits. And scholars disagree about whether, as societies modernize, the religious room is getting smaller or just changing shape.

## Eli's Analogy

The arrangements are like ways two roommates can share a kitchen. They can keep separate shelves (separation), one roommate can own the kitchen outright (establishment), one can do all the cooking and rule the menu (theocracy), or they can share holiday decorations while each keeps their own pantry (civil religion).

The analogy has limits. Real states are more complicated than roommates: a country can combine arrangements, its constitution may say one thing while daily practice differs, and religious communities are internally diverse — no single arrangement describes what every member believes or does.

## Worked Example

A student analyzes a fictional country, Aldara. Aldara’s constitution declares no official religion and guarantees religious freedom; in practice, a religious party holds a third of the seats, clergy have campaigned for a family code based on religious law, courts recently allowed a Nativity display in a town hall while forbidding a prayer led by officials at a council meeting, and national holidays open with a moment of reflection. The student classifies each element: institutional separation (no established church), religious actors in politics (the party and the clergy’s campaign), a contested boundary (the display and prayer rulings), and civil-religious ceremony (the holiday moment). She then asks whether the controversies show religion’s decline or persistence, connecting the case to the secularization debate rather than declaring a winner.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating separation of religion and state as hostility to religion.** Separation is an arrangement about state institutions and law; a separated state can still protect religious freedom and host active religious communities.
- **Confusing the secularization thesis with secularism.** The thesis is an analytical claim about religion’s declining influence; secularism is a separate position about religion’s place in public life, covered in its own topic.
- **Using theocracy and established religion interchangeably.** Establishment is state recognition or support of one tradition; theocracy is rule by religious authorities or religious law.
- **Assuming any religious group active in politics is a religious party.** Religious actors also work through movements, lobbies, schools, and social services; parties are one organizational form among several.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Separation of religion and state / Established religion:** The state neither establishes nor endorses a religion versus formally recognizing and supporting one.
- **Theocracy / Established religion:** Religious authorities or religious law govern the state versus one tradition being officially supported while state institutions remain distinct.
- **Civil religion / Established religion:** Informal religious language and ritual in national life versus formal legal recognition of one tradition.
- **Secularization thesis / Secularism:** An analytical claim about religion’s declining public influence versus a normative position about religion’s place in public life (a sibling topic).

## Key Takeaway

Religion and politics interact through varied arrangements — separation, establishment, theocracy, and civil religion — within a framework of legally protected religious freedom, and scholars actively debate whether modernity is shrinking religion’s public role.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Which term describes government by divine guidance or by officials regarded as divinely guided, often with clergy in office and religious law as the law of the state?**  
   A. Separation of religion and state
   B. Civil religion
   C. Theocracy
   D. Established religion
   **Answer:** C. Theocracy

2. **Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights protects which rights?**  
   A. Freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including manifesting belief in worship, observance, practice, and teaching
   B. The right of a state to require citizens to belong to its official religion
   C. The right of religious groups to be exempt from all criminal law
   D. The right of legislatures to prohibit religions they judge untrue
   **Answer:** A. Freedom of thought, conscience, and religion, including manifesting belief in worship, observance, practice, and teaching

3. **Which example best illustrates what scholars mean by civil religion?**  
   A. A state declares one church the official religion of the country
   B. National inaugurations include prayers and references to God without an official church
   C. Religious courts apply religious law to family disputes
   D. A religious party wins a majority and appoints clergy to government
   **Answer:** B. National inaugurations include prayers and references to God without an official church

4. **A student claims that a country with no official religion cannot really be separated from religion because religious groups run schools, a religious party competes in elections, and courts hear cases about religious symbols in public buildings. What is the strongest analytical response?**  
   A. The country must be a theocracy because religious activity is widespread
   B. Institutional separation of state and religion can coexist with religious actors participating in public life
   C. Religious parties are unconstitutional in every democracy
   D. Courts should refuse to hear cases involving religion
   **Answer:** B. Institutional separation of state and religion can coexist with religious actors participating in public life

5. **One scholar argues that modernization will continue to shrink religion’s public role; another responds that religion remains influential and publicly visible. Which approach handles this disagreement most carefully?**  
   A. Accept the first view because early theorists predicted decline
   B. Accept the second view because the United States is religious
   C. Declare the question unanswerable because religion is private
   D. Present both positions with attributed sources and evidence, treating the secularization thesis as contested
   **Answer:** D. Present both positions with attributed sources and evidence, treating the secularization thesis as contested
