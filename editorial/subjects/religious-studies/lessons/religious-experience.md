# Religious Experience

## In 30 Seconds

In Religious Studies, religious experience is a category for experiences that individuals or communities interpret as encounters with the sacred, the divine, or ultimate reality. Scholars describe these experiences from the inside — what they mean to the people who have them — without judging whether they are true. Influential framings include William James's four marks of mystical experience and Rudolf Otto's account of the numinous. Scholars study first-person reports, phenomenology, psychology, and contested brain research.

## Why This Matters

The experiential dimension of religion — prayer, meditation, visions, conversion, the felt presence of the sacred — is one of the main ways people live their traditions, alongside beliefs and rituals. Because these experiences are intensely personal, they are easy to misunderstand or to treat as proof that one tradition is right and another is wrong. Learning how scholars describe them, compare them, and study them carefully gives students a way to read accounts of religious experience accurately, to tell an insider's interpretation from a scholarly description, and to recognize which questions about them remain genuinely unsettled.

## Learning Objectives

- Define religious experience as a scholarly category built on how individuals and communities interpret their experiences.
- Distinguish insider interpretations from scholarly descriptions, and religious experience from mere religious feeling.
- Explain William James's four marks of mystical experience and Rudolf Otto's account of the numinous, including scholarly critiques of each.
- Compare major types of religious experience: mystical, conversion, prayer and meditative states, visions and auditions, and nature-based experiences of the sacred.
- Analyze how different research approaches — first-person accounts, phenomenology, psychology, and neuroscience — shape what scholars can claim.
- Apply the descriptive approach to a new account of religious experience without adjudicating whether the experience was genuine.

## The College Version

### A category built on interpretation

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy characterizes religious experiences generally as experiences that seem to the person having them to be of some objective reality and to have religious import. That reality may be a person, a state of affairs, a fact, or even an absence, depending on the tradition the experience belongs to. What makes an experience religious, in other words, is not a checklist of sensations but an interpretation: the individual or community takes the experience to be an encounter with the sacred, the divine, or ultimate reality. Scholars begin with that insider interpretation and describe it without deciding whether the encounter really happened. The same discipline distinguishes religious experience from religious feeling: elation during a service is not yet a religious experience unless it seems to the person to be an experience of something religiously significant.

### Classic scholarly framings: James and Otto

William James, an American psychologist and philosopher, delivered the Gifford Lectures later published as The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), and the term "religious experience" became a widely used technical phrase around his work. James treated first-person accounts — diaries, letters, testimonies — as the primary data of religion. In his lectures on mysticism he identified four marks of mystical experience: ineffable, because it defies adequate description in words; noetic, because it carries a sense of insight or knowledge; transient, because it cannot be sustained for long; and passive, because the subject feels the will is in abeyance, as if grasped by a superior power. James's criterion was itself contested: later scholars noted that making ineffability essential would rule out many experiences commonly called mystical.

Rudolf Otto, a German theologian and historian of religions, approached experience from a different angle. In The Idea of the Holy (English translation 1923) he named the felt object of religious experience the numinous — from Latin numen, "spirit" — and described it as a mysterium tremendum et fascinans: a mystery before which people both tremble and are fascinated. Otto held that the numinous could not be derived from anything other than an a priori sacred reality. Critics responded that religious experience involves more than such affective states, and that universal claims about the numinous need historical and comparative testing.

### Kinds of religious experience

Descriptive scholars commonly distinguish several types. Mystical experience involves deliberate practice — meditation, contemplation, ascetic discipline — undertaken to reach realization, unity with the divine, or direct awareness of ultimate reality; James's marks apply most naturally here. Conversion experience is the gradual or sudden process by which a divided self becomes unified around a new religious orientation, and many such experiences come unsought rather than as the result of a practice designed to produce them. Prayer and meditative states are the ordinary settings in which people report a felt connection to the divine. Visions and auditions are experiences reported in sensory terms, though subjects often insist that what they saw or heard was internal rather than bodily. Nature-based experiences find the sacred in ordinary surroundings — a sky, a flower, a mountain — where others nearby see only the scene. The types are not rigid boxes: an experience can combine marks of several, and different communities interpret similar events in different ways.

### How scholars study them

Researchers work from first-person accounts — diaries, testimonies, interview transcripts — which James treated as the central evidence. Phenomenologists of religion seek the basic structures of consciousness and meaning that appear in such accounts across traditions. Psychologists study the processes behind them; naturalistically oriented psychologists such as Freud and Leuba explained religious experience as a projection of wishes and desires, a claim other scholars rejected. More recently, neuroscience has entered the discussion: brain activity during meditative states resembles activity in certain seizures, mental disorders, and drug states, and some researchers treat those similarities as reasons to doubt that the experiences are what they seem. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes the counterargument: every experience, whatever its source, is accompanied by a corresponding brain state, so that fact alone cannot show an experience illusory. The neuroscience of religious experience is therefore a contested research area, not a settled verdict.

### The evidential debate, and why the category matters

Whether religious experience can justify religious belief is a philosophical question. Philosophers of religion disagree: some, such as Richard Swinburne and William Alston, argue that experience can provide grounds for belief, while critics argue that differences from ordinary perception undermine its evidential value. This lesson only references that debate; the philosophy-of-religion topic in the philosophy subject examines it in depth.

The category matters to the study of religion because experience is one of the main dimensions scholars track, alongside beliefs and rituals. Sociologists distinguish the experience, beliefs, and rituals of a religion, describing religious experience as the conviction or sensation of connection to the divine, often felt in prayer or meditation. Attending to experience lets researchers study religion as it is lived, compare how traditions cultivate or interpret such states, and keep description separate from verdicts about truth.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Religious experience:** An experience that an individual or community interprets as an encounter with the sacred, the divine, or ultimate reality.
- **Insider interpretation:** The meaning participants themselves give to an experience, expressed in their own terms.
- **Ineffable:** Unable to be described adequately in words; one of James's four marks of mystical experience.
- **Noetic quality:** A felt sense of insight or knowledge that seems to come from the experience itself, beyond ordinary reasoning.
- **Numinous:** Otto's term, from Latin numen (spirit), for the felt presence of the holy as a mysterious, awe-inspiring reality.
- **Mysterium tremendum et fascinans:** Otto's phrase for the holy as a mystery that inspires both trembling and fascination.
- **Conversion experience:** A gradual or sudden change in which a person's sense of self and commitments become reorganized around a religious orientation.
- **Phenomenology of religion:** A scholarly approach that describes the structures and meanings of religious experience as they appear to participants.

## Eli-10

Imagine someone says they felt the presence of God during a quiet walk, or that a vision changed their life. Scholars of religion do not argue about whether that really happened. They call it a religious experience, and they study the account itself: what the person felt, what words they chose, what their community taught them to expect, and how the experience changed how they live. The person's own understanding comes first; the scholar describes it carefully and compares it with other accounts from other traditions. The scholar's job is description and comparison, not a verdict on whether the sacred truly appeared.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a radio. Different listeners hear music, a news bulletin, or static, depending on the station. A scholar of religion is like someone who studies how listeners describe what they hear and what the broadcasts mean to them — not someone who claims the station is imaginary or proves it is real. The engineer can even study the signal without ever deciding what the music is really about.

The analogy breaks down because a radio signal can be measured directly, while a religious experience exists only through someone's report, shaped by memory, language, and community. There is no independent instrument a scholar can point to, so interpretation is always part of the evidence.

## Worked Example

A course assignment asks students to analyze a first-person account: a woman writes that during a dawn walk in the desert she suddenly felt the presence of God so strongly that she wept, and later told friends that "words cannot do it justice." A careful analysis works like this: (1) identify the type — a nature-based experience with ineffable features; (2) name the insider interpretation — she took the moment as an encounter with the divine; (3) check James's marks — ineffable, passive, and transient all fit; (4) state what scholars can claim — they can describe the report, its language, and its context, but they cannot verify the encounter; and (5) note the community dimension — how she described it afterward was shaped by the language of her tradition. The analysis describes; it does not adjudicate.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating an insider's claim as the scholar's verdict:** Report what the experience meant to the person, then describe and compare it without endorsing or denying the claim.
- **Confusing religious feelings with religious experience:** A feeling that occurs in a religious setting is not yet a religious experience; the person must take it to be an experience of something religiously significant.
- **Treating neuroscience as a settled verdict on religious experience:** Brain research on religious experience is contested; a corresponding brain state accompanies every experience and does not by itself show illusion.
- **Assuming all religious experiences are mystical experiences:** Mystical experience is one type; conversion, prayer and meditative states, visions and auditions, and nature-based experiences are others.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insider interpretation | Scholarly description | The insider reports what the experience means to them; the scholar describes the report and its context without judging the claim. |
| Mystical experience | Conversion experience | Deliberately cultivated states of unity or insight versus a gradual or sudden reorganization of the self, often unsought. |
| Religious experience | Religious feeling | An experience that seems to be of a religiously significant reality versus a bare emotion that happens to occur in a religious setting. |
| James's four marks | Otto's numinous | James catalogues the features of mystical states (ineffable, noetic, transient, passive); Otto names the felt object of encounter (mysterium tremendum et fascinans). |

## Key Takeaway

Religious experience is a scholarly category for experiences that individuals and communities interpret as encounters with the sacred, the divine, or ultimate reality; scholars describe, compare, and study them without adjudicating whether they are genuine.

## Practice Question Bank

1. According to William James in The Varieties of Religious Experience, which four marks characterize mystical experience? **Answer: B.**
2. Rudolf Otto used the phrase "mysterium tremendum et fascinans" to describe which aspect of the holy? **Answer: C.**
3. A researcher collects a diary entry in which a hiker writes that a mountain sunrise felt holy and that she stood "in the presence of something sacred," though she adds that words cannot capture it. Which scholarly move is most appropriate? **Answer: A.**
4. A neuroscientist observes that brain activity during a reported religious experience resembles activity during certain seizures. Which conclusion is best supported by the scholarly discussion? **Answer: D.**
