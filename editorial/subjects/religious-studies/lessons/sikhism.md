# Sikhism

## In 30 Seconds

Sikhism is a monotheistic religion founded in the Punjab region of South Asia in the late 15th century by Guru Nanak (1469-1539). Sikhs revere a line of ten human Gurus and, since 1708, the Guru Granth Sahib, the scripture regarded as the final, permanent Guru. Core teachings centre on one God, the equality of all people, seva (selfless service), and nam simran (remembering God). Sikhs worship together in the gurdwara, share the free langar meal, and sing hymns in kirtan.

## Why This Matters

Sikhism is one of the world's major religions, with tens of millions of followers, most of them in India but with significant diaspora communities in the United Kingdom, North America, and elsewhere. Because Sikhism developed in a region shaped by Hindu and Muslim traditions, students often misdescribe it as a blend of the two; learning to describe it as the distinct tradition Sikhs and scholars say it is teaches careful, source-based comparison. The tradition also offers clear case studies in egalitarian practice, communal worship, and how a scripture can function as a community's living authority.

## The College Version

### Origins: Guru Nanak and the line of ten Gurus

Sikhism began in the Punjab, a region of South Asia that today lies partly in India and partly in Pakistan. It emerged in the late 15th century, in an area where the main religious traditions were Hinduism and Islam. The founder was Guru Nanak (1469-1539). BBC Religions reports that he began teaching a faith "quite distinct from Hinduism and Islam," and that while his ideas drew on Hindu and Islamic thought, they were "far more than just a synthesis." Sikhs and scholars describe him as an original spiritual thinker whose poetry became the basis of Sikh scripture. The word "Sikh" means "learner," and the Sikh community is called the Panth. Sikh tradition counts a line of ten human Gurus, from Guru Nanak to Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708). The fifth Guru, Arjan, compiled the first authorized collection of Sikh scripture, the Adi Granth, in 1604, and made Amritsar the centre of the Sikh world; he was executed in 1606, and the ninth Guru, Tegh Bahadur, in 1675. In 1699 Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa. He was the last human Guru.

### Core teachings: one God, equality, seva, and nam simran

Sikh teaching centres on one God, described as without form or gender. Everyone is equal before God, and a good life is lived honestly as part of a community. Equality is a defining theme: Sikh tradition records that Guru Nanak refused the sacred thread that marked caste status, arguing that people should be distinguished by their deeds and qualities rather than by birth. Gurdwaras welcome members of all castes equally, and congregational worship may be led by any competent Sikh, male or female. Two practices express the tradition's orientation. Seva is selfless service to others, a core ideal. Nam simran is remembering and meditating on God's name, keeping God in heart and mind. Sikhs also share with other South Asian traditions the ideas of karma and a cycle of rebirth, from which liberation (mukti) comes through God's grace rather than human effort.

### The Khalsa and the Five Ks

In 1699, on the festival of Vaisakhi, Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa ("the Pure"): a community of initiated men and women who adopted a visible identity and trained to defend their faith. Initiated Sikhs, called Amritdhari, have undergone the Amrit ceremony of initiation and wear the Five Ks, five articles of faith whose Punjabi names begin with "k": kesh, uncut hair, a sign of accepting God's creation; kara, a steel bracelet, a reminder of restraint and of connection to the Guru; kanga, a wooden comb, for cleanliness; kaccha, cotton undergarments, for modesty; and kirpan, a steel sword, symbolizing the duty to protect. BBC Religions notes that the Five Ks make the Khalsa visible and bind the community together. The Khalsa remains the community of initiated Sikhs within the wider Sikh community.

### Worship and community: the gurdwara, langar, and kirtan

Sikhs worship together in the gurdwara, a Punjabi word meaning "the residence of the Guru" or "the door that leads to the Guru." The Guru is not a person but the Guru Granth Sahib, and its presence is what makes a building a gurdwara. Gurdwaras contain no idols or statues, because Sikhs worship one God without physical form. Anyone, of any faith, may visit and is welcome. Every gurdwara has a langar, a free kitchen where simple food is served to all visitors without charge, whatever their caste or faith; the word also names the communal meal itself. Kirtan, the singing of hymns from the Guru Granth Sahib, is an essential part of Sikh worship. The most prominent gurdwara is the Golden Temple (Harmandir Sahib) at Amritsar, widely regarded as Sikhism's pre-eminent spiritual site.

### The Guru Granth Sahib and Sikhs today

The Guru Granth Sahib is the scripture Sikhs regard as the final, permanent Guru. Compiled by Guru Arjan in 1604, it holds around six thousand hymns composed by the Sikh Gurus and by other saints. When Guru Gobind Singh died in 1708, the line of human Gurus ended, and Sikhs have treated the Guru Granth Sahib as the living Guru ever since; this lesson describes the book in general terms and never quotes from it. Sikhism today is a global tradition with tens of millions of followers, most of them in the Indian Punjab. Diaspora communities are substantial: BBC Religions recorded about 336,000 Sikhs in the United Kingdom at the 2001 census and roughly 200 gurdwaras there, and the Pluralism Project documents an active Sikh community in the United States. Scholars and Sikhs describe Sikhism as a distinct tradition with its own scripture, community, and identity, not a blend of Hinduism and Islam. Britannica notes that many Western scholars argue that in its earliest stage Sikhism was a movement within the Hindu tradition; that is an open historical debate, and this lesson attributes it rather than resolving it.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Guru:** A spiritual teacher or guide; the title Sikhs use for their ten human teachers and, since 1708, for the Guru Granth Sahib.
- **Guru Granth Sahib:** The Sikh scripture, a collection of hymns compiled in 1604, regarded by Sikhs as the final, permanent Guru since 1708.
- **Gurdwara:** The Sikh place of congregational worship and community gathering; literally "residence of the Guru."
- **Langar:** The free communal meal served to all visitors at a gurdwara, and the kitchen where it is prepared.
- **Kirtan:** The singing of hymns from the Guru Granth Sahib, an essential part of Sikh worship.
- **Seva:** Selfless service to others, a core Sikh ideal.
- **Nam simran:** Remembering and meditating on God's name, a daily practice Sikhs use to keep God in mind.
- **Khalsa:** The community of initiated Sikhs, created by Guru Gobind Singh in 1699.
- **Five Ks:** The five articles of faith worn by initiated Sikhs: kesh, kara, kanga, kaccha, and kirpan.
- **Panth:** The Sikh community as a whole; the Punjabi word means "path."

## Eli-10

Imagine a teacher who lived five hundred years ago in a busy region where two big religions met. Guru Nanak taught people to remember one God, to treat every person as equal, and to help others by serving them. He did not want people ranked by birth, and he did not care for empty show. After him came nine more teachers, and then a book. Sikhs believe that after the tenth teacher, Guru Gobind Singh, the book itself became the teacher: the Guru Granth Sahib still guides them today. When Sikhs gather to worship, they sit together, sing hymns, and share a free meal where everyone, rich or poor, eats the same simple food. That meal is a real lesson, because in the gurdwara everyone is equal.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of Sikhism as a house with its own foundations. The builders gathered some materials from the neighbourhood: Guru Nanak grew up where Hindu and Muslim ideas were all around him. But the house they built has its own walls, its own door, and its own address. It is not a pile of borrowed bricks, and it is not half of one house and half of another. Sikhs live in their own house, with their own book on the table and their own way of welcoming every guest to the meal.

The analogy has limits. A house is finished when it is built, while Sikhism has kept developing as a living community for five centuries. And comparing a religion to a building can make it sound rigid, when in practice Sikhs interpret and practise their faith in many ways. The point of the picture is only that a tradition can grow in a shared neighbourhood and still be fully its own.

## Worked Example

A student writing about Sikhism finds a blog post calling it "a religion that mixes Hinduism and Islam." Before using the claim, she checks BBC Religions, which says Guru Nanak's ideas drew on Hindu and Islamic thought but were "far more than just a synthesis," and that he taught a faith "quite distinct" from both. She also checks Britannica, which notes that some scholars debate whether earliest Sikhism began within the Hindu tradition. She rewrites her sentence to say that Sikhism developed in a region shaped by Hindu and Muslim traditions but is described by Sikhs and scholars as a distinct tradition with its own scripture and community.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling Sikhism a blend or synthesis of Hinduism and Islam.** Sikhism developed where Hindu and Muslim traditions met and Guru Nanak's ideas engaged with that world, but Sikhs and scholars describe the tradition as distinct and original, with its own scripture, community, and identity.
- **Confusing the ten human Gurus with the Guru Granth Sahib as a second founder.** The ten human Gurus ran from Guru Nanak (1469-1539) to Guru Gobind Singh (1666-1708); since 1708 the Guru Granth Sahib has been the sole Guru.
- **Assuming every Sikh has undergone Khalsa initiation and wears the Five Ks.** The Five Ks mark initiated Khalsa members (Amritdhari); BBC Religions describes the Khalsa as the community of initiated Sikhs, with initiation a distinct step within the wider Sikh community.
- **Imagining the gurdwara is only a prayer hall.** It is also a community centre offering food, shelter, and learning, and its langar is open to everyone, of any faith.
- **Assuming Sikh worship uses images of God.** Sikhs worship one God without form; gurdwaras contain no idols or statues, and the focus of reverence is the Guru Granth Sahib.

## Key Takeaway

Sikhism is a distinct monotheistic tradition founded by Guru Nanak in the Punjab, guided since 1708 by the Guru Granth Sahib, and centred on equality, service, and remembrance of God.
