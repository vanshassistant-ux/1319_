# Present Value

## In 30 Seconds

Present value is what a future amount of money is worth today — the working definition used in this lesson comes from OpenStax's Principles of Finance, which calls it the value today of tomorrow's cash flow. Future money is worth less than the same amount now, because money in hand today can earn a return. Discounting shrinks future amounts back to today; it is the reverse of growth. The formula is PV = FV ÷ (1 + rate)^years. Receiving $110.25 in 2 years at 5% is worth $100 today.

## Why This Matters

Money arrives at different times, and finance needs to compare amounts that arrive at different times. Present value is the tool that does it: it puts every future amount on today's scale. For savings, it tells you how much to set aside now to reach a goal later. For loans, it tells you what a future repayment is really worth today. For projects, it tells a business whether future earnings justify the cost spent now. Without present value, a dollar promised next year and a dollar promised in ten years look identical — with it, they become numbers you can weigh against each other and against the money you hold today.

## The College Version

### What present value is

OpenStax's Principles of Finance frames the concept in one phrase: present value is the value today of tomorrow's cash flow. CFI states the underlying idea from the other side: a dollar today is worth more than the same dollar promised later. Together they give the working definition used in this lesson: present value is the current worth of money promised in the future, discounted at a rate. Why is a future dollar worth less than one in hand? Because money in hand can be put to work. OpenStax's Section 7.1 puts it plainly: it is better to be paid today, or you will lose out on the money you would have earned in interest. Money you hold now can earn a return while you wait; money promised later earns nothing — and, as CFI notes, it may never arrive. So the same amount is worth more today than later, and present value says how much more. One boundary: present value concerns a single future amount — what OpenStax calls a lump sum, a one-time cash flow at one point in time. Bundles of future amounts, and their difference from an upfront cost, belong to the net present value sibling topic.

### Discounting: shrinking the future back to today

To find a present value, OpenStax's Section 7.4 says, we reverse the growth concept and discount the future value back to the current period. Growth moves money forward: $100 in an account earning 5% becomes $105 in a year. Discounting moves money backward: a $105 payment a year from now, at 5%, is worth $100 today. OpenStax states it directly: compounding takes a present valuation of money into the future, while discounting equates a future value to present dollar terms. Discounting is not a guess or a penalty; it undoes the growth the money could have earned while you waited. The longer the wait, the more growth is undone, and the smaller the present value becomes.

### The formula and the worked example

The future value formula grows an amount forward: FV = PV × (1 + rate)^years. Present value runs it in reverse — OpenStax derives PV = FV × 1 ÷ (1 + rate)^years, the same thing as dividing: PV = FV ÷ (1 + rate)^years. Original worked example: you are promised $110.25 two years from now, and you could earn 5% per year in the meantime. Two years of growth at 5% multiplies by 1.05 twice: 1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025. Dividing the future amount by that factor gives 110.25 ÷ 1.1025 = 100. So $110.25 received in 2 years at 5% is worth $100 today — the same $100 that would grow to $110.25 if invested at 5%, so the two directions check each other. OpenStax's Section 7.3 offers a check: with a positive rate, the present value is always less than the future value unless the rate is negative. If your arithmetic ever produces a present value larger than the future amount, you multiplied when you should have divided.

### The discount rate

The rate in the formula is called the discount rate, and the name explains the job. OpenStax's Section 7.4: the interest rate used to find the present value of a future cash flow is called the discount rate because it brings the money back in time — the annual rate of reduction on a future value, the inverse of the growth rate. What should you choose for it? Simply the return you could earn on money in hand over the same period — what you give up by waiting. If a savings account pays 5%, that is a natural discount rate for comparing a future payment against it. A higher discount rate shrinks future amounts more; a lower one shrinks them less. Where rates come from is the interest-rates sibling topic; here the discount rate is the tool that does the shrinking.

### Present value versus future value

Present value and future value are the two ends of one relationship. Future value is what an amount held today becomes after earning a return; present value is what money promised later is worth today. One formula produces both: multiply forward for future value, divide back for present value. $100 today at 5% has a future value of $110.25 in two years; $110.25 in two years has a present value of $100 today. Which direction you use depends on where the question starts: money you hold now points forward; money promised later points back. Future value is a sibling topic covered in its own lesson; it shows up here only as the mirror that makes present value clear.

### Why it matters

Present value exists so that money at different times can be compared — its core use. One line each. Savings: how much to set aside today for a later goal — OpenStax's Section 7.4 applies present value to college funds and retirement savings. Loans: a loan's future repayments, discounted, show what they are worth in today's dollars. Projects: businesses discount future earnings from new products, equipment, or facilities — CFI's examples — to judge whether the payoff justifies the outlay. The honest framing: present value is how finance puts different times on the same scale. It does not change the money itself — $110.25 remains $110.25 — but converts it into a number that can sit beside today's dollars in the same comparison. And the answer is only as good as the rate you choose: the discount rate, not the arithmetic, holds the real judgment.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Present value** — The current worth of money promised in the future, found by discounting the future amount at a rate. The working definition comes from OpenStax's Principles of Finance: the value today of tomorrow's cash flow.
- **Discounting** — The process of reducing a future amount back to today's terms by dividing instead of multiplying; the reverse of growth and compounding.
- **Discount rate** — The rate used to shrink a future amount back to today; in OpenStax's words, the annual rate of reduction on a future value.
- **Future value** — What an amount held today will be worth at a later date after earning a return over the wait.
- **Lump sum** — A one-time amount of cash received or paid at a single point in time, as opposed to a series of payments.
- **Compounding** — Growth that earns interest on earlier interest, so the amount grows faster each period; the process that discounting reverses.
- **Interest rate** — The percentage return money can earn over a period, expressed per year; the same number often serves as the discount rate.

## Eli-10

Present value answers one question: what is a future amount worth today? Imagine you are promised $110.25 two years from now. It will still be $110.25 on that day — the money itself does not change. But to stand it next to today's dollars, you shrink it back through the growth it could have earned while you waited. At 5% per year, $100 grows to $110.25 in two years, so $110.25 in two years is worth exactly $100 today. Growth multiplies money forward; discounting divides it back. One formula does both jobs — multiply for the future, divide for today — and the rate you divide by is the discount rate.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of looking at a lighthouse from a boat. The lighthouse does not change, but it looks smaller the farther away you are. Present value is the same money seen from today's distance: a future payment looks smaller when you view it from now, and each year of the discount rate adds distance. Future value is the view from the other end — how large today's money will look when you reach the future.

The analogy has a limit: the shrink is not an optical trick, and it is not a guess — it is arithmetic that undoes the interest the money could have earned. A lighthouse only looks smaller but is unchanged; a future $110.25 really does have less value than $110.25 today in the comparison sense, because today's dollars can grow. And the amount of shrink depends on the rate you choose, so a different discount rate gives a different present value.

## Worked Example

You are owed $110.25, but the payment arrives two years from now. You can earn 5% per year on money you hold. What is the payment worth today?

Step 1 — write the formula: PV = FV ÷ (1 + rate)^years.
Step 2 — fill in the numbers: PV = 110.25 ÷ (1 + 0.05)^2.
Step 3 — compute the growth factor: 1.05 × 1.05 = 1.1025, the amount one dollar grows to over two years at 5%.
Step 4 — divide: 110.25 ÷ 1.1025 = 100.00. The payment is worth $100 today.

Check the answer by growing it forward: $100 invested at 5% earns $5 in the first year ($105), then 5% of $105 is $5.25 in the second year, for $110.25. The round trip works, which is the whole point: you would be indifferent between $100 in hand today and the $110.25 promise in two years, because at 5% one can become the other.

## Common Mistakes

- **Multiplying when the question asks for present value** — using the future value formula (FV = PV × (1 + rate)^years) on a future amount. Ask which direction the money is moving. If the question starts with an amount today and asks what it becomes, multiply. If it starts with a future amount and asks what it is worth today, divide. Present value always divides.
- **Treating the years as a multiplier instead of an exponent** — computing 110.25 ÷ (1 + 0.05 × 2) instead of 110.25 ÷ (1.05)^2. Interest compounds year by year, so two years at 5% means 1.05 × 1.05, not 1.05 × 2. The multiplier shortcut gives $100.23 instead of $100.00 — close enough to hide the error, wrong enough to matter.
- **Assuming a higher discount rate makes the present value larger** — The discount rate is the shrink rate, the growth the money could have earned. A higher rate means more growth is undone, so the present value is smaller. At 5%, $110.25 in two years is $100 today; at 10% it is about $91.12.
- **Confusing present value with net present value** — summing a whole project's flows when only one future amount is involved. Present value converts one future amount to today's terms. Net present value — subtracting an upfront cost from the present value of a project's cash flows — is a separate tool with its own lesson.

## Compare / Contrast

| | Present value | Future value |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Today's worth of a future amount | What an amount today becomes later |
| Direction | Divides a future amount back | Multiplies today's amount forward |

Present value and future value are the two ends of one relationship, produced by the same formula run in opposite directions. Future value is a sibling topic covered in its own lesson.

| | Discounting | Compounding |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Shrinks a future amount back to today | Grows money forward, earning interest on interest |

OpenStax calls discounting the exact opposite of compounding. Compound interest is a sibling topic covered in its own lesson.

| | Discount rate | Interest rate |
|---|---|---|
| Job | The rate that shrinks a future amount back to today | The return money can earn, or the price of borrowing, per year |

The discount rate is the same kind of number as an interest rate, used for a different job; the name depends on the direction the money is moving. How rates are set belongs to the interest-rates sibling topic.

## Key Takeaway

Present value is the current worth of a future amount: divide the future amount by (1 + rate)^years, and you can compare money at different times on the same scale.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. What does present value measure?**

- A. The current worth of a future amount of money, found by discounting it at a rate
- B. The amount a deposit today will grow to after earning interest
- C. The total interest paid on a loan over its full life
- D. The face value printed on a savings bond

**Correct answer: A.** Present value is the current worth of money promised in the future, discounted at a rate — OpenStax's Principles of Finance calls it the value today of tomorrow's cash flow. The second option describes future value, and the other two describe unrelated numbers.

**2. A friend promises to pay you $121 two years from now. Using a discount rate of 10%, what is that payment worth today?**

- A. $121
- B. $110
- C. $100
- D. $91

**Correct answer: C.** The formula is PV = FV ÷ (1 + rate)^years, so 121 ÷ (1.10)^2 = 121 ÷ 1.21 = $100. The first option ignores the discounting, the second discounts for only one year (121 ÷ 1.10 ≈ $110), and the fourth applies the rate in the wrong direction.

**3. Why is discounting described as the reverse of growth?**

- A. Because it multiplies today's amount forward to a later date
- B. Because it divides a future amount back to today's terms, while growth multiplies today's amount forward
- C. Because it removes inflation from a price
- D. Because it adds interest to a loan balance

**Correct answer: B.** OpenStax's Section 7.4 says discounting lowers a future value back to the current period — the exact opposite of compounding, which carries a present value forward. The first option describes growth itself, and the last two describe unrelated processes.

**4. Using a 5% discount rate for all of these, which option is worth the MOST in today's dollars?**

- A. $100 received today
- B. $105 received one year from now
- C. $110.25 received two years from now
- D. $120 received three years from now

**Correct answer: D.** At 5%, the first three options all equal $100 today: $100 now is $100; $105 ÷ 1.05 = $100; $110.25 ÷ (1.05)^2 = $100. But $120 ÷ (1.05)^3 = $120 ÷ 1.157625 ≈ $103.66, which is more than $100. The larger far-away payment wins once it is brought back to today.

**5. A small business expects a new delivery van to bring in $5,000 of extra income one year from now. With a discount rate of 10%, what is the present value of that $5,000?**

- A. $4,545.45
- B. $5,000.00
- C. $5,500.00
- D. $4,000.00

**Correct answer: A.** PV = FV ÷ (1 + rate)^years = 5,000 ÷ 1.10 = $4,545.45, the amount the business can compare against what the van costs today. The second option forgets to discount, the third multiplies by 1.10 instead of dividing, and the fourth applies the rate incorrectly.
