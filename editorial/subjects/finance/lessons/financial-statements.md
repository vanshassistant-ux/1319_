# Financial Statements

## In 30 Seconds

Financial statements are the standardized reports that summarize a business's financial position and performance — the working definition comes from OpenStax's Principles of Accounting and CFI. Decision-makers read three together: the income statement shows profit over a period, the balance sheet shows what the business owns and owes at a point in time, and the cash flow statement shows where cash came from and went. The accounting subject owns how they are prepared; finance reads the numbers to make decisions.

## Why This Matters

Every serious money decision — lending, investing, hiring, expanding — starts with the same question: what is this business actually like? Financial statements are how anyone outside the day-to-day operations answers it. Lenders use them to decide who gets credit, investors use them to decide where money should go, and managers use them to steer the company. The numbers on these reports become the raw material of finance decisions, which is why reading them is a core finance skill. Statements describe the past in accounting terms, but the decisions built on them shape the future — and that gap is worth understanding.

## The College Version

### What financial statements are

OpenStax's Principles of Accounting frames the working definition: financial statements are the reports accountants prepare so that stakeholders — investors, creditors, employees, managers, regulators, customers, and suppliers — can understand an organization's financial performance and use it in financial decisions. CFI states the same idea in the vocabulary this lesson uses: the three financial statements summarize a company's performance, financial position, and cash generation, and together they provide a complete view. In OpenStax's image, the complete set of statements acts as an X-ray of a company's financial health. Two words in the working definition carry the weight. Standardized: the statements follow shared rules, so a reader can compare one business with another. Summarize: they compress a year of transactions into a few pages. How the statements are prepared is the accounting subject's territory, with lessons of its own; this lesson reads what the reports say.

### The three main reports

Decision-makers lean on three reports. The income statement shows revenues and expenses over a period — a month, a quarter, or a year — and ends in net income, the bottom line. It answers: how much profit did the business earn over this period? The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and owners' equity at one point in time — a snapshot — and it must balance, because assets equal liabilities plus equity. It answers: what does the business own and owe right now? The cash flow statement shows cash movements from operating, investing, and financing activities over a period. It answers: where did cash come from, and where did it go? One line for each in the finance view: the income statement is the profitability report, the balance sheet is the position report, and the cash flow statement is the cash-movement report. CFI stresses that the three are intricately linked — analysts read them as a set. Preparation belongs to the accounting subject, whose lessons cover each statement in depth; here each appears with one line and the question it answers.

### Reading across the three — one story, three angles

Here is an original example. Riverbend Bikes, a small shop, reports for the year ended December 31. The income statement shows $420,000 in sales, $374,000 in expenses, and $46,000 of net income. The balance sheet at year-end shows $210,000 of assets — $18,000 cash, $62,000 inventory, and $130,000 in equipment — against $90,000 of liabilities and $120,000 of owners' equity; the two sides balance. The cash flow statement explains the year in cash: operating activities added $34,000, buying new equipment used $45,000, and financing added $14,000 from a new loan while repaying part of an older one — a net increase of $3,000, taking cash from $15,000 to $18,000. One year, three angles. The income statement says the business earned $46,000; the balance sheet says where that leaves it at year-end; the cash flow statement shows that profit and the cash trail are different things — equipment purchases and loan repayments consume cash without touching profit. OpenStax's Principles of Finance shows the same wiring: net income flows into retained earnings on the balance sheet, and the balance sheet's cash balance ties to the cash flow statement. The three statements interlock.

### The finance view — what each reader looks for

The finance view asks what different readers look for in each statement. Lenders want to know whether the business can repay — they read the cash flow statement to see whether operations actually generate cash, and the balance sheet to see what stands behind the loan and how much debt is already there. OpenStax notes that a banker reads the statements to decide whether to lend. Investors want profitability and growth — they read the income statement to see whether earnings are rising period over period, and the balance sheet for the position behind the earnings. Managers live in all three — they use the statements to steer: what sold, what it cost, what cash the business can deploy. OpenStax describes management using this information as one tool for strategic short- and long-term decisions. One line each: lenders read for repayment, investors read for profit and growth, managers read for steering.

### Statements and decisions — and the honest framing

The numbers on the statements are the raw material of finance decisions. A lender's yes or no, an investor's buy or hold, a manager's decision to expand — each starts with the reported numbers, then adds judgment. The step after reading the statements is squeezing comparisons out of them — margins, coverage, leverage — which belongs to the sibling topic on financial ratios; here the connection is simply that ratios are built from these reports. The limits deserve an honest note. Statements describe the past, in accounting terms: the income statement covers a period that has already ended, the balance sheet is a snapshot on a date that has passed, and the cash flow statement reports cash movements that already happened. They say what occurred under accounting rules, not what will occur next. The honest framing: statements are a map of the business, not the territory — an X-ray that informs a doctor's judgment but is not the diagnosis. Read them with the same care.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Financial statements** — The standardized reports that summarize a business's financial position and performance for decision-makers to read.
- **Income statement** — The report showing revenues and expenses over a period, ending in profit or loss — how much the business earned.
- **Balance sheet** — The snapshot of what a business owns, owes, and the owners' stake at one point in time, and it must balance.
- **Cash flow statement** — The report of cash moving in and out over a period, split into operating, investing, and financing activity.
- **Financial position** — The state of a business at a moment: what it owns, what it owes, and what remains for the owners.
- **Financial performance** — How well a business did over a period, measured by the revenue it generated and the expenses it incurred.
- **Stakeholder** — Anyone affected by a company's decisions who reads its statements — investors, creditors, employees, managers, regulators, customers, and suppliers.
- **Net income** — Profit for a period: what remains after all expenses are subtracted from revenue — the bottom line.
- **Asset** — Something a business owns that has value, such as cash, inventory, or equipment, listed on the balance sheet.
- **Liability** — An amount a business owes, such as a loan or an unpaid bill, listed on the balance sheet as a claim against its assets.

## Eli-10

Financial statements are the standardized scorecards that summarize how a business is doing. There are three, and each answers one question. The income statement asks: how much profit did the business earn over this period? The balance sheet asks: what does the business own and owe at this moment? The cash flow statement asks: where did cash actually come from, and where did it go? Read together, they tell one story from three angles — performance over time, position at a moment, and the cash trail. The accounting subject owns how they are prepared; finance reads the results to make decisions.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a business as a house you might buy. The income statement is the household's budget results — money earned and spent over the year, and what was left over. The balance sheet is the walkthrough on one day: the rooms, the furniture, the mortgage still owed, and the equity built up. The cash flow statement is the bank statement showing every deposit and withdrawal and where each came from. A lender looks at whether the household can cover the mortgage; a buyer looks at whether the house is gaining value; the owners look at all three to decide what to fix next. Same house, three views.

Where the analogy breaks down: a household rarely prepares three standardized reports, while a business must, under accounting rules — and those rules are the accounting subject's territory. A house's value is guesswork until sold, while statements report numbers under specific rules that can differ from economic reality. And the reports describe the past: the bank statement is already written. Statements are a map of the business, not the territory — useful for deciding, never a guarantee of what comes next.

## Worked Example

Riverbend Bikes finished the year with a $46,000 profit on its income statement — $420,000 of sales minus $374,000 of expenses. Its balance sheet at year-end shows $210,000 of assets ($18,000 cash, $62,000 inventory, $130,000 in equipment), $90,000 of liabilities, and $120,000 of owners' equity — and the two sides balance. The cash flow statement explains the gap between profit and cash: operating activities added $34,000, buying new equipment used $45,000, and financing added $14,000 from a new loan, for a net increase of $3,000. One year, three angles: profit earned, position at year-end, and the cash trail behind both.

## Common Mistakes

- **Reading one statement and skipping the other two** — the income statement can look strong while the balance sheet and cash flow statement tell different parts of the story; the three are meant to be read together.
- **Treating the balance sheet as a period report** — it is a snapshot at one point in time, not a summary of the year; comparing snapshots from different dates is how it becomes useful.
- **Assuming profit equals cash** — a business can show a profit while its cash falls, because income is measured under accounting rules while the cash flow statement tracks actual money movement.
- **Expecting the statements to predict the future** — they describe the past in accounting terms; decisions use them as input, never as a forecast.
- **Judging a single number without context** — a $46,000 profit means little without last year's figure or the size of the business; statements gain meaning in comparison.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Income statement vs balance sheet** — The income statement covers a period and shows performance; the balance sheet is a snapshot of position at one point in time.
- **Profit vs cash** — Profit is measured under accounting rules over a period; cash flow tracks the money that actually moved, and the two can differ.
- **Statements vs the business** — Statements are the standardized, rule-based report of what happened, a map of the business rather than the business itself.

## Key Takeaway

Financial statements — the income statement, the balance sheet, and the cash flow statement — summarize a business's position and performance, and decision-makers read them together as the raw material of finance decisions. They are a map, not the territory.

## Practice Question Bank

**Q1.** Which three reports make up the core set of financial statements that decision-makers read together?

- A. Income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement
- B. Budget, forecast, and invoice log
- C. Tax return, payroll summary, and sales receipt
- D. Bank statement, ledger, and inventory count

**Key: A.** The three core statements are the income statement (performance over a period), the balance sheet (position at a point in time), and the cash flow statement (cash movements over a period).

**Q2.** A manager at Maple & Main Café wants to know how much profit the café earned over the past year. Which statement holds that answer?

- A. The cash flow statement, because it shows cash movements
- B. The balance sheet, because it shows what the café owns and owes
- C. The income statement, because it reports revenues and expenses over a period
- D. The bank reconciliation, because it matches every deposit and withdrawal

**Key: C.** Profit for a period lives on the income statement, which reports revenues and expenses and ends in net income. The balance sheet is a snapshot at a point in time, and the cash flow statement tracks cash movements.

**Q3.** Northgate Print Shop's income statement shows $52,000 of profit for the year, yet its balance sheet shows cash fell from $30,000 to $12,000. Which reading of the three statements together best explains the two facts?

- A. The income statement must be wrong, because profit and cash always move together
- B. The cash flow statement is missing, since only two statements were provided
- C. The shop must have stopped printing in the second half of the year
- D. The shop bought new equipment and repaid loan principal during the year, while profit is measured under accounting rules that differ from cash movement

**Key: D.** The three statements tell one story from three angles: profit is measured over the period under accounting rules, while cash moves with collections, equipment purchases, and loan repayments. Buying equipment and repaying debt consume cash without reducing profit, so cash can fall in a profitable year.

**Q4.** An investor is comparing two retail chains and wants the clearest read of how profitably each chain runs its day-to-day operations. Which statement gives the most direct view?

- A. The balance sheet, because it shows total assets and debt
- B. The income statement, because it reports the revenues and expenses of running the business over a period
- C. The cash flow statement, because cash is the only number that cannot be adjusted
- D. The statement of retained earnings, because it shows dividends

**Key: B.** The finance view for investors centers on profitability and growth, which the income statement shows directly — revenues and expenses over a period, ending in net income.

**Q5.** A founder reviews last year's financial statements before deciding whether to open a second location next year. Which caution applies to using those statements?

- A. The statements describe what already happened, so they inform but do not guarantee next year's results
- B. The statements are required by law to predict the coming year's sales
- C. The statements replace the need for any market research or judgment
- D. The statements are prepared by the founder, so they cannot be trusted

**Key: A.** Statements describe the past in accounting terms — a map of the business, not the territory. They inform the decision, but the future still depends on judgment, forecasts, and conditions the statements cannot see.
