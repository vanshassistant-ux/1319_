# Financial Ratios

## In 30 Seconds

Financial ratios are relationships between numbers on a company's financial statements — the working definition comes from CFI — and they exist so decision-makers can judge performance and health. Finance asks three questions with them: can the company pay its near-term bills (liquidity), is it earning enough (profitability), and how much debt does it carry (leverage)? The accounting subject owns the formulas; finance uses the answers. A ratio alone means little — it earns meaning compared over time and against peers.

## Why This Matters

Lenders, investors, and managers make real decisions on the strength of ratios: who gets credit, whose stock gets bought, which operations get fixed. Ratios shrink a year of statements into numbers that can be compared across companies of different sizes, which is why they are the common language of financial judgment. Learn them and you can follow the reasoning behind a loan decision or a stock analysis instead of taking the verdict on faith. The honest part matters too: ratios describe the past in accounting terms, so reading them well means knowing what they can and cannot promise.

## Learning Objectives

- Define financial ratios using the working definition from CFI: calculations that compare figures from a company's financial statements to measure performance and financial health.
- State the three finance questions — liquidity, profitability, and leverage — and name the family of ratios that answers each.
- Describe how lenders, investors, and managers each use ratios, one use per decision-maker.
- Explain why a single ratio means little on its own, using the comparison practice of tracking trends over time and benchmarking against peers.
- State the limits of ratios: accounting rules shape the numbers they are built from, and past results do not equal future results.
- Connect ratio analysis to valuation: ratios feed judgments of value, which the finance subject's valuation topics build on.

## The College Version

### What financial ratios are

CFI's working definition anchors this lesson: financial ratios are calculations that compare two or more figures from a company's financial statements to measure its performance and financial health. They turn raw statement numbers into relationships — profit per sales dollar, assets per liability dollar, debt per equity dollar — that mean the same thing for a corner bakery and a global manufacturer. That is the whole point of the ratio: it strips out size so the underlying relationship can be seen. Where the numbers come from and how the math works is the accounting subject's territory, with its own lessons on the current ratio, profit margin, return on equity, and debt-to-equity. This lesson is the finance view: once the ratios exist, what do decision-makers do with them?

### The three finance questions

Finance reads ratios as answers to three standing questions. Liquidity: can the company pay its near-term bills? The liquidity family — the current ratio is the classic — tests whether what the company can turn into cash quickly covers what it owes soon. Profitability: is the company earning enough? Profitability ratios such as profit margin and return on equity measure whether sales and owners' equity are producing profit. Leverage: how much debt is the company carrying? Leverage ratios such as debt-to-equity show how much of the operation is financed by borrowing rather than owners' money. Each question points at a different risk, and each family of ratios is the vocabulary for answering it. The accounting lessons show the formulas; here, the questions matter.

### Who uses the ratios

Each decision-maker reads the ratios for the question that matters to them. Lenders and creditors use them to judge creditworthiness — whether the borrower can repay — and to set the terms of a loan. Investors use them to monitor how their investment is performing and to weigh returns against risk. Managers and corporate finance teams use them to spot operational strengths and weaknesses, set performance targets, and steer decisions. The same set of numbers serves all three, because each reader brings a different question to it. A bank asking about repayment ability, a shareholder asking about return, and a manager asking about operations are not reading different reports — they are reading the same relationships through different lenses.

### Ratios over time and against peers

A ratio alone means little. A current ratio of 1.6 is not good or bad until you know what it is being compared with. The comparison practice has two directions. Over time: is the ratio improving, declining, or stable across several reporting periods? A profit margin slipping year after year tells a different story than one holding steady. Against peers: how does the company's ratio compare with competitors or the industry benchmark? Analysts rarely rely on any single ratio — combinations are tracked together, so one number is always read in context. That is why a ratio is a starting point for judgment, never the verdict itself.

### The limits

Ratios inherit the limits of the numbers they are built from. Accounting rules shape those numbers — accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash moves — so a ratio reflects the rules and choices behind the reports, not an unvarnished reality. And ratios describe what has already happened. The future is uncertain; the return may not be what anyone expected, which is what finance means by risk. A healthy-looking history does not guarantee a healthy-looking future, and a weak one does not sentence a company to failure. Read ratios as evidence about the past, and treat every forward judgment as exactly that — a judgment.

### Ratios and valuation

Ratios also feed judgments of value. When investors ask what a share of stock is worth, they lean on ratio-based comparisons: the price-to-earnings ratio, which shows the price an investor must pay for each unit of the company's earnings, is one of the most consistently used calculations in common-stock evaluation, alongside price-to-book and price-to-sales. These valuation multiples are ratios applied to the value question. The finance subject's valuation topics — stocks, and the return-and-risk pair — build on this link; this lesson only marks the connection.

### The honest framing

Ratios are the language of financial judgment. They are how lenders, investors, and managers put the numbers of a business into comparable words — a lens, not a verdict. The lens has limits: it is built on past, rule-shaped numbers, and it needs time and peer comparisons to focus. Used honestly, ratios sharpen the questions that matter: can this company pay its bills, is it earning enough, and how much debt does it carry? Those questions are the point, and the ratio is the way finance asks them.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Financial ratio** — A calculation that compares two or more figures from a company's financial statements to measure its performance and financial health.
- **Liquidity ratios** — The family of ratios that tests whether a company can cover its short-term obligations — whether it can pay its near-term bills.
- **Profitability ratios** — The family of ratios that measures profit relative to sales, assets, or owners' equity — whether the company is earning enough.
- **Leverage ratios** — The family of ratios that shows how much a company relies on debt compared with owners' equity — how much borrowing backs the business.
- **Benchmark** — A reference point for comparison, such as a competitor's ratio or an industry average, that gives a single ratio its meaning.
- **Trend analysis** — The practice of comparing a company's figures across several reporting periods to see whether performance is improving, declining, or staying stable.
- **Price-to-earnings (P/E) ratio** — A valuation ratio that shows the price an investor must pay for each unit of the company's earnings; one of the most used figures in stock evaluation.

## Eli-10

A financial ratio is a relationship between two numbers on a company's statements, made so people outside the company can judge how it is doing. Think of the three questions finance asks. Can it pay its near-term bills? That is liquidity. Is it earning enough? That is profitability. How much debt does it carry? That is leverage. The accounting subject does the math — the formulas for the classics like the current ratio and debt-to-equity live there. Finance takes the answers and compares: this year against last year, this company against its peers. The comparison is what turns a bare number into a judgment.

## Eli's Analogy

A single ratio is like a single blood-pressure reading. One number tells you little by itself. The same reading at the same time of day, month after month, shows a trend; the same reading next to other people your age shows where you stand. Doctors do not diagnose from one number, and lenders and investors do not judge a company from one ratio. The measurement matters because it is tracked and compared — that is when it starts to mean something.

Where the analogy breaks down: blood pressure has broadly accepted healthy ranges for everyone, but financial ratios have no universal normal. A current ratio that is comfortable for a grocery store with fast cash cycles can look thin for a shipbuilder whose projects take years, and what counts as heavy debt varies by industry. The comparison is always against the right peers and the company's own history — never against a one-size-fits-all number.

## Worked Example

Hollow Oak Furniture makes dining tables. A supplier is deciding whether to extend the company a 90-day trade-credit line, so she pulls the latest statements. Hollow Oak's current ratio — its short-term cushion — has fallen from 2.4 two years ago to 1.6 last year, while peer furniture makers average about 1.8; its debt-to-equity sits close to the peer average, and its profit margin has held steady. No single number settles the decision. The trend toward the peer average earns a closer look, the leverage is ordinary for the industry, and the margin says the core business still earns. The supplier decides the line is worth offering with a smaller limit and a review after the first quarter — a judgment built from comparisons, not from any one ratio.

## Common Mistakes

- **Judging a company from a single ratio, as if one number were the whole picture.** A ratio alone means little; read combinations, track them over time, and benchmark them against peers before drawing any conclusion.
- **Treating ratios as predictions of the future.** Ratios are built from the reported past; the future is uncertain, so treat every forward judgment as a judgment, not a forecast.
- **Forgetting that accounting rules shape the numbers behind the ratios.** Accrual rules and reporting choices affect the figures, so a ratio reflects the rules behind the reports — the accounting lessons cover how the numbers are built.
- **Expecting one healthy ratio to fit every industry.** There is no universal normal; compare against the company's own history and against the right peer group or industry benchmark.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Raw statement numbers vs financial ratios** — Ratios divide and relate the numbers so businesses of very different sizes can be compared on the same scale.
- **Trend comparison vs peer comparison** — Trends compare a company with its own past across periods; peers compare it with competitors or an industry benchmark.
- **The accounting view of ratios vs the finance view of ratios** — Accounting builds the ratios and owns the formulas and preparation; finance uses the results as decision tools for lending, investing, and managing.

## Key Takeaway

Financial ratios are the language of financial judgment: they turn statement numbers into comparable measurements that lenders, investors, and managers use to ask whether a business can pay its bills, earn enough, and carry its debt — but a ratio means little alone and describes the past, so the judgment is always comparative.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **What does a financial ratio compare, according to the working definition used in this lesson?**
   - A. Two or more figures from a company's financial statements
   - B. A company's stock price with its dividend payments
   - C. A company's budget with its forecast
   - D. A company's tax bill with its payroll
   - **Answer: A.** The working definition, from CFI, is that financial ratios are calculations comparing two or more figures from a company's financial statements to measure performance and financial health. The other options name comparisons that are not statement-figure relationships of the kind ratios capture.

2. **Which finance question does the liquidity family of ratios help answer?**
   - A. How much profit does the company earn on its sales?
   - B. Can the company pay its near-term bills?
   - C. How much is the company's stock worth?
   - D. How efficiently does the company use its equipment?
   - **Answer: B.** Liquidity ratios measure short-term payment ability — whether the company can cover its near-term obligations. Profitability ratios answer the earning question, valuation ratios the worth question, and efficiency ratios the asset-use question.

3. **Harborline Coffee's current ratio has fallen from 2.1 to 1.3 over two years while peer roasters average about 1.8. Which judgment is most reasonable for a lender reviewing the company?**
   - A. The falling ratio proves the company will default on its loan.
   - B. The company's profitability must have doubled.
   - C. The company's short-term cushion has thinned against its peers, so the lender should look deeper.
   - D. The ratio guarantees the loan is safe because it is still above one.
   - **Answer: C.** A ratio alone means little: the judgment comes from the trend (falling over two years) and the peer comparison (now below the peer average of about 1.8). That combination warrants a closer look; it proves neither default nor safety, and it says nothing directly about profitability.

4. **Why do investors treat the price-to-earnings ratio as useful when judging a company's value?**
   - A. It guarantees the stock price will rise.
   - B. It replaces the need to read the financial statements.
   - C. It measures how much cash the company holds.
   - D. It shows the price an investor must pay for each unit of the company's earnings.
   - **Answer: D.** The P/E ratio represents the price an investor must pay for every unit of current or future corporate earnings — one of the most consistently used calculations in common-stock evaluation. It informs judgments of value; it guarantees nothing, replaces no statement, and measures no cash balance.

5. **Why does a single financial ratio mean little on its own?**
   - A. Because ratios are only meaningful when compared over time and against peers
   - B. Because ratios are always computed incorrectly
   - C. Because lenders are the only ones allowed to read ratios
   - D. Because ratios measure cash rather than accounting profit
   - **Answer: A.** Analysts rarely rely on a single ratio; ratios are most useful when tracked over time, benchmarked against peers, and analyzed in combination. The other options are not true of ratio analysis.
