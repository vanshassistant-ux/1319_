# Cubism

## In 30 Seconds

Cubism is an early twentieth-century set of experiments in representation, developed centrally by Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque in Paris and extended by other artists. Rather than use one fixed viewpoint and deep illusionistic space, Cubist works can fracture objects into planes, combine contrasting vantage points, and insist on the flat surface. In Synthetic Cubism, pasted newspaper, wallpaper, letters, rope, and drawn forms make material facts part of the image. Look for how each work organizes evidence; do not mistake Cubism for a literal total view.

## Why This Matters

Cubism teaches that representation does not have to imitate a camera view to be meaningful. It also makes material visible: a newspaper clipping is both a printed object and part of a pictured bottle, table, or instrument. Studying its phases and limits prevents a common simplification that Cubism is merely “shapes.” The topic also calls for historical care. Early Cubist source encounters occurred in colonial-era institutions; identifying a particular source object and route of encounter is more responsible than reducing diverse African traditions to a vague influence.

## Learning Objectives

- Define Cubism as a historically specific set of experiments in representation and surface.
- Distinguish Analytic Cubism from Synthetic Cubism without treating the phases as rigid boxes.
- Explain how collage and papier collé make material and representation interact.
- Analyze multiple vantage points and shallow space as visual strategies rather than literal total views.
- Discuss early Cubist source encounters with object-specific and colonial-historical care.

## The College Version

### Cubism reorganizes representation

Cubism developed in Paris between about 1907 and 1914 through the close collaboration of Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque, then was taken up and reshaped by artists including Juan Gris, Fernand Léger, Robert and Sonia Delaunay, Jean Metzinger, Albert Gleizes, and others. It is not simply a style of geometric shapes. Cubist artists questioned inherited expectations that a painting should offer a seamless window onto a world seen from one stable position. They fractured objects into planes, shifted or combined vantage points, used shallow space, and drew attention to the support as a flat surface.

A Cubist still life may contain a bottle, glass, guitar, newspaper, table, or face, yet the object may be distributed across overlapping facets and visual cues. A viewer does not need to reassemble every part into a coherent optical illusion for the work to function. The composition may offer different kinds of information at once: a contour, a shadow, a written word, a simulated texture, an object fragment, and an actual piece of paper. “Multiple viewpoints” is therefore a useful shorthand only when it is explained. It does not mean the artist photographed the subject from every angle or produced a neutral, complete account. It names a way of organizing selected visual evidence on a surface.

Cubism also belongs to a historical setting: Parisian studios, dealers, exhibitions, printed media, material exchange, and modern urban life. A work’s date, medium, scale, maker, and exhibition history can change how its formal decisions are interpreted. Do not use the word Cubism as a universal measure of progress toward abstraction or as proof that every later abstract work comes from the same source.

### Analytic and Synthetic Cubism describe different emphases

The terms Analytic and Synthetic Cubism are useful for orientation, not rigid laws. Analytic Cubism commonly describes work from about 1910 to 1912, when Picasso and Braque broke figures and still lifes into many small overlapping facets, often using restricted browns, grays, blacks, and muted colors. The work can appear difficult to read because shallow planes, lines, letters, and fragments overlap. Yet subjects such as instruments, bottles, glasses, newspapers, cards, and faces remain important. The analysis is visual: a form is taken apart and reorganized into cues that invite comparison and reconstruction.

Synthetic Cubism, especially from 1912, added conspicuous color, larger shapes, pasted papers, words, simulated wood grain, and sometimes more naturalistic elements. The term papier collé means pasted paper. In a Cubist papier collé, a printed news fragment can be both actual newspaper and a shape that helps construct a depicted object. Wallpaper with fake wood grain can be real printed paper while suggesting a violin’s material. Drawing may continue across a pasted edge, making the viewer aware of both the paper’s flatness and the represented form. These are not tricks that cancel meaning; they invite multiple readings of material, word, image, surface, and depth.

The phases overlap and individual artists used them differently. Rather than label a work and stop, identify its visible evidence: Is it oil paint, charcoal, printed paper, wallpaper, rope, or another material? Are the forms divided into small facets or built from larger cut shapes? Does a word function as text, visual form, or both? What does actual texture do beside illusionistic drawing? Those questions make Cubism a method of close looking rather than a checklist.

### History, source material, and evidence limits

Early Cubism has been connected to artists’ encounters with African art, including objects displayed in Parisian ethnographic institutions during a period of colonial extraction and unequal power. This history requires precision. African art is not a single style, and a modern European artist’s encounter does not erase an object’s maker, community, function, or collection history. When evidence permits, name the particular source object or tradition, the place of encounter, and the colonial conditions that structured access. Avoid repeating colonial catchall language as though it were a neutral description.

A responsible interpretation separates kinds of claims. A viewer can observe collaged newspaper, multiple diagonal planes, a word, or a shallow composition. A dated exhibition catalogue can support a claim that a work appeared in a Cubist context. An object record may establish medium and dimensions. A letter, review, or scholarly source may bear on a maker’s stated interest or a source encounter. The visible planes alone cannot prove a private intention, a whole political position, or every viewer’s experience.

Compare two Cubist works by asking how their materials and representational strategies differ. A near-monochrome Analytic painting and a Synthetic papier collé may both disrupt traditional spatial illusion, but one may invite attention through interlocking facets while the other stages contact between actual paper, printed language, and drawn form. That comparison explains choices rather than ranking one work as more Cubist or more advanced.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Cubism:** An early twentieth-century set of artistic experiments that reorganized objects, space, and surface through fractured forms, selected viewpoints, and new material strategies.
- **Analytic Cubism:** A term commonly used for Cubist works of about 1910–12 that break forms into small overlapping facets, often with restricted color.
- **Synthetic Cubism:** A term commonly used for Cubist work from about 1912 that combines larger forms, color, collage, paper, text, and varied representational strategies.
- **Collage:** A composition made by combining materials such as paper, fabric, printed matter, or found elements on a support.
- **Papier collé:** A form of collage made with pasted paper, often used in Cubism to make paper’s material and graphic properties part of the composition.
- **Shallow space:** A pictorial organization in which forms appear close to the surface rather than receding deeply into an illusionistic distance.
- **Vantage point:** A position from which a subject is viewed; Cubist works can combine or contrast selected vantage points in one composition.

## Eli-10

Cubism is like explaining a bicycle by showing its handlebars from one side, its wheels from another, and its name printed on a real piece of paper. You would not get one photograph-like picture, but you would get several clues about the bicycle at once. Cubist artists used lines, planes, words, colors, and sometimes actual paper to give those clues.

A paper cut from a newspaper is not pretending to be paper—it really is paper. But in a collage it can also help make a bottle, a guitar, or a table. That double job makes you notice both the object in the picture and the materials used to build the picture.

## Eli's Analogy

It is like a tabletop where someone has placed a menu, a pencil sketch, a wood sample, and a map of the same café. Each item shows something different, and together they make you think about the place without giving one window view.

The analogy has limits. Cubist images are not instruction sheets and do not provide every view of an object. The analogy also cannot explain a work’s individual history, the artist’s sources, or the colonial conditions that shaped some early encounters.

## Worked Example

A class studies a 1912 hypothetical papier collé. A strip of printed newspaper curves into the outline of a bottle; faux-wood wallpaper touches a charcoal drawing of a violin; and the word JOURNAL remains visible. The class first identifies actual materials: paper, print, charcoal, and pasted edges. It can then infer that the work asks the viewer to shift between literal paper and represented objects, flat surface and suggested depth. It should not claim that the artist showed every possible viewpoint of the still life, or that the newspaper automatically supplies a political message. A dated object record or exhibition source would be needed for claims about the work’s maker, audience, or historical use.

## Common Mistakes

- **Calling Cubism a style of random geometric shapes.** Identify the object cues, planes, surface, materials, and representational questions that the composition organizes.
- **Assuming multiple viewpoints give a literal total view.** Treat them as selected visual strategies, not a neutral or complete record of every angle.
- **Treating Analytic and Synthetic as rigid boxes.** Use the terms as helpful descriptors, then explain the particular work’s date, materials, facets, collage, color, and forms.
- **Ignoring actual pasted materials in collage.** Describe what the paper, print, texture, and edges are physically doing as well as what they may depict.
- **Reducing diverse African traditions to a vague influence.** Name specific source objects or traditions and the colonial routes of encounter when the evidence permits.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Analytic Cubism / Synthetic Cubism:** Analytic Cubism commonly uses small overlapping facets and restricted color; Synthetic Cubism often combines larger forms, color, collage, paper, text, and varied representational strategies.
- **Actual paper / Represented paper or wood:** Actual pasted paper is a physical material on the support; drawn or printed marks can represent a material, and Cubist works can make both roles interact.
- **Multiple vantage points / Complete visual record:** Multiple vantage points organize selected visual cues; they do not supply every angle or an objective total account of a subject.

## Key Takeaway

Cubism reorganizes representation through planes, selected vantage points, shallow space, and—especially in Synthetic Cubism—actual materials such as pasted paper. Analyze what a work physically contains and how it organizes visual clues, then make historically precise claims about its contexts and sources.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **How do planes, selected vantage points, and surface distinguish Cubist representation from a photographic view?**
   - A. A single photographic method for showing every side of an object.
   - B. A historically specific set of experiments that reorganized objects, space, and surface through selected visual strategies.
   - C. Any painting that uses squares.
   - D. A universal end point for all modern art.
   - **Answer:** B. Cubism is a specific historical set of experiments.

2. **What is papier collé?**
   - A. A method of carving a woodblock.
   - B. A perspective system with one vanishing point.
   - C. A form of collage made with pasted paper.
   - D. A type of monochrome oil paint.
   - **Answer:** C. Papier collé uses pasted paper.

3. **A composition combines near-monochrome overlapping facets with small cues of a bottle and violin. Which label is most useful as an initial description?**
   - A. Analytic Cubism, provided the date and other evidence also fit.
   - B. Synthetic Cubism, because every still life is a collage.
   - C. Renaissance perspective, because it depicts objects.
   - D. A complete visual record of the table.
   - **Answer:** A. It fits Analytic Cubism’s facets and restricted color, pending context.

4. **Why can a pasted newspaper fragment be especially significant in a Synthetic Cubist work?**
   - A. It erases the work’s material surface.
   - B. It proves the work is purely abstract.
   - C. It has no relation to representation because it is real paper.
   - D. It can operate both as actual printed material and as a visual element that helps construct a depicted object or space.
   - **Answer:** D. Collage makes material and depiction interact.

5. **Which research practice is most responsible when discussing early Cubism’s encounters with African art?**
   - A. Treat African art as one undifferentiated influence.
   - B. Identify specific objects or traditions, routes of encounter, and colonial conditions when evidence permits.
   - C. Assume a Cubist artist’s encounter explains the original work’s meaning.
   - D. Avoid all historical context and discuss only planes.
   - **Answer:** B. It preserves distinct histories and attends to unequal conditions.
