# Motivational Interviewing Basics

## In 30 Seconds

Motivational interviewing is a defined way of talking with people about change. It is not a synonym for being warm, and it is not persuasion with better manners. It has a stated spirit, four recurring tasks, a core skill set, and one distinguishing target: the person's own language in favor of change. It also has a real but uneven evidence base. This lesson covers what MI is, what it is not, and what the trials actually found.

## Why This Matters

MI turns up in social work, nursing, probation, health coaching, and school programs, so you will meet it in coursework, in field placement, and in job postings, usually described loosely enough to be unrecognizable. Knowing the actual method lets you tell MI from general encouragement, read a fidelity score, and judge whether an effectiveness claim holds in the domain being discussed. It also protects the people you work with: MI's own literature treats maneuvering someone toward a decision you have already made as a misuse rather than an advanced application. And because the fourth edition changed several terms in 2023, you need to read older manuals and syllabi with the edition in mind.

## Learning Objectives

- Define motivational interviewing using its current fourth-edition framing and distinguish it from warmth, rapport, and persuasion.
- Explain the four elements of MI spirit and the four tasks, including why the tasks recur rather than run in sequence.
- Distinguish change talk from sustain talk and explain how practitioner behavior measurably shifts the balance between them.
- Analyze the reported evidence for MI, including effect sizes, decay at follow-up, and variation by practitioner, fidelity, and domain.
- Evaluate a described use of MI against the method's own limits, including the misuse of MI to secure a predetermined outcome.

## The College Version

### What motivational interviewing actually is

Motivational interviewing is a specific conversational method developed by William R. Miller and Stephen Rollnick and set out across four editions of their book, most recently in 2023. That edition defines MI as a particular way of talking with people about change and growth in order to strengthen their own motivation and commitment. Each part of that sentence carries weight. *A particular way* means MI has describable features that can be observed, coded, and taught, so a claim that a program uses MI is a checkable claim rather than a mood. *Their own motivation* means the motivation being strengthened belongs to the person, not to the practitioner, the agency, or the court. And *change and growth* marks a deliberate widening in 2023: Miller and Rollnick broadened the focus, and the book's subtitle, to helping people change and grow, on the grounds that MI is used to accompany people over longer spans by teachers, mentors, coaches, leaders, and supervisors, not only in clinical work.

Two things MI is not: it is not the working alliance itself, which **Helping Relationships** covers, and it is not the general micro-skill set, which **Active Listening** covers. MI assumes both and adds something to them.

### The spirit, and what the fourth edition changed

MI is described as resting on an underlying spirit, without which the skills are hollow. Through the third edition (2013) that spirit had four elements, often taught with the acronym PACE: partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation. SAMHSA's TIP 35 unpacks acceptance further into absolute worth, accurate empathy, autonomy support, and affirmation.

In the fourth edition Miller and Rollnick broadened the evocation element into **empowerment**, in order to emphasize people's own strengths, motivations, resourcefulness, and autonomy; partnership, acceptance, and compassion are unchanged. This matters for reading. Much of the material a student meets, including TIP 35 itself, is written against the third edition and will say evocation. Both terms name the same core commitment, that motivation is drawn out of the person rather than installed by the practitioner, but the newer term deliberately widens the emphasis toward what the person already has.

### Four tasks that recur, not four steps

MI is organized around engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning. The third edition called these *processes*; the fourth calls them *tasks*, part of a general move toward plainer language. They are not stages. TIP 35 describes the shift from a linear, rigid model of change to a circular, fluid one: you return to engaging when the relationship frays, refocus when the agenda turns out to be someone else's, and drop back from planning to evoking when a commitment thins out.

Engaging establishes a working relationship. Focusing settles what the conversation is actually about, which is not always the referral reason. Evoking draws out the person's own reasons and confidence. Planning turns those into steps, and only when the person is ready. Evoking is the task that most distinguishes MI from general person-centered work: TIP 35 describes MI as more directive than purely person-centered counseling because it also guides toward a change goal the client has named.

### OARS, and why reflective listening is not enough

The core skills are OARS: open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summaries. **Active Listening** treats these as general helping skills, and MI does not replace that account. What makes them motivational interviewing is what they are aimed at. A practitioner using OARS with no attention to the direction of the person's talk is listening well, which is worth doing, but is not yet doing MI.

The fourth edition adds a distinction between simple and complex affirmations, partly to separate affirmation from praise, and renames the elicit-provide-elicit structure for offering information to **ask-offer-ask**. Information and advice are not forbidden in MI; giving them without permission or in a way meant to convince is coded as persuasion, a distinct practitioner behavior rather than an MI skill.

### Change talk, sustain talk, and the practitioner's share

Change talk is the person's own speech in favor of change; sustain talk is their speech in favor of the status quo. Both are normal, and both are sides of the same ambivalence. Change talk is conventionally sorted with DARN-CAT: desire, ability, reasons, need, then commitment, activation, and taking steps. The third edition retired the older idea of client *resistance* and split it into sustain talk and discord, discord being a strain in the working relationship rather than a property of the person; the fourth edition keeps that split.

The strongest reason to take this seriously is measured rather than theoretical. A meta-analysis of MI process by Magill and colleagues (2018; 36 primary studies, N = 3,025) found that MI-consistent practitioner skills correlated with more client change talk (r = .55) and also with more sustain talk (r = .40), that MI-inconsistent skills correlated with more sustain talk but not change talk, and that a higher proportion of change talk was associated with reduced risk behavior at follow-up (r = -.16). Tested independently, sustain talk predicted worse outcomes (r = .19) more reliably than change talk predicted better ones. The practical reading is a ratio, not a target: what a practitioner does changes the mix of what they hear back.

### Ambivalence and the righting reflex

MI treats ambivalence as an ordinary feature of change rather than as denial, resistance, or a symptom. TIP 35 states this directly and adds the consequence: a practitioner who reads ambivalence as denial tends to produce discord.

The **righting reflex**, which the fourth edition renames the **fixing reflex**, is the trained impulse to correct a problem you can see by arguing for the obvious solution. It reliably backfires, because in an ambivalent person the two sides are already both present. If the practitioner voices one side, the person is left to voice the other, and having argued against change out loud they are measurably less likely to move toward it. TIP 35 names related traps: the expert trap, the labeling trap, the question-and-answer trap, premature focus, and blaming.

### What the trials actually show

MI's evidence base is real, concentrated, and frequently overstated.

- Hettema, Steele and Miller (2005) pooled 72 trials and found an average short-term between-group effect of 0.77 that fell to 0.30 at follow-ups out to a year, and described effectiveness as highly variable across providers, populations, problems, and settings.
- The Cochrane review of MI for substance abuse (Smedslund et al., 2011; 59 studies, 13,342 participants) found effects against no-treatment control strongest immediately after the intervention (SMD 0.79) and progressively weaker at short (0.17) and medium (0.15) follow-up, with no significant effect at long follow-up, and found no significant difference between MI and treatment as usual.
- In medical settings, Lundahl and colleagues (2013; 48 trials, 9,618 participants) reported a modest advantage, an odds ratio of 1.55.
- The broadest appraisal, a systematic review of reviews by Frost and colleagues (2018) covering 104 reviews and 155 meta-analytic comparisons, found no high-quality evidence at all, graded 128 of 155 comparisons as low or very low quality, and found moderate-quality evidence of small, mainly short-term benefits in 11 comparisons, about 7%.

Honest summary: substantial support in substance use and brief health-behavior work, typically modest effects, common decay, no reliable superiority over usual treatment, and thin evidence in many places MI is now sold.

### Fidelity, practitioners, and why results scatter

Part of that variability is measurement. Whether a study delivered MI is assessed with the Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity code (MITI), which in version 4 rates both relational and technical elements from recorded sessions. Its developers reported good to excellent reliability except for two measures, emphasize autonomy and percent complex reflections, and noted that reliability falls with fewer raters and smaller samples. An independent study reported a mean intraclass correlation of 0.78 across multiply rated sessions, but only fair reliability for the rare codes and for softening sustain talk, which is precisely where MI theory locates the action.

Practitioner skill is also perishable: a meta-analysis of 21 training studies found workshop gains of d = 0.76, erosion of d = -0.30 over six months without follow-up coaching, and maintenance when three to four feedback or coaching sessions were provided. So two studies both labelled MI may not describe the same behavior, and a program that trained staff once several years ago may no longer be delivering it.

### Limits and misuse

Three limits belong with the method.

1. MI is collaborative by definition, so using it to steer someone toward an outcome you settled on in advance is a misuse, not a sophisticated application; the tell is that you would not accept the person's own conclusion if it differed from yours.
2. MI is a conversational method and does not substitute for treatment, for a safety response, or for material resources. A person who needs housing, medication, detoxification, or protection needs those things; a well-conducted conversation about motivation is not a replacement, and MI's own trials compare it with and alongside treatment rather than instead of it.
3. MI is not established everywhere it is marketed, so a claim that it works should be met with a question about which behavior, which population, and at what follow-up.

This lesson is educational material about a practice method. It is not clinical, diagnostic, or treatment advice, and nothing here should be used to direct the care of a specific person.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Motivational interviewing (MI)** - A defined counseling method in which a practitioner uses a collaborative, autonomy-respecting conversation to strengthen a person's own motivation and commitment to a change they have named.
- **MI spirit** - The underlying stance the method requires, stated in the fourth edition as partnership, acceptance, compassion, and empowerment, and in the third edition as partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation.
- **Four tasks** - Engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning: the recurring activities that organize an MI conversation, called processes before the 2023 edition and returned to as needed rather than completed in order.
- **OARS** - The core skill set of open questions, affirmations, reflective listening, and summaries, used throughout the method but not by itself sufficient to make a conversation MI.
- **Change talk** - A person's own statements favoring a change, conventionally sorted as desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment, activation, and taking steps (DARN-CAT).
- **Sustain talk** - A person's own statements favoring the status quo, understood as one ordinary side of ambivalence rather than as a personality flaw or a symptom.
- **Discord** - Strain, argument, or disengagement in the working relationship itself, treated as a signal about the practitioner's current approach rather than as a trait of the person.
- **Righting reflex** - The trained impulse to fix a problem by arguing for the solution, which tends to provoke the other side of a person's ambivalence; the fourth edition calls this the fixing reflex.
- **Ambivalence** - Simultaneously wanting and not wanting a change, treated in this method as a normal feature of the change process rather than as denial or resistance.
- **MITI** - The Motivational Interviewing Treatment Integrity code, an observational instrument that rates recorded sessions on relational and technical dimensions to judge whether the method was actually delivered.

## Eli-10

People who are stuck usually already know both sides of their own argument. They can tell you why they should change and why they would rather not, often in the same breath. Motivational interviewing takes that seriously. Instead of supplying the reasons for change, the practitioner asks and listens in a way that gets the person to say their own reasons out loud, and then explores those. There is real evidence behind this: when researchers code recorded sessions, what the practitioner does measurably changes how much the person talks in favor of change versus in favor of staying put. It is not a trick and it is not a magic script. It is a disciplined way of not taking over someone's decision, combined with paying close attention to one specific thing in what they say.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a tug-of-war. If you pull hard on the rope shouting that the person should change, they have to pull back to stay standing, and now they are the one arguing for staying the same. MI is the choice to drop your end of the rope and walk around to their side, so you are both looking at the same problem instead of at each other.

**Where the analogy breaks down:** Dropping the rope sounds passive, and MI is not passive or neutral: it deliberately leans toward one side of the person's own ambivalence, which is what separates it from simply listening well. And a tug-of-war is one moment, while MI is a repeated pattern of behavior that gets measured across whole sessions. Some situations also need action, not a conversation.

## Worked Example

A field-education seminar codes a two-minute training recording.

In segment one the worker says: "You know that missing appointments puts your case at risk, right?" The client answers: "I have a job. They do not exactly hand out afternoons off." The class marks the worker's turn as persuasion and the client's reply as sustain talk.

In segment two the worker instead says: "You have kept the last three appointments even with the job. What made those work?" The client answers: "I swapped a shift. If I had a week's notice I could do that again." The class marks an open question and an affirmation, and marks the reply as change talk in the ability and commitment range.

Neither segment proves anything about this client. What the exercise demonstrates is the meta-analytic finding in miniature: the practitioner's behavior changed which side of the client's ambivalence got spoken aloud, and it is that ratio, not the worker's sincerity, that MI fidelity coding looks at.

## Common Mistakes

- **Using MI to move someone toward an outcome the agency, the court, or the worker has already chosen.** The method is collaborative by definition, so this is a misuse rather than an advanced application. The test is whether you would accept the person's own conclusion if it differed from yours. Trying to convince someone is coded as persuasion, which is a distinct practitioner behavior, not an MI skill.
- **Treating warm, skilled reflective listening as sufficient to make a conversation MI.** OARS is necessary but not sufficient. What distinguishes MI is strategic attention to the direction of the person's own talk, which is why fidelity instruments rate technical elements alongside relational ones. Listening well without that is good practice under a different name.
- **Reading ambivalence, hesitation, or sustain talk as denial, resistance, or a lack of insight.** Ambivalence is a normal feature of change, and federal practice guidance states that interpreting it as denial tends to produce discord. Sustain talk is one side of a person's real position, and discord is information about the conversation rather than a property of the person.
- **Citing MI's evidence base as if it established the method everywhere it is now used.** Trial support is concentrated in substance use and brief health-behavior work, effects are typically modest, they often shrink by longer follow-up, and MI has not reliably outperformed usual treatment. A review of 155 meta-analytic comparisons found moderate-quality evidence for small mainly short-term effects in about 7% of them and no high-quality evidence at all.
- **Assuming the terminology in a course reading or agency manual is current.** The 2023 fourth edition renamed several third-edition terms: processes became tasks, evocation in the spirit was broadened to empowerment, the righting reflex became the fixing reflex, and elicit-provide-elicit became ask-offer-ask. Widely used sources, including SAMHSA's 2019 TIP 35, are written against the third edition.

## Compare / Contrast

| A | B | Distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Motivational interviewing | Persuasion or advice-giving | MI evokes the person's own arguments for change and treats the decision as theirs. Persuasion supplies the practitioner's arguments; in MI fidelity coding it is a separate, named behavior, and is only counted differently when permission has been sought or autonomy explicitly supported. |
| Change talk | Sustain talk | Both are the person's own speech and both are normal in ambivalence. Process meta-analysis finds the proportion of change talk relative to total motivational talk predicts reduced risk behavior, while sustain talk predicts worse outcomes more consistently than change talk predicts better ones. |
| Discord | Sustain talk | Sustain talk is about the change itself, for example why quitting would be hard. Discord is about the relationship, for example arguing with or dismissing the practitioner. The third edition split the older concept of resistance into these two, and the fourth edition keeps the split. |
| Fourth-edition terminology (2023) | Third-edition terminology (2013) | Tasks replace processes, empowerment broadens evocation in the spirit, the fixing reflex replaces the righting reflex, and ask-offer-ask replaces elicit-provide-elicit. OARS, DARN-CAT, guiding, and the sustain-talk-and-discord split are unchanged. |

## Key Takeaway

Motivational interviewing is a specified, observable method built on partnership and on strategic attention to a person's own change talk, not a style of being agreeable; its trial support is genuine but modest, uneven across domains, and dependent on fidelity that erodes without ongoing coaching.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. Which four elements does the fourth edition (2023) of Miller and Rollnick's *Motivational Interviewing* name as the spirit of MI?** *(recall)*

- A. Partnership, acceptance, compassion, and empowerment
- B. Partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation
- C. Empathy, discrepancy, resistance, and self-efficacy
- D. Engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning

**Answer: A.** The fourth edition broadened the earlier evocation element into empowerment, keeping partnership, acceptance, and compassion. Option B is the third-edition list, which still appears in most current manuals and syllabi. Option C lists principles from earlier editions rather than the spirit, and option D names the four tasks, not the spirit.

**2. A student writes that an MI conversation is finished once you have worked through engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning in that order. What is the accurate correction?** *(understanding)*

- A. The correct order is focusing, engaging, planning, evoking.
- B. The fourth edition replaced the four tasks with six.
- C. The four tasks recur throughout a conversation, and practitioners return to earlier tasks when engagement, focus, or commitment breaks down.
- D. Planning is optional, and the other three are fixed stages that cannot be revisited.

**Answer: C.** MI describes a circular, fluid model rather than a linear sequence; a practitioner returns to engaging when the relationship frays or to evoking when a commitment weakens. There is no fixed alternative order, the tasks remain four, and none of them is a one-way stage.

**3. Magill and colleagues' 2018 meta-analysis of MI process examined recorded sessions. Which summary of its findings is accurate?** *(analysis)*

- A. Client language was found to be unrelated to what practitioners did in session.
- B. MI-consistent practitioner skills correlated with more change talk and also with more sustain talk, while a higher proportion of change talk was associated with reduced risk behavior at follow-up.
- C. Practitioner empathy ratings predicted outcomes more strongly than any measure of client language.
- D. Change talk predicted better outcomes far more reliably than sustain talk predicted worse ones.

**Answer: B.** The analysis reported r = .55 for MI-consistent skills with change talk and r = .40 with sustain talk, and r = -.16 for proportion change talk with reduced risk behavior. It did not support the relational hypothesis in trial data, and when tested independently sustain talk was the more consistent predictor, of worse outcome, which rules out the other three options.

**4. A student on placement is told to "use some MI" so that a client will agree to a residential program the team has already selected for him. What is the most accurate response?** *(application)*

- A. This is ordinary focusing, since MI always works toward a specific target behavior.
- B. This is appropriate as long as the student believes the program would genuinely help the client.
- C. This is correct practice because the client is ambivalent, which is exactly when evoking is indicated.
- D. Using MI to move someone toward a predetermined outcome is a misuse of a collaborative method, and MI is in any case not a substitute for the care or resources the person may need.

**Answer: D.** MI rests on partnership and on the person's own goals, so deploying it to secure a decision made in advance is a misuse rather than a skilled application; attempting to convince is coded as persuasion, a separate behavior. Good intentions, the presence of ambivalence, and the existence of a target behavior do not change this, and a conversational method does not replace needed treatment or resources.

**5. Which statement best represents what the published evidence supports about MI's effectiveness?** *(understanding)*

- A. MI has been established as effective across essentially all behaviors and settings where it is offered.
- B. MI has substantial randomized-trial support, concentrated in substance use and brief health-behavior work, with typically modest effects that often shrink at longer follow-up and a wider literature graded largely low quality.
- C. MI has shown no benefit over no-treatment control at any follow-up point.
- D. MI is a practice-wisdom approach that has not been examined in randomized controlled trials.

**Answer: B.** Cochrane found effects against no-treatment control that were strongest immediately post-intervention and non-significant at long follow-up, with no significant difference from treatment as usual; a review of 155 meta-analytic comparisons found no high-quality evidence and moderate-quality evidence of small, mainly short-term effects in about 7%. That rules out both the promotional claim in option A and the dismissals in options C and D, since MI has been tested in hundreds of trials and did show short-term benefit.
