# Interest

## In 30 Seconds

Interest is the price paid for borrowing money — and, flipped around, the payment a saver receives for lending it. Borrowers pay interest on top of what they owe; savers earn interest on what they deposit. Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount, while compound interest also pays on previously earned interest. APR (Annual Percentage Rate) states the yearly cost of borrowing, including fees. Rates vary by person, product, and market, so the same amount of money can cost different amounts to borrow — or earn different amounts when saved.

## Why This Matters

Almost every adult money decision touches interest: a car loan, a credit card balance, a savings account, a mortgage. Knowing what interest is — and which side of it you are on — changes how you read any loan or deposit offer. It matters academically because interest sits underneath bigger ideas like compounding and credit. It matters practically because a few percentage points can cost thousands of dollars over the life of a loan. And it matters forward-looking because the same mechanism that makes borrowing expensive is what makes saved money grow.

## Learning Objectives

- Define interest as the price paid for borrowing money or the payment received for lending it, attributing the working definition to SEC Investor.gov.
- Distinguish simple interest, calculated only on the original principal, from compound interest, which also pays on previously accumulated interest.
- Explain what APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is — the annualized cost of borrowing, including interest charges and fees — and why it matters when comparing loans.
- Explain that interest rates vary by borrower, product, and market, and describe how a rate change looks from the borrower's side and the saver's side.
- Apply the simple interest formula (interest = principal × rate × time) to calculate interest in an original scenario.

## The College Version

### What interest is

Interest is the price paid for borrowing money, expressed as a percentage rate over a period of time. That is the working definition used in this lesson, drawn from the glossary of SEC Investor.gov, the U.S. securities regulator's investor-education site. The same payment has a second face: interest is also what a saver or lender receives for letting someone else use money. A bank charges interest on the loans it makes, and it pays interest on the deposits it holds — the rate a bank pays its clients for keeping deposits is, from the saver's side, the reward for lending the bank your money. One transaction, two views: the borrower pays the price of using money now, and the lender collects the payment for giving it up.

### Simple interest

Simple interest is interest calculated only on the original amount, called the principal. It never charges on interest that has already accumulated. The formula is short: interest = principal × rate × time. The rate and the time must be in matching units — a yearly rate calls for time measured in years. If you borrow $2,000 at 5% simple interest for one year, the interest is $2,000 × 0.05 × 1 = $100. The Corporate Finance Institute's explainer makes the same point with its worked examples: interest equals the principal multiplied by the rate multiplied by the number of periods. Because nothing compounds, the interest each period is identical, and the total grows in a straight line with time. This is the one formula this lesson teaches; it is the honest backbone of every other interest calculation.

### Compound interest

Compound interest is interest paid on the principal and on previously accumulated interest — the familiar phrase is “interest on interest.” SEC Investor.gov's glossary defines it exactly that way: interest paid on principal and on accumulated interest. The difference from simple interest shows up over time: simple interest pays only on the original amount, while compound interest pays on the growing balance, so the same rate produces more total interest the longer the money stays. That makes compounding the engine behind long-term savings growth — and the reason debts can balloon if left unpaid. The mathematics of compounding belong to the compound-growth lesson in this course; here it matters as the contrast that defines simple interest, and as the reason the two types of accounts are not interchangeable.

### APR: the yearly cost of borrowing

APR stands for Annual Percentage Rate, and it is the number to read when comparing loans. MyMoney.gov, the U.S. Financial Literacy and Education Commission's site, describes the APR as one of the most important aids when shopping for a loan: it is the total cost, including interest charges and fees, described as a yearly rate. That is why the APR can be higher than the advertised interest rate — the rate is the base percentage on the principal, while the APR folds certain fees into a single annualized figure. The Corporate Finance Institute adds that the APR is the yearly rate of interest paid on a loan, used across mortgages, car loans, and credit cards. Credit cards have their own lesson in this course; here the point is the habit: compare APRs, not just rates, because the APR is closer to what borrowing actually costs in a year.

### Rates vary — by person, product, and market

There is no single interest rate. The rate you are quoted depends on who is borrowing, what the money is for, and the broader market. Borrowers judged riskier pay more: the Corporate Finance Institute states that interest rates are directly proportional to the amount of risk associated with the borrower. The product matters too — a secured loan backed by collateral typically carries a lower rate than an unsecured loan, and the rate a bank pays on savings is usually well below the rate it charges on loans. Markets matter as well: rates rise and fall with economic conditions, which is why a loan quoted today may not match one quoted next year. The theory of how rates are set belongs to the finance subject in this curriculum; here it is enough to know that rates are not fixed facts — they are prices that move, which is exactly why shopping and comparing matter.

### Two views of the same rate, and the honest framing

A rate change moves both sides at once, in opposite directions. Take the borrower's view first: higher interest makes debt costlier. If Rashid borrows $2,000 at 9% simple interest for two years, he owes $2,000 × 0.09 × 2 = $360 in interest. At 12% for the same two years, he owes $480 — the higher rate costs him $120 more for the identical loan. Now the saver's view: higher interest makes saving more rewarding. In an account paying 2%, a $500 balance earns $10 in a year; if the rate rises to 4%, the same $500 earns $20. Same money, same year — double the reward. The honest framing that ties both views together is that interest is the price of time. OpenStax's Principles of Finance calls the interest rate the rental price of money and the reward for delaying consumption: the lender gives up using the money now, and interest is the payment for that wait. Every rate on every offer is that price wearing a percentage.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Interest:** The price paid for borrowing money, or the payment received for lending it, usually expressed as a percentage of the amount involved.
- **Principal:** The original amount of money borrowed or deposited, before any interest is added to it.
- **Interest rate:** The percentage of the principal charged for borrowing or paid for saving, normally stated for a one-year period.
- **Simple interest:** Interest calculated only on the original principal, never on interest that has already accumulated.
- **Compound interest:** Interest paid on the principal and on previously accumulated interest, so the balance grows faster over time.
- **Annual Percentage Rate (APR):** The yearly cost of borrowing, including interest charges and certain fees, expressed as a single percentage.
- **Lender:** The person or institution that provides money to a borrower and receives interest in return.
- **Borrower:** The person who receives money from a lender and pays interest for the right to use it.

## Eli-10

Interest is the fee that comes with time. When you borrow, you are using someone else's money, and the lender charges you for that use — the charge is interest. When you save or lend, someone else is using your money, and they pay you for it. The amount depends on three things: how much money (the principal), the rate (a percentage), and how long (the time). Simple interest charges only on the original amount. Compound interest, a topic of its own, charges on the original amount plus the interest already earned. The honest summary: interest is the price of time — money now is worth more than the same money later.

## Eli's Analogy

**Analogy.** Think of interest as rent on money. You do not own the borrowed dollars — the lender does — and renting them costs a fee, just as renting a house costs rent. Saving works the same way from the other side: when you deposit money at a bank, you are renting your dollars out, and the bank pays you rent in the form of interest. A landlord charges rent for letting someone live in a house; a lender charges interest for letting someone use money.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** The analogy breaks down because rent is usually a fixed monthly amount set in a lease, while interest rates move with markets, the borrower's risk, and the type of product. Rent pays for a house that stays where it is; interest compensates the lender for not having the money available and for the risk the loan may not be repaid. And a house never charges rent on rent — compound interest does exactly that.

## Worked Example

Lena borrows $3,000 from her credit union at 4% simple interest per year to replace her car's transmission, and she plans to repay the loan in 18 months. Step one: write the formula — interest = principal × rate × time. Step two: match the time to the rate. The rate is yearly, so 18 months becomes 1.5 years. Step three: multiply. Interest = $3,000 × 0.04 × 1.5 = $180. Step four: add the interest to the principal. Lena repays $3,000 + $180 = $3,180 in total. Had she stretched the loan to two years, the interest would be $3,000 × 0.04 × 2 = $240 — the extra six months cost $60 more.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating the APR and the interest rate as the same number.** The interest rate is the base percentage on the principal; the APR is the yearly cost including interest charges and fees, so it can be higher. Compare APRs when comparing loans.
- **Using the formula with mismatched time units.** Rate and time must use the same period. A yearly rate needs time in years — 18 months is 1.5 years, not 18.
- **Assuming simple and compound interest grow identically.** Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest, covered in its own lesson, also pays on accumulated interest, so the balance grows faster over time.
- **Assuming one interest rate applies to everyone.** Rates vary by borrower, product, and market conditions. Two people can be quoted different rates on the same kind of loan, so shopping around pays.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Simple interest vs Compound interest:** Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal; compound interest is also calculated on previously earned interest, so the balance grows faster over time.
- **The borrower's view of interest vs The saver's view of interest:** To a borrower, interest is a cost added to what is owed; to a saver, it is a payment received on money deposited — the same mechanism, opposite sides of one transaction.
- **Interest rate vs Annual Percentage Rate (APR):** The interest rate is the base percentage on the principal; the APR expresses the total yearly cost of borrowing, including interest charges and fees, and is the better figure for comparing loans.

## Key Takeaway

Interest is the price of time: borrowers pay it to use money now, and savers earn it for letting money work for them later. The rate decides how much either side pays or receives — simple interest = principal × rate × time.

## Practice Question Bank

1. Jonah takes out a $2,000 personal loan, and his lender charges interest on it. Which statement correctly describes what that interest is?
   - A. A payment the lender owes Jonah for keeping the account open
   - B. The price Jonah pays for borrowing the money
   - C. A refund Jonah receives if he repays the loan early
   - D. The portion of the loan Jonah must repay first before touching the rest
   **Answer: B.** Interest is the price paid for borrowing money, expressed as a percentage rate over a period of time (SEC Investor.gov). The other options describe lender payments, early-repayment refunds, and repayment ordering — none of which is what interest is.
2. Priya borrows $2,000 at 6% simple interest for 3 years. Using the formula interest = principal × rate × time, how much interest will she owe in total?
   - A. $360
   - B. $120
   - C. $720
   - D. $36
   **Answer: A.** Interest = $2,000 × 0.06 × 3 = $360. The $120 figure is only one year's interest, $720 doubles the rate or triples it wrongly, and $36 moves the decimal one place too far.
3. Tia and Uma each deposit $1,000 into separate accounts paying the same interest rate. Tia's account pays simple interest; Uma's pays compound interest. After several years with no withdrawals, which account has the larger balance, and why?
   - A. Tia's, because simple interest is added to the balance more often
   - B. They are equal, because the deposit and the rate are the same
   - C. Tia's, because simple interest never charges fees
   - D. Uma's, because compound interest pays on the principal and on previously earned interest
   **Answer: D.** Compound interest is interest paid on the principal and on accumulated interest (SEC Investor.gov), so Uma's balance grows on the original $1,000 plus everything it has already earned. Simple interest pays only on the original principal, so Tia's balance trails over time.
4. A lender advertises a loan with a 6% interest rate and an APR of 6.9%. Which is the most likely reason the APR is higher than the interest rate?
   - A. The APR is always exactly 0.9 percentage points above the interest rate
   - B. The interest rate applies only to borrowers with strong credit
   - C. The APR includes certain fees on top of the yearly interest charge
   - D. The APR is the rate charged after the loan has been paid off
   **Answer: C.** The APR is the total cost of borrowing, including interest charges and fees, described as a yearly rate (MyMoney.gov), so it can sit above the base interest rate. The other options describe fixed arithmetic, credit tiers, and post-payment charges — none of which explains the gap.
5. A bank raises the rate it pays on savings accounts and the rate it charges on personal loans. Which outcome best describes the effect on a saver and a borrower?
   - A. Saving becomes more rewarding and borrowing becomes costlier
   - B. Saving becomes less rewarding and borrowing becomes cheaper
   - C. Both become cheaper for the consumer
   - D. Neither is affected, because rates and interest are unrelated
   **Answer: A.** Interest has two faces: the rate a bank pays depositors rewards saving, and the rate it charges borrowers prices borrowing. A rise in both makes saving earn more while making debt more expensive — the same rate change, opposite effects.
