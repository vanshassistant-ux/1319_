# Income

## In 30 Seconds

Income is the money a person receives, usually from work or investments — the money coming in. Most people get income from a job, but it also arrives as freelance earnings, interest and dividends on savings, and government benefits. The number on an offer letter is gross income; what lands in your account after taxes and deductions is net income, or take-home pay. Income is the fuel — a budget decides what it powers.

## Why This Matters

Every money decision starts with what comes in. The headline number on a job offer is not the number that pays the rent, and the difference between the two confuses more people than any other money idea. Knowing what income is, which types you have, and what actually reaches your account turns an abstract figure into something you can plan with. It also keeps expectations honest: a salary sounds stable, freelance income varies, and benefits count. Getting income right makes every later topic — budgeting, expenses, saving, taxes — land on solid ground instead of on a number that never existed.

## Learning Objectives

- Define income as money a person receives, usually from work or investments, following how the Census Bureau measures money income.
- Name the four main types of income — wages and salaries, self-employment earnings, interest and dividends, and government benefits — each with one line and an example.
- Distinguish gross income from net income using the before-and-after-deductions distinction.
- Explain take-home pay as the amount of a paycheck that actually reaches the account after taxes and deductions.
- Recognize that income can be irregular for hourly, freelance, and gig workers.
- Explain the honest framing of income as fuel that a budget directs.

## The College Version

### What income is

Income is the money a person receives, usually from work or investments. That working definition follows how the Census Bureau measures money income: income received on a regular basis, before payments for personal income taxes, from sources that include earnings, interest, and dividends. For most people, a paycheck from a job is the largest piece, but income also arrives from side work, from savings and investments, and from government programs. The Census Bureau's list of income sources is a useful map: earnings, interest, dividends, pension and retirement payments, Social Security, unemployment compensation, and public assistance, among others. Income is the money coming in, and everything else in personal finance starts from that.

### The main types of income

Four types cover most of what people receive. Wages and salaries are pay from an employer for work: Lena earns $18.50 an hour as a lab assistant, while her department head is paid a $58,000 annual salary. Self-employment earnings come from running a business or doing freelance work rather than from an employer: Marcus charges $1,200 for a wedding photo shoot, and what he keeps after expenses is self-employment income, generally reported on a 1099-NEC form. Interest and dividends are money paid on savings and investments: a $5,000 savings bond pays about $150 a year in interest, and a shareholder may receive quarterly dividend payments. Government benefits are payments from public programs: a retired worker receives monthly Social Security payments, and a laid-off worker may collect unemployment compensation while looking for work. Most people draw on several of these types at once.

### Gross income vs net income

Gross income is the whole amount earned before anything is taken out; net income is what remains after taxes and deductions. The gap between the two is rarely small. Consider Tomas, whose offer letter says $3,200 a month. His pay stub shows federal and state income tax, Social Security and Medicare contributions, health insurance, and retirement savings taken out of that $3,200 — about $740 in total. His net income is $2,460. The CFPB frames it plainly: the amount you earn from a job is different from the amount you receive in your paycheck. Confusing the two is the most common income mistake there is.

### Take-home pay: the number that matters

Take-home pay is the practical number: the amount that actually lands in your account on payday. MyMoney.gov, the federal money-management site, puts it directly — take-home pay (net income) is what you receive after any taxes and deductions are subtracted. Typical deductions include income taxes, Social Security and Medicare taxes, retirement contributions, and health-insurance premiums. Because benefits and withholding differ, two jobs with the same gross pay can deliver different take-home amounts. When people plan rent, groceries, or savings, the take-home number is the one to use: the gross figure is for understanding the job, and take-home pay is for living on it. The budgeting topic shows what to do with the number once you know it.

### Irregular income

Not all income arrives in steady, equal amounts. Hourly workers' paychecks change when their hours change, and freelance or gig earnings can swing from job to job and month to month. A rideshare driver might bring in $540 one week and $310 the next. Self-employment income is its own category precisely because it does not behave like a salary: no employer sets a fixed rate or a steady schedule. The honest note is simple — irregular income is normal, and it changes how carefully people think about the months when money is thin. Planning for that variability belongs to the budgeting topic; this lesson only names the reality.

### Income and taxes

In the United States, wages, salaries, and tips must be included in gross income, and most income is taxed in some form. How each type is taxed — withholding, estimated payments, credits, and filing — is the taxes topic's job. This lesson's only point is the honest one: the gross number is before taxes, and taxes are a real, ordinary part of the gap between gross and take-home pay. Every dollar named in this lesson, from wages to dividends, meets the tax system somewhere; the details live in the sibling lesson.

### The honest framing

Income is the fuel; the budget decides what it powers. Money coming in does not, by itself, produce security or progress — the same take-home pay can fund rent and savings in one household and leak away in another. That is the reality check of this lesson: income is necessary but not sufficient. Knowing your true take-home number, recognizing the types of income you have, and staying honest about irregular months are the parts you control here. What the fuel powers — housing, groceries, goals, emergencies — is the budget's job, and that is exactly where this subject goes next.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Income** — Money a person receives, usually from work or investments, measured before taxes are paid on it.
- **Wage** — Pay calculated by the hour or by the amount of work done rather than as a fixed annual amount.
- **Salary** — A fixed annual amount of pay from an employer, usually delivered in regular installments.
- **Gross income** — The full amount earned from a source before taxes or other deductions are taken out.
- **Net income** — What remains of earnings after taxes and deductions are subtracted.
- **Take-home pay** — The amount of a paycheck that actually lands in the worker's account, after taxes and deductions.
- **Deduction** — An amount taken out of gross pay, such as taxes, insurance premiums, or retirement contributions.
- **Self-employment income** — Earnings from running a business or freelance work rather than from an employer.
- **Government benefits** — Payments people receive from government programs, such as Social Security or unemployment compensation.

## Eli-10

Income is the money coming in — a paycheck from a job, payment for freelance work, interest on savings, dividends from investments, or benefits from a government program. Two numbers matter for almost every kind of income: the full amount (gross) and what is left after taxes and deductions (net, or take-home pay). Income can arrive in steady equal amounts, like a salary, or it can jump around when hours or gigs change. It is where every money decision starts, but it is not the whole story.

## Eli's Analogy

Picture a hose filling a tank. Income is the water coming in — the flow. The tank has valves that decide where the water goes: one to rent, one to food, one to savings. Without water, nothing flows and the tank stays empty; but a tank that fills quickly still runs dry fast if the valves are wide open. The flow matters, and so does what the tank does with it.

The analogy stops short in a few places. Unlike water, income is not a steady stream — hours change and freelance gigs come and go. And income is not stored like water in a tank: money kept rather than spent is savings, which is its own topic. The hose also has a filter: taxes and deductions come out before the water reaches the tank, which is exactly the gross-versus-net gap.

## Worked Example

Priya works 30 hours a week at $17.50 an hour in a bookstore. Her gross pay is 30 × $17.50 = $525 per week. Her pay stub lists deductions: $62 federal income tax, $40 for Social Security and Medicare, $18 state tax, and $35 for her health-insurance premium — $155 in total. Her take-home pay is $525 − $155 = $370 a week, about $1,480 a month. Her roommate accepted a job advertised at $2,400 gross a month; after that job's deductions, roughly $350, the roommate takes home about $2,050. Same town, different numbers — and the numbers that matter for rent, groceries, and savings are the take-home ones.

## Common Mistakes

1. **Treating the salary in a job offer as the amount that lands in the bank account.** The offer figure is gross income. Taxes, insurance, and other deductions come out first; the amount that actually arrives is take-home pay.
2. **Assuming income only means pay from a job.** Interest and dividends on savings and investments, and government benefits, are also income types with their own examples.
3. **Expecting every month's income to match the last one.** Hourly, freelance, and gig earnings vary with hours and available work; one pay stub describes one pay period, not a guarantee.
4. **Counting borrowed money or refunds of your own money as income.** Loans must be repaid and refunds return your own money; income is money you receive, usually from work or investments.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Gross income vs net income** — Gross is the whole amount earned before anything is taken out; net is what remains after taxes and deductions — the difference is the deduction total, as shown on a pay stub.
- **A wage vs a salary** — A wage is paid by the hour or by work done, so weekly pay moves when hours move; a salary is a fixed annual amount, so each paycheck repeats.
- **Income from work vs income from investments** — Work income trades your time for pay, while investment income is money your saved money produces; both count as income, and both meet the tax system in some form.

## Key Takeaway

Income is the money coming in, usually from work or investments. The number that matters day to day is take-home pay — what actually lands in your account after taxes and deductions. Income is the fuel; a budget decides what it powers.

## Practice Question Bank

1. A friend says she "makes $52,000 a year" at her new job. Which label fits that figure best?
   - A. Her take-home pay
   - B. Her gross income
   - C. Her net income
   - D. Her annual savings
   - **Answer: B.** The salary figure is the full amount offered before taxes and deductions come out, so it is gross income; take-home pay and net income both describe what remains after those amounts are subtracted.

2. Which of the following is income from investments?
   - A. A weekly paycheck from a retail job
   - B. A refund for an overpaid utility bill
   - C. Money borrowed from a relative
   - D. Interest paid on a savings account
   - **Answer: D.** Interest on savings is money received from money already saved, which makes it investment income. A paycheck is income from work, a refund returns your own money, and borrowed money must be repaid, so none of those count as investment income.

3. Kofi's gross pay is $4,800 a month. Taxes and other deductions take out $890. What is his take-home pay?
   - A. $4,800
   - B. $5,690
   - C. $3,910
   - D. $890
   - **Answer: C.** Take-home pay is gross pay minus deductions: $4,800 − $890 = $3,910. The other options confuse the gross figure, an added total, or the deduction amount itself with the net result.

4. Two workers each received $3,000 last month: Ana on a fixed salary and Diego doing freelance design work. Which statement about them is most accurate?
   - A. Diego's income is more likely to change from month to month
   - B. Their income situations are identical because the amounts match
   - C. Ana's income is not taxed because it is a salary
   - D. Diego cannot spend his earnings until the end of the year
   - **Answer: A.** Freelance and self-employment earnings vary with the work available, while a fixed salary repeats each pay period; the equal one-month totals do not make the two situations the same.

5. Which of the following counts as income for the person receiving it?
   - A. A loan repayment from a friend
   - B. Monthly Social Security payments
   - C. A store credit for returned merchandise
   - D. Money found on the sidewalk and returned to its owner
   - **Answer: B.** Social Security payments are government benefits, a recognized income source. Repaying a loan and returning found money do not add income, and store credit replaces a purchase rather than adding money received.
