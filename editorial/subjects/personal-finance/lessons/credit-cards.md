# Credit Cards

## In 30 Seconds

A credit card is a card that lets you borrow money up to a set limit and pay it back later. Each month the card issuer sends a statement of what you owe, with a due date; pay the full balance by then, and the grace period usually means no interest. The costs are named and knowable: interest on carried balances, late fees, annual fees, cash-advance fees. Paying only the minimum keeps the balance alive — for years. Responsible use builds a credit history; misuse is a trapdoor.

## Why This Matters

Almost everyone will hold a credit card at some point, and the gap between using one well and using one badly is one of the largest in personal finance. The mechanics are simple: borrow now, pay later, and the terms are written down in advance. What trips people up is not the mechanics but the rhythm — the billing cycle, the due date, the minimum payment — and the honest fact that the card spends borrowed money, not your money. Learning the cycle and the costs now makes the card a tool; skipping that learning makes it a trapdoor. The same habits, pay on time and pay in full, carry into every other loan a person will ever take.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a credit card as a card that lets a person borrow money up to a set limit, to be paid back later.
- Describe the billing cycle in terms of its three landmarks: the statement, the due date, and the grace period.
- Name the main costs of a credit card — interest on carried balances, late fees, annual fees, and cash-advance fees.
- Explain what a minimum payment is and what paying only the minimum does to a balance over time.
- Distinguish a credit card, which spends borrowed money, from a debit card, which spends your own money.
- Explain how responsible credit-card use builds a credit history, and how that record matters to lenders.

## The College Version

### Borrowing with a card

A credit card is a card that lets a person borrow money up to a set limit and pay it back later. The Federal Trade Commission puts it plainly: when you use a credit card, you are borrowing money to buy something now, and you pay the money back later, usually with interest. The card issuer — the company behind the card — sets a credit limit, which the FTC describes as the most you are allowed to borrow on the card. Spend up to that limit; the limit is the ceiling. Because the money is borrowed, every purchase is a small loan the issuer has already paid on your behalf, and you owe that amount until you pay your bill.

### The billing cycle: statement, due date, grace period

The rhythm of a credit card runs on a billing cycle, usually about a month long. Each cycle ends with a statement: the monthly record of your purchases, payments, fees, and the balance you owe. The statement also names a due date — the day your payment must be received to count as on time (CFPB guidance: generally by 5 p.m. on the due date, or the next business day if the due date lands on a weekend or holiday). Between the end of the billing cycle and the due date sits the grace period. The CFPB defines it as the period between the end of a billing cycle and the date your payment is due, during which you may not be charged interest as long as you pay your balance in full by the due date. Issuers are not required to offer a grace period, but most do on purchases. Pay the full statement balance by the due date and the grace period does its job: no interest on those purchases.

### The costs, named

Credit cards come with costs, and the honest approach is to name them before they surprise you. Interest is what you pay for carrying a balance: if you do not pay the full statement balance by the due date, the unpaid part carries into the next cycle and the issuer charges interest on it — the mechanics of interest belong to the sibling lesson on interest. A late fee applies when a payment arrives after the due date. An annual fee is a flat charge just for holding the card, billed once a year no matter how you use it. A cash-advance fee applies when you use the card to get cash rather than to make a purchase. The FTC lists all of these — annual fees, cash-advance fees, late fees, and interest on unpaid balances — as the typical costs built into credit cards.

### The minimum payment and the honest note

The minimum payment is the smallest amount you can pay each month and keep the account in good standing. It sounds harmless, and that is exactly the problem. The honest note: paying the minimum keeps the balance alive and growing. The CFPB requires issuers to show, on every statement, how long it would take to pay off the current balance if you made no new purchases and paid only the minimum — the disclosure box. An original example: Priya's statement shows a balance of $1,800 and a minimum payment of $35. The box estimates that paying only $35 a month would take more than a decade to clear the balance, while paying $135 a month would clear it in about a year and a half. The figures are illustrative; the box on any real statement gives that card's actual numbers. The CFPB states the general truth plainly: if you make only the minimum payment, it could take years to pay off your credit card.

### Credit cards and credit history

Using a credit card responsibly — paying on time, not owing more than you can repay — builds a credit history, the running record of how you handle borrowed money. The FTC states that paying your bills on time can help you build a good credit history, and that businesses look at that record when deciding whether to do business with you and what to charge. The CFPB adds that a credit history affects the interest you pay and even whether you can get housing, a bank account, a phone plan, or a credit card at all. Late payments and high balances show up as negative items that make credit harder and more expensive to get. The mechanics of the score itself belong to the sibling lesson on credit scores; here the point is the connection — the card is a way to build a record, for better or worse.

### Debit versus credit, and the trapdoor

The distinction that clears up the most confusion: a debit card spends your own money, straight from your checking account; a credit card spends borrowed money that you must repay. The CFPB draws the line in one sentence: prepaid cards and debit cards are ways to spend money you already have, while credit cards are ways to borrow money. Checking-account mechanics belong to the sibling lesson on checking accounts. The honest framing: a credit card is a tool with a trapdoor. Used well — full payment by the due date, costs known, balance watched — it is convenient and builds a record. Used badly — minimum payments only, fees ignored, spending beyond what you can repay — the borrowed money turns into years of payments. The same plastic does both; the difference is entirely in how the cycle is handled.

## Key Vocabulary

- **credit card** — A card that lets a person borrow money up to a set limit from the card issuer and pay it back later.
- **credit limit** — The most money the card issuer allows you to borrow on the card at one time.
- **billing cycle** — The period of time, usually about a month, that a credit card statement covers.
- **statement** — The monthly record of purchases, payments, fees, and the balance owed on a credit card.
- **due date** — The day by which a credit card payment must be received to count as on time.
- **grace period** — The time between the end of a billing cycle and the due date when paying the full balance may avoid interest.
- **minimum payment** — The smallest amount you can pay each month to keep a credit card account in good standing.
- **carried balance** — The part of a credit card balance that is not paid by the due date and moves into the next billing cycle.
- **annual fee** — A flat fee charged once a year simply for holding a credit card.
- **cash advance** — Cash obtained with a credit card, which usually comes with a fee of its own.

## Eli-10

**Simplified explanation.** A credit card is a card that lets you borrow money up to a set limit and pay it back later. The card issuer pays the store for you, and you owe the issuer. Every month you get a statement of what you owe, with a due date. Pay the full amount by the due date and you usually pay no interest, thanks to the grace period. Pay only the minimum and the unpaid balance keeps growing with interest, and the statement tells you it could take years to clear. The costs are named in advance: interest on balances you carry, late fees, annual fees, and cash-advance fees. A credit card spends borrowed money; a debit card spends your own.

**Analogy.** Think of a credit card as a friend who covers your lunch tab. The friend pays the restaurant now, you promise to pay the friend back later, and the deal has a deadline: pay in full by the agreed day and the friend is happy — no extra charge. Pay late, or pay only a little, and the friend adds a fee and starts charging you for every day the rest goes unpaid. Pay only the smallest amount allowed and the tab grows faster than you can chip away at it.

**Where the analogy breaks down.** A friend usually forgives a slow repayment now and then, and rarely charges interest on it; a card issuer charges interest on any unpaid balance, by the written terms. A friend does not report your payment habits to credit bureaus; a card issuer reports to companies that track credit history, which is why responsible use builds a record. And a friend's limit is informal; a credit limit is a hard ceiling set by the issuer — spend past it and the charge is declined or fees apply.

## Practice Question Bank

1. At its core, what does a credit card do? **A.** It lets you borrow money up to a set limit and pay it back later
2. What is a grace period on a credit card? **C.** The time between the end of a billing cycle and the due date, when paying the full balance may avoid interest
3. Maya's card statement covers purchases from May 5 through June 4, and the payment is due June 26. She pays the full statement balance on June 18. What is the most likely result? **B.** She pays no interest on those purchases, because she paid the full balance by the due date within the grace period
4. Devon owes $1,200 on his credit card and pays only the minimum payment each month. What does the honest note about minimum payments say will happen? **D.** The balance stays alive and shrinks very slowly, with interest piling on, so it can take years to pay off
5. Nia buys a $90 jacket. With her debit card, $90 leaves her checking account immediately. With her credit card, what happens instead? **B.** The card issuer pays the store the $90, and Nia owes the issuer that amount until she pays her bill

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating a credit card like a debit card and spending as if the money were already yours.** — Remember the card spends borrowed money: the issuer paid the store, and you owe it back by the due date.
- **Paying only the minimum and assuming the balance is shrinking at a healthy pace.** — Read the disclosure box on the statement; it shows how many years of minimum payments the current balance would take to clear.
- **Confusing the due date with the date the next statement arrives.** — The due date is fixed on the statement and usually falls weeks before the next statement; paying after it triggers a late fee and can cost you the grace period.
- **Assuming every credit card has a grace period.** — Issuers are not required to give one; most do on purchases, but each card's terms say whether it does and on what.

## Key Takeaway

A credit card is borrowed money with a limit. Pay the full statement balance by the due date and the grace period protects you; pay only the minimum and the balance can grow for years.
