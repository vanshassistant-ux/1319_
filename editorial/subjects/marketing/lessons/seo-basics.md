# SEO Basics

## In 30 Seconds

SEO — search engine optimization — is the practice of improving a website so search engines rank it higher for relevant queries. Search engines work in three stages: crawling (discovering pages), indexing (analyzing and storing them), and ranking (ordering results by relevance). SEO splits into on-page factors the site controls and off-page factors like links from other sites. The honest part: useful content earns rankings, rankings take time, and nobody can guarantee or buy a top spot.

## Why This Matters

Whenever you search for anything — a recipe, a repair shop, a hiking trail — you are using the system SEO improves. For a business, appearing when customers search is often the difference between being found and being invisible, and search traffic costs nothing per click. Understanding SEO also makes you a sharper reader of search results: you can tell earned relevance from paid placement, and you can spot the difference between honest advice and guaranteed-rankings hype. For marketers it is the foundation of digital promotion — the channel that keeps working after the campaign budget runs out.

## The College Version

### What SEO is

Search engine optimization — SEO for short — is the practice of improving a website so that search engines understand it and rank it higher for the queries that matter to the site. Google's SEO Starter Guide, the working reference for this lesson, defines the practice as helping search engines understand your content and helping users find your site through a search engine. Two ideas sit inside that definition. First, understandability: a site must present its subject clearly enough that an automated system can tell what it is about. Second, findability: once the engine understands the site, the site should show up when people search for what it offers. A bakery that sells sourdough does not need to rank for the single word "bread"; it needs to rank for the words its customers actually type, like "sourdough bakery near me".

### How search works: crawling, indexing, and ranking

Google's own documentation describes search as a three-stage process, and it is worth naming each stage because SEO work targets them separately. Crawling is discovery: automated programs called crawlers — Google's is named Googlebot — move from page to page, mostly by following links, and download the text, images, and videos they find. Indexing is filing: the engine analyzes each page's content and stores what it learned in the index, a massive database of pages it can draw from. Ranking is answering: when a user types a query, the engine searches the index and returns the results it judges most relevant, ordered by hundreds of signals. The honest catch is that most pages never finish the journey: Google states plainly that it does not guarantee a page will be crawled, indexed, or served, and that not all pages make it through each stage.

### Keywords: the words searchers use

A keyword is simply a word or phrase a searcher types into a search engine. SEO begins with expecting those words, because people rarely search the way a site describes itself. Consider Gable Street Roasters, a coffee shop: the owner might label a page "Beverage Menu", but a customer brewing at home types "how to make cold brew coffee". Google's guide makes the same point — expect your readers' search terms — because someone new to a topic uses different words than an expert does. Placing the words people actually use in prominent spots such as page titles and main headings helps a page line up with the query. Keyword work is not about repeating a phrase until it loses meaning; it is about noticing the words real searchers type and letting those words shape titles, headings, and text.

### On-page versus off-page

SEO splits into two baskets that are easy to keep apart. On-page SEO is everything the site owner controls on the site itself: page titles, headings, the words in the content, descriptive text for images, and how pages link to one another. Off-page SEO is everything outside the site that signals what others think of it — above all, links from other websites, which serve double duty: they are the main way crawlers discover new pages in the first place, and they are read as a sign that the page is worth visiting. Moz, the company behind the long-running Beginner's Guide to SEO, organizes its whole curriculum around this split: making the site friendly to search engines on one side, and building links and establishing authority on the other. The rule of thumb: on-page is what you say about yourself; off-page is what others say about you.

### Content and SEO

The connection between content and SEO is simple: search engines exist to serve the person searching, so the pages they rank are the ones judged most relevant and useful for the query. Google's key best practice is to create helpful, reliable, people-first content, and its spam policies punish the opposite — pages built mainly to manipulate rankings. That is why useful content earns rankings: a genuinely good answer to a real question satisfies the searcher, attracts links naturally, and lines up with the words people type. Content marketing — creating and distributing useful content to attract an audience — is the sibling practice that supplies that content. This lesson covers the search-engine side of the connection; the content marketing topic covers the craft of making the content itself.

### The honest note and the honest framing

Two honest notes close the lesson. First, rankings take time and change constantly. Google's own guide says some changes show up in hours while others take months, and recommends waiting weeks before judging whether a change worked. Nobody can guarantee a first-page position, and anyone who sells one is selling something Google does not offer: Google does not accept payment to crawl a site more often or rank it higher. Second, SEO is earning relevance, not gaming the system. The practice rewards sites that focus on the best content and experience for people; tactics designed to trick the ranking systems fall under spam policies that can drop a page or remove it from results altogether. The realistic picture is steady, honest work: make the site clear, useful, and linkable, then wait, watch, and keep improving.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Search engine optimization (SEO)** — The practice of improving a website so search engines can understand it and rank it higher for relevant searches.
- **Crawler** — An automated program a search engine sends out to discover and download web pages by following links.
- **Index** — A search engine's large database of pages it has analyzed and can draw from when answering a query.
- **Ranking** — The order in which a search engine lists results for a query, based on how relevant it judges each page to be.
- **Keyword** — A word or phrase that a searcher types into a search engine to find information.
- **On-page SEO** — Search improvements made on the website itself, such as page titles, headings, and the words in the content.
- **Off-page SEO** — Search factors outside the website, mainly links and mentions from other sites, that signal how others regard the page.
- **Organic results** — The unpaid listings a search engine returns for a query, ordered by relevance rather than by payment.

## Eli-10

Every time you search, the answer list already exists: the search engine has spent years reading pages and filing them by topic before you ever type a word. SEO is the work of making sure your site is filed under the right topics and that it honestly deserves a good spot. You cannot buy a better spot, and nobody can promise you one. What you can do is keep your site clear, keep it useful, and let other sites point to it — the same qualities that make a neighbor recommend a shop to a friend.

## Eli's Analogy

Picture a farmers' market with hundreds of stalls. The market keeps a master map of every stall and what it sells, and shoppers ask the information desk for directions. Crawling is the market staff walking the aisles to see what is new. Indexing is writing each stall onto the map. Ranking is the information desk deciding which stalls to point a shopper toward first. SEO is doing the things that get your stall onto that map under the right heading — a clear sign (your page title), goods that match what shoppers ask for (your content), and other vendors pointing shoppers your way (links from other sites).

The analogy breaks down in two ways. A farmers' market has one map and rules you can read; search engines keep their ranking formulas secret and change them regularly, so a spot you earn today can shift tomorrow. The market also shows every stall to every shopper, while search engines decide on your behalf what counts as relevant — your job is to send clear signals, not to control the outcome.

## Worked Example

Meadow Bakery's website has a page titled "Our Breads" that lists the menu, yet the shop stays invisible when customers search. The owner checks what people actually type: "sourdough bread near me" and "how to keep sourdough fresh". She rewrites the page title to "Sourdough Bread in Fairview — Baked Daily", adds a heading and a short, genuinely useful guide to storing sourdough so it stays crusty, and replaces the image descriptions with plain words. A few weeks later the local food blog links to the storage guide. Three months on, the page shows up for "sourdough bread near me" — not because anyone paid for a spot, but because the page became understandable, useful, and linked. It still drifts in the rankings from week to week; no one promised otherwise.

## Common Mistakes

- **Paying someone who guarantees a number-one ranking.** No one can guarantee a ranking position, and Google does not accept payment to rank pages higher; treat guarantees as a warning sign.
- **Stuffing a page with the same keyword over and over.** Google's spam policies can drop pages that exist mainly to manipulate rankings; write naturally for people and use keywords where they belong — titles, headings, and honest text.
- **Treating paid ads and SEO as the same thing.** Ads buy placement and stop when the budget stops; organic rankings are earned and keep working, but they take time.
- **Writing pages for the search engine instead of the person.** Search engines rank what they judge most relevant for the user, so helpful, people-first content is the core best practice.
- **Setting up SEO once and forgetting it.** Search behavior and ranking systems change constantly; SEO is ongoing — refresh content, watch what searchers type, and keep improving.

## Compare / Contrast

- **On-page SEO vs. Off-page SEO** — On-page is what the site itself controls — titles, headings, and wording; off-page is what others say about it — chiefly links from other websites.
- **Organic search results vs. Paid search ads** — Organic results are earned through relevance and cannot be bought; paid ads are purchased placement, covered in the digital advertising topic.
- **SEO vs. Content marketing** — SEO makes a site findable by search engines; content marketing creates the useful content that earns attention and links. They work together but are separate practices.

## Key Takeaway

SEO is earning relevance, not buying it: make your site understandable and genuinely useful, let the words searchers use guide your titles and headings, and accept that rankings take time, change constantly, and can never be guaranteed.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **Google's SEO Starter Guide defines search engine optimization (SEO) as what?**
   - A. Helping search engines understand your content, and helping users find your site through a search engine
   - B. Paying a search engine to display your site above its competitors
   - C. Buying advertisements that appear beside search results
   - D. Guaranteeing a website a first-page position within two weeks
   - **Answer: A.** Google's starter guide defines SEO as helping search engines understand your content and helping users find your site. Paying for placement describes advertising, not SEO — Google does not sell rankings — and no one can guarantee a position. *(recall)*

2. **In what order do the three main stages of how Google Search works happen?**
   - A. Indexing, then ranking, then crawling
   - B. Ranking, then indexing, then crawling
   - C. Crawling, then indexing, then ranking
   - D. Crawling, then advertising, then ranking
   - **Answer: C.** Google's documentation names three stages in order: crawling discovers pages, indexing analyzes and stores them in the index, and ranking matches a query against the index and returns ordered results. Advertising is not a stage of search. *(understanding)*

3. **Riverside Cycles posts a page titled "Chain Repair Kit Details" but notices nobody finds it, while customers search for "how to fix a slipped bike chain". Which change best follows how SEO treats keywords?**
   - A. Pay the search engine to list the page first for bike-chain searches
   - B. Rename the page "How to Fix a Slipped Bike Chain" and write the guide people actually search for
   - C. Repeat the phrase "chain repair kit" as many times as possible on the page
   - D. Remove the words "bike" and "chain" so the page appeals to more searches
   - **Answer: B.** SEO starts from the words searchers actually use — here, the question-style query about a slipped chain — and places them in titles and headings. Paying for placement is advertising, and repeating or deleting keywords does not earn relevance. *(application)*

4. **A florist rewrites her page titles to name the flowers customers search for, and a local newspaper's website links to her shop. Which statement sorts these efforts correctly?**
   - A. The newspaper link is an on-page factor and the rewritten titles are off-page
   - B. Both are on-page factors because the florist started them
   - C. Both are off-page factors because other people are involved
   - D. The rewritten titles are on-page factors and the newspaper link is off-page
   - **Answer: D.** On-page SEO is what the site itself controls — here, the page titles and the words on the site. Off-page SEO is what happens outside the site — here, the link from the newspaper, which is also how search engines discover new pages. *(analysis)*

5. **A consultant promises a bakery that it will reach the first page of Google "within two weeks, guaranteed." What should the bakery expect, based on how search engines actually work?**
   - A. The promise is reliable, because consultants control how search engines rank pages
   - B. The promise is reliable, because Google sells first-page positions
   - C. No one can guarantee rankings, and changes typically take weeks to months to show up
   - D. Rankings update instantly once a page is published, so the promise is easy to keep
   - **Answer: C.** Google states that it does not accept payment to rank pages higher, that meeting best practices does not guarantee crawling, indexing, or serving, and that changes can take from hours to several months. A guaranteed two-week first-page promise contradicts all of that. *(analysis)*
