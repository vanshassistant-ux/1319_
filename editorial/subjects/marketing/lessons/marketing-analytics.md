# Marketing Analytics

## In 30 Seconds

Marketing analytics is measuring and analyzing marketing data to understand what is working and to inform decisions. The core metrics: reach (how many different people saw something), impressions (how many times it was shown), engagement (likes, shares, comments), clicks (how many people clicked), and conversion rate (the share who did the desired thing). Dashboards gather these metrics into one view. The honest limit: numbers show what people did, rarely why. A/B testing compares two versions to see which performs better.

## Why This Matters

Why measure? Because a bakery that checks its numbers and one that guesses spend the same effort, but only one of them learns. Analytics turns scattered activity into a running scoreboard: which channels bring clicks, which offers convert, which experiments deserve repeating. It matters academically because every decision in this curriculum — pricing, promotion, placement — eventually gets judged by data. Practically, it separates repeating what works from repeating what does not. And as a consumer you will see marketing numbers everywhere; knowing what a metric can and cannot claim keeps you from being swayed by a big, shiny impression count.

## The College Version

### What marketing analytics is

Marketing analytics is the practice of measuring and analyzing marketing data to understand what is working and to inform decisions. The American Marketing Association treats it as a distinct practice area within marketing, with its own training, certifications, and events, built around questions like "what did marketing actually contribute?" The raw material is marketing metrics — measurable, quantifiable values that marketers track to assess marketing performance, in the wording of OpenStax's Principles of Marketing. The working definition used in this lesson follows the same arc: analytics is measuring and analyzing marketing data to inform decisions. It is not a hunch dressed up in a spreadsheet; it starts with numbers that describe what people actually did.

### The core metrics: reach, impressions, engagement, clicks, conversion rate

Five metrics do most of the work, and each answers one question. Reach: how many different people saw the content? Impressions: how many times was it displayed? Engagement: how many people took an action on it — liking, sharing, commenting? Clicks: how many people clicked the link or the ad? Conversion rate: of the people who could have done the desired thing, what share actually did? A Meadow Bakery post about a new sourdough loaf might reach 8,000 people while racking up 21,000 impressions, because the platform shows it to the same people more than once — reach counts heads, impressions counts showings. Engagement counts the likes, shares, and comments; clicks count the people who went further. Conversion rate is the one that needs arithmetic: divide the number of people who completed the desired action by the audience size and multiply by 100. If 40 of 800 email subscribers ordered the loaf, the conversion rate is 40 ÷ 800 × 100, or 5 percent. What counts as a conversion — a purchase, a signup, a download — depends on the goal; the conversion lesson in this curriculum goes deeper, and here the point is the metric itself.

### Dashboards: one view of many metrics

A dashboard gathers the metrics a team has chosen to watch into a single view. OpenStax describes the modern version as real-time marketing dashboards that give instant visibility of the metrics you have selected to track — a quick overview of where you stand and how you are progressing toward your goals. Instead of opening one spreadsheet per channel, a roaster looks at a single screen on Monday morning: coffee subscriptions added, email signups, clicks on the roast-of-the-month page, orders by channel. Dashboards make ongoing monitoring cheap. They do not change what the numbers can say — a dashboard is a way of looking at metrics, not a new source of meaning.

### Analytics versus market research

Marketing analytics and market research both work with information, and the line between them is worth drawing. Analytics is ongoing measurement: it watches what is happening with your marketing as it happens, week after week. OpenStax puts it plainly — metrics tell us what is happening at any point in time. Market research, by contrast, is a designed study that answers a specific question at a point in time; OpenStax's research chapter describes it as the way marketers listen and assess the needs of the market so managers can make data-driven decisions instead of assumptions. Bluebird Bakery ran a one-time survey last spring to choose a new cupcake flavor — research. Its website dashboard, checked every Monday, is analytics. The two feed each other: research can explain what analytics reveals. The market-research lesson in this curriculum owns that topic; here, the distinction is the point.

### What metrics can and cannot say

The honest limit of every number in this lesson is the same: metrics record behavior, not motives. A thousand clicks on an ad for Harbor Books' reading challenge tell the bookstore that a thousand people clicked; they do not say why the other nine hundred forty people who saw the ad did not, and they do not say whether the ad persuaded anyone. OpenStax makes the point about impressions directly: an ad view does not indicate an intent to purchase, and there is no way of knowing whether the ad was persuasive or changed behavior. Metrics tell you the what; they rarely tell you the why. That is why teams test. A/B testing compares two versions of a page, email, or ad by showing each to a random slice of the audience and seeing which performs better — two variants, random split, compare results. The why still needs judgment, follow-up questions, and sometimes research. Analytics is a scoreboard, not a mind reader.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Marketing analytics** — Measuring and analyzing marketing data to understand performance and inform decisions; a recognized practice area of the American Marketing Association.
- **Metric** — A measurable, quantifiable value that marketers track to assess how marketing is performing.
- **Reach** — The number of different, unique people exposed to a piece of content or a campaign.
- **Impressions** — The total number of times content or an ad is displayed, counting repeated views by the same people.
- **Engagement** — Audience actions taken on content, such as likes, shares, comments, and retweets.
- **Click-through rate** — The percentage of people who see an ad or link and click on it.
- **Conversion rate** — The percentage of an audience that completes a desired action, calculated as completions divided by audience size.
- **Marketing dashboard** — A single view that gathers the metrics a team has chosen to monitor, updated on a regular basis.
- **A/B testing** — Comparing two versions of a page, email, or ad by showing each to a random slice of the audience to see which performs better.

## Eli-10

Marketing analytics is keeping score of your marketing. After a bakery posts a video, someone counts how many people saw it, how many times it appeared, how many liked or shared it, how many clicked the link, and how many actually bought something. Those counts are metrics. A dashboard puts them on one screen so the baker can see at a glance whether things are improving. When the bakery is unsure which of two ads works better, it can show each to half its audience and compare — that is A/B testing. One rule keeps it all honest: the numbers show what people did, but they do not say why.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a scoreboard at a soccer match. It shows the score, the shots, the corners — everything that has happened. The coach does not need to ask anyone how the game is going; the board answers. But the scoreboard will not tell you why the team is losing: whether the midfield is tired, the wing is off form, or the other side is simply better. That is for the coach to figure out.

The analogy has limits. A scoreboard is fixed and official, while a marketing team chooses its own metrics — two teams can count "reach" differently, so a number only makes sense when you know how it was defined. A scoreboard measures the same rules for both teams, but marketing data comes from platforms that change their definitions and from people whose behavior rarely matches their stated motives.

## Worked Example

Harbor Books, an independent bookstore, posted a video of a staff member recommending a summer novel. The platform reported a reach of 5,000 — that many different people saw the post. Impressions came to 12,000, because many viewers saw the video more than once. Nine hundred people liked, commented, or shared: engagement. Two hundred forty clicked the link to the book's page: clicks. Of those 240 clickers, 36 added the book to a cart and bought it. Conversion rate = 36 ÷ 240 × 100 = 15 percent. The team learned the video moved viewers to the page well — and that the number says nothing about why the other 204 clickers left without buying.

## Common Mistakes

- **Treating reach and impressions as the same number.** Reach counts different people; impressions counts every display, including repeats. Impressions is usually the larger number, and the gap between the two is the repeat viewings.
- **Celebrating a huge impression count as proof a campaign is working.** An impression only means the content was displayed. It carries no information about attention, interest, or intent to buy.
- **Expecting the numbers to explain themselves.** Analytics reports what happened — clicks, signups, purchases — not why. Explaining the why needs judgment, follow-up questions, or research.
- **Confusing analytics with market research.** Analytics measures performance continuously. Market research is a designed study that answers a specific question at a point in time.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Marketing analytics versus Market research:** Analytics measures performance continuously with metrics like reach and conversion rate; research is a designed study that answers a specific question at a point in time.
- **Reach versus Impressions:** Reach counts how many different people saw the content; impressions count every time it was displayed, including repeats.

## Key Takeaway

Marketing analytics measures what is working by tracking reach, impressions, engagement, clicks, and conversion rate in one dashboard view — but it records what people did, not why they did it.
