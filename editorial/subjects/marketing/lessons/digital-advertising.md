# Digital Advertising

## In 30 Seconds

Digital advertising is paid advertising delivered through websites, apps, and platforms: display banners, search ads, social media ads, and video ads, each placed where a target audience already spends time. Advertisers aim ads at specific audiences using demographic data, interests, and behavior, and much ad space is sold by auction. Every ad produces countable numbers — impressions, clicks, conversions — which makes digital advertising measurable attention rather than a guess.

## Why This Matters

Most of the ads you meet online are digital ads, and they now absorb the majority of advertising spending. Knowing how they work — how an ad gets chosen for a page, how it is aimed at an audience, and what the numbers actually mean — makes you a more careful reader of everything you see online. For marketers, the appeal is measurement: instead of guessing how many people saw a billboard, a digital campaign produces exact counts of displays, clicks, and actions. For consumers, the same mechanics raise the question this lesson keeps in view: no matter how an ad is delivered, it must still be identifiable as an ad.

## Learning Objectives

- Define digital advertising as paid advertising delivered through websites, apps, and platforms, and distinguish it from unpaid digital promotion.
- Name the four main digital advertising formats — display/banner, search, social, and video — and state in one line what each does.

- Apply the FTC disclosure rule: explain why digital ads must be identifiable as ads.

## The College Version

### What digital advertising is

Digital advertising is the paid corner of digital marketing: advertising messages delivered through websites, apps, search engines, social platforms, and other digital media. OpenStax's Principles of Marketing frames the practice with its definition of advertising — paid communication messages that identify a brand or organization and are intended to reach a large number of recipients — and its description of digital media as any advertising done via the Internet, mobile phones, and devices other than television and radio. Investopedia's definition of the wider field agrees: digital marketing is the use of websites, apps, mobile devices, social media, search engines, and other digital means to promote and sell products and services, and paid advertising is one of its channels. The word paid does the work: a business's own social posts and its search-engine-optimized pages cost time and effort, not placement fees; digital advertising buys placement.

### The formats

Digital advertising comes in four standard shapes. Display ads — also called banner ads — are graphic boxes of images, text, or both, placed on websites and apps; Meadow Bakery runs a rectangular banner on a neighborhood news site's homepage. Search ads are text ads shown alongside search-engine results when someone searches a related term; Harbor Books' ad appears above the list when a shopper types “independent bookstore near me.” Social media ads are paid posts or sponsored content inside social feeds, marked as sponsored; Riverside Cycles promotes a free tune-up day into the feeds of local cycling fans. Video ads are commercials that play before, during, or beside video content; a short Bluebird Bakery spot runs before a recipe video on a cooking channel. Investopedia lists pay-per-click advertising and video platforms among the standard digital vehicles; OpenStax notes marketers create sponsored content they pay for on social platforms. In one line each: display interrupts with a graphic, search answers a query, social rides the feed, video borrows the screen.

### Targeting: how an ad finds its audience

Unlike a billboard, a digital ad can be aimed. OpenStax's Principles of Marketing describes how marketers build campaigns that target demographic attributes such as gender, age, and location, plus psychographic attributes such as interests and viewing behavior. Investopedia describes the same mechanics for pay-per-click campaigns, which can segment users by demographic characteristics (such as age or gender), particular interests, or location. Behavior is the extra ingredient: what a person has searched, watched, or clicked tells the system what they likely want next, so a cycling shop's ad can follow someone who just searched for bicycle repair. That is the mechanics of targeting, stated plainly: audience data — who people are, what they like, what they do — decides which ad shows to whom. This lesson takes no position on the privacy questions this raises; that belongs to the ethics-in-marketing topic.

### The auction: how ad space is sold

Much digital ad space is not sold at a fixed price; it is auctioned. When a page or app is about to load an ad slot, advertisers' systems bid for that particular showing, and the winning bid's ad appears. The technical name is real-time bidding: advertising inventory is bought and sold on a per-impression basis through an instantaneous programmatic auction, and when a bid is won, the buyer's ad is displayed at once. The auction explains a familiar experience — the same ad slot showing different ads to different people — because each showing is a separate sale to whichever bidder valued that viewer most. Stated simply: ad space is sold by bidding, per showing, in fractions of a second.

### Measurement: impressions, clicks, conversions

Digital advertising is countable. An impression is one instance of an ad being displayed to a user; Investopedia defines it as each instance an advertisement or webpage is displayed on a digital platform. A click is one person choosing the ad; OpenStax treats the number of times audience members click an internet ad as the measure of its engagement. A conversion is the desired action after the click — a purchase, a signup, a download; Investopedia defines conversion rate as the percentage of people who took a desired action, such as a purchase, relative to the audience the ad reached. Conversion mechanics are a sibling topic; here, conversion is the third number in the measurement trio. Reading the three together tells a campaign's story: 5,000 impressions and 25 clicks means most viewers never clicked; four of those clicks ending in a signup says the ad attracts the right few.

### The disclosure rule and the honest framing

Digital ads must be identifiable as ads. The FTC, the U.S. agency that enforces truth-in-advertising law, states the rule plainly: an ad is deceptive if it promotes goods or services but is not readily identifiable to consumers as an ad, and advertisers must ensure ads are identifiable as advertising before consumers reach the ad's main page. The FTC's .com Disclosures guidance adds that consumer protection laws apply online and disclosures must be clear and conspicuous — a label such as “Ad:” or “Sponsored” tells consumers a message is advertising. FTC staff guidance to the search industry makes the same point: paid results must be clearly distinguishable from natural results. The honest framing: digital advertising is measurable attention. The numbers are real — every impression, click, and conversion is counted — but they measure attention and action, not meaning: ten thousand impressions mean an ad was shown ten thousand times, not that ten thousand people wanted to see it. Precise measurement of attention is still no guarantee of a sale.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Digital advertising** — Paid advertising delivered through websites, apps, search engines, social platforms, and other digital media; the paid-advertising slice of digital marketing.
- **Display ad** — A graphic advertisement — images, text, or both — placed on a website or app; also called a banner ad.
- **Search ad** — A text advertisement shown alongside search-engine results when a person searches a related term.
- **Sponsored post** — A social media post a brand pays to place or promote inside users' feeds, typically marked as sponsored.
- **Video ad** — A commercial that plays before, during, or beside video content on a digital platform.
- **Targeting** — Choosing which people see an ad, using demographic data, interests, and behavior.
- **Impression** — One instance of an advertisement being displayed to a user on a digital platform.
- **Click** — One person clicking on an ad; the standard countable measure of engagement with an ad.
- **Conversion** — A desired action a person takes after clicking an ad, such as a purchase, signup, or download.
- **Ad auction** — A bidding process in which advertisers compete, per showing, for the right to display an ad.

## Eli-10

Digital advertising is paying to put your ad where people already are online: on websites, inside apps, next to search results, in social feeds, and before videos. The ad can be aimed — a bike shop can show its ad mostly to people who search for bike repair — and the space is often sold by auction, with advertisers bidding per showing. Every time the ad appears, that is an impression; every click is a click; every purchase or signup after that is a conversion. All three are counted automatically, which is why digital advertising is called measurable attention.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a busy train station where advertisers rent small display boards, one passerby at a time. Each time a commuter walks past, the station runs a quick auction: the advertiser who bids the most for that commuter's attention wins the board for that moment. The station records how many commuters saw each ad, how many stopped to look, and how many walked into the shop afterward.

_Where the analogy breaks down:_ The auction runs in milliseconds, run by computers rather than people, and the highest bid does not always win — relevance and quality can matter too. And the station's counts describe attention, not meaning: a thousand passersby is not a thousand customers, just as a thousand impressions is not a thousand sales.

## Worked Example

Meadow Bakery runs its first digital campaign. It places a display banner on a neighborhood news site, a search ad for “bakery near me,” and a short video ad before a cooking show. In one week the numbers come back: the banner was displayed 4,000 times (impressions), 40 people clicked it, and 9 of those clicks led to a newsletter signup — a small conversion count. The search ad drew 120 clicks from 800 impressions. The owners read the numbers honestly: most people who saw the ads did not click, and clicks outnumbered conversions by a wide margin — but for the first time the bakery knows exactly how many people each ad reached, which ad pulled better, and what a click costs. Next month they move budget toward the search ad.

## Common Mistakes

- **Mistake:** Treating impressions as results. **Fix:** An impression only means an ad was displayed. A thousand impressions can produce zero clicks; read clicks and conversions alongside impressions before judging a campaign.
- **Mistake:** Hiding that an ad is an ad, on the theory that a disguised ad works better. **Fix:** Under FTC truth-in-advertising law, an ad that is not readily identifiable as an ad can be deceptive. Clear labels such as “Ad” or “Sponsored” are the rule, not a penalty.
- **Mistake:** Judging a campaign by one number. **Fix:** Impressions, clicks, and conversions tell different parts of the story; a cheap click that never converts is worth less than a pricier click that does.
- **Mistake:** Assuming targeting guarantees buyers. **Fix:** Targeting narrows who sees an ad using demographics, interests, and behavior; it raises the odds of relevance, but it does not create demand or guarantee a sale.
- **Mistake:** Treating every online ad format as interchangeable. **Fix:** Display, search, social, and video ads each fit a different moment — interrupt, answer, ride the feed, borrow the screen. The format should match the job.

## Compare / Contrast

- **A display ad** versus **A search ad:** A display ad interrupts whatever a person is reading with a graphic box; a search ad answers a query the person typed, appearing alongside results for that term.
- **An impression** versus **A click:** An impression counts a display of the ad; a click counts someone actually choosing it. Impressions measure reach; clicks measure engagement.
- **A paid search ad** versus **An organic search result:** A paid search ad is advertising — the advertiser pays for placement, and FTC guidance requires it to be distinguishable from natural results; an organic result is ranked by the search engine's own relevance process, not bought.

## Key Takeaway

Digital advertising is paid, targeted, auctioned, and counted: paid ads delivered through websites, apps, and platforms, aimed at specific audiences, sold by bidding, and measured in impressions, clicks, and conversions — attention you can count, and ads that must stay identifiable as ads.

## Practice Question Bank

**1. Per this lesson, what is digital advertising?**

A. Paid advertising delivered through websites, apps, search engines, and other digital media ✓
B. Free content a brand publishes to attract readers and build trust
C. Any message a company posts on its own social media profiles
D. Advertising broadcast over television or radio airwaves

*Answer: A. The lesson defines digital advertising as paid advertising delivered through websites, apps, platforms, and other digital media — the paid slice of digital marketing. Unpaid content and a brand's own social posts are not paid advertising, and television or radio are traditional media.*

**2. Meadow Bakery's ad appears as a box of images and text at the top of a recipe website, while Riverside Cycles' ad shows up as a short commercial before a bike-repair video. Which two digital advertising formats are these?**

A. A search ad and a social media ad
B. A display ad and a video ad ✓
C. A social media ad and a search ad
D. Two display ads in different sizes

*Answer: B. A graphic box on a website is a display (banner) ad, and a commercial that plays before video content is a video ad. Search ads accompany search results, and social ads ride inside social feeds, so the other pairings mislabel both.*

**3. Gable Street Roasters wants its ad shown mainly to people who searched “coffee roaster near me” in the past hour. Which mechanism makes that possible?**

A. Buying a fixed banner slot for a full month on a coffee blog
B. Running a video ad before every video on a cooking site
C. A search ad triggered by the search term, aimed at people with that demonstrated behavior ✓
D. Posting the offer on the roastery's own social profile and hoping customers share it

*Answer: C. Search ads appear alongside results for a specific term, and targeting uses behavior — here, the recent search — to decide who sees the ad. A fixed banner buys placement for everyone on the page, a video ad borrows a screen, and an unpaid post is not advertising at all.*

**4. Bluebird Bakery's campaign produced 5,000 impressions, 25 clicks, and 4 newsletter signups. Which reading of these numbers is correct?**

A. The ad reached 5,000 paying customers, and 4 of them signed up
B. All 25 people who clicked were shown the ad 5,000 times
C. Every impression was a person who deliberately looked at the ad
D. Most people who saw the ad did not click it, since impressions count displays, not attention ✓

*Answer: D. An impression is one display of the ad, not a person choosing to look, so 25 clicks out of 5,000 impressions means most viewers did not click. Signups are conversions, a separate and smaller count. The other options confuse impressions with clicks or treat displays as customers.*

**5. A brand pays a cooking site to publish an article that looks exactly like the site's own recipes, with no label saying it is an ad. Under the FTC rule this lesson teaches, what is the problem?**

A. There is no problem — matching the site's style is smart targeting
B. The ad will be charged per impression instead of per click
C. An ad that is not readily identifiable as an ad can be deceptive under truth-in-advertising law ✓
D. The article will be outranked by organic search results

*Answer: C. The FTC states that an ad is deceptive if it promotes a product but is not readily identifiable to consumers as an ad, and advertisers must ensure ads are identifiable as advertising. Matching a site's style without a disclosure is exactly the disguised-ad situation the rule addresses; targeting and pricing are different questions.*
