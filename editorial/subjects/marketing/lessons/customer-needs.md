# Customer Needs

## In 30 Seconds

A customer need is a problem to solve or a desire to satisfy: something a person or business lacks and is willing to trade value for. This working definition comes from OpenStax's Principles of Marketing, where buyers bring a want or need and trade value to satisfy it. A need is not a want; the need is the essential, and the want is the specific form it takes. Needs are functional, emotional, and social, and they change across people and over time. Businesses find them by observing, asking, and reading feedback. Guessing usually misses.

## Why This Matters

Every product you have ever bought answered a need, or failed to. When a business understands the real need, the product feels obvious and the sale feels easy; when it guesses, shelves fill with things nobody wanted. Understanding needs makes you a sharper customer, because you can see the problem behind the pitch. And if you run a business, launch a product, or work with customers in any role, finding out what people actually need is the difference between building something people use and building something people ignore.

## Learning Objectives

- Define a customer need using the working definition drawn from OpenStax's Principles of Marketing: a problem to solve or a desire to satisfy that a customer is willing to trade value for.
- Distinguish needs from wants and give an original example of each.
- Name the three need categories, functional, emotional, and social, with an original example of each.
- Explain how observation, conversation, and feedback help a business find customer needs, and why guessing instead tends to fail.
- Explain why needs differ across people and change over time, and connect needs to the value a product creates.

## The College Version

### What a customer need is

This lesson's working definition comes from OpenStax's Principles of Marketing. In its account of the exchange process, the buyer "has a want or need" and is "willing to pay money or provide other personal resources to satisfy this need or want." So a customer need is a problem to solve or a desire to satisfy: something a person or business lacks and is willing to trade value for. The same text warns that needs are not always easy to read. Some customers have needs they are not fully aware of, and others cannot put their needs into words at all. The American Marketing Association's definition of marketing, "institutions and processes that create, communicate, deliver, and exchange offers carrying value for customers, clients, partners, and society at large," points the same direction: marketing exists because customers have needs that offerings can satisfy. A practical test falls out of this: would the customer trade money, time, or effort to close the gap? If yes, it is a need worth marketing to. OpenStax also separates the buyer from the user: the customer purchases the product, while the consumer uses it, as when a grandmother buys a toy for her grandson.
### Needs versus wants

A need is the essential; a want is the specific form you would like it in. OpenStax's Principles of Marketing makes the same point from the other side: "marketing does not create needs; rather, it opens consumers' eyes to their wants." Hunger is a need; the burrito you choose at lunch is a want. The same need can wear many wants: two commuters both need to get to work, and one wants a monthly bus pass while the other wants a parking spot near the office. In practice the line is blurrier than the definitions suggest, and OpenStax lists five types of customer needs that show why. Stated needs are what the customer asks for, like telling a salesperson you "need a new phone." Real needs are what the request actually means, such as long battery life or a better camera. Unstated needs are what the customer expects but does not say, like good service from the carrier. Delight needs provide the "wow" factor that makes one offer more desirable than another. Secret needs are motives the customer will not admit, such as wanting a phone as a status symbol. The lesson to draw: responding only to the stated need may not satisfy the customer.
### Functional, emotional, and social needs

A useful way to group needs is by what they are about, and the grouping lines up with the value types OpenStax names when it discusses customer value. Functional needs are about what the product does; OpenStax describes functional value as "what the product does for the customer in terms of solving a particular want or need." A student who needs a backpack that carries a heavy laptop without tearing has a functional need. Emotional needs are about how the buyer wants to feel; OpenStax describes psychological value as how much the product allows the customer to feel better. A new parent who needs to feel calm at the end of a long day has an emotional need. Social needs are about connection; OpenStax describes social value as how much owning the product allows the customer to connect with others. A rider who needs to feel part of the weekend group ride has a social need. Few purchases are purely one kind. A commuter bike is functional because it gets the rider to work, emotional because the ride makes the rider feel free, and social because it keeps the rider in the conversation at the shop. The marketer who designs for all three creates more value than the one who designs for one.
### Finding needs, and the honest truth about guessing

OpenStax's Principles of Marketing is blunt about how hard needs are to read: "some customers have needs of which they're not fully aware; others can't articulate their needs." That is why needs are found rather than assumed. Observation means watching what customers actually do, because what people do often differs from what they say. Conversation means asking, and asking again; OpenStax says the marketer must "probe further" when an answer is vague, such as a customer who asks for an "inexpensive hybrid vehicle." Feedback means reading surveys, reviews, complaints, and returns for what fell short. The SBA's Business Guide names the formal toolkit, surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, and in-depth interviews, and notes that asking consumers yourself gives a nuanced understanding of your target audience; that formal study is market research, which is its own topic in this subject. The honest note is that needs are not fixed. They differ across people, which is why OpenStax says marketers "must increasingly respect individual preferences," and they drift over time as lives, seasons, and trends change, which is why the SBA advises keeping up with current trends. The uncomfortable truth follows: a business that guesses needs guesses wrong often. The fix is not cleverness; it is listening.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Need** — A problem to solve or a desire to satisfy that a customer is willing to trade value for; the working definition in this lesson comes from OpenStax's Principles of Marketing.
- **Want** — The specific form a need takes, shaped by taste, culture, and circumstances; the burrito chosen to satisfy hunger is a want.
- **Functional need** — A need about what a product must do for the customer, such as a backpack that carries a heavy laptop without tearing.
- **Emotional need** — A need about how the buyer wants to feel, such as wanting calm at the end of a long day.
- **Social need** — A need about connection with others, such as wanting to feel part of the group on a weekend ride.
- **Stated need** — What the customer explicitly asks for, which may not capture the real need underneath the request.
- **Value** — The benefit a customer gets from a product relative to its cost; OpenStax writes it as benefits divided by price.
- **Value proposition** — A short promise stating what the customer wants and how the product meets it better than the alternatives.
- **Exchange** — The act of trading something of value, such as money, for a product or service that satisfies a need or want.

## Eli-10

A customer need is a gap: something you lack that you want fixed. Marketing starts at that gap. Needs are not the same as wants; the need is the empty space or the job that must get done, and the want is the particular thing you reach for. Needs come in three flavors: functional (what the product does), emotional (how it makes you feel), and social (how it connects you to others). Businesses find needs by watching, asking, and listening to feedback, because people often cannot say exactly what they need. And needs are not fixed: they change with age, season, and circumstance, so the business that keeps listening keeps winning.

## Eli's Analogy

Think of a need as thirst. Thirst is the need: it is real, it must be dealt with, and it does not care what brand you drink. The want is the drink you choose, iced tea on a hot afternoon, hot cocoa in winter, water at the gym. The same thirst can be satisfied a hundred ways, and the drink that delights you today may bore you tomorrow. A smart drink stand does not just sell liquid; it watches which drinks people order on hot days, asks regulars what they crave, and notices when a new flavor gets ignored. That is observation, conversation, and feedback at work.

The analogy has limits. The analogy breaks down because thirst is physical and easy to name, while many needs are not. Emotional and social needs, feeling secure, feeling respected, feeling part of a group, are harder to put into words, and some customers will not admit them. Also, unlike thirst, needs do not arrive on a fixed schedule; they shift with life changes, which is why businesses keep checking.

## Worked Example

Riverbend Books noticed something odd: lunchtime shoppers browsed the cookbook table, then left with nothing but coffee. Instead of guessing, the shop watched, and people lingered longest near the window bench and the cookbook shelf. Then it asked: a few regulars said they had no time to cook, they just liked reading recipes. Finally, feedback: an online review mentioned that the shop felt cramped. The real need was not more cookbooks; it was a comfortable place to sit with a coffee and a cookbook. The shop added two armchairs and a small reading table, and cookbook sales rose along with the seating. The product only created value once it matched the actual need.

## Common Mistakes

- Treating every want as a need: "everyone needs my product" is a want dressed up, and the need behind it is the problem the product solves; if that problem is small, the want will not survive a price check.
- Listening only to the stated need: OpenStax warns that responding only to what customers ask for may not satisfy the customer, because the real, unstated, or secret need gets missed.
- Assuming needs are the same for everyone: needs differ across people and situations, so a product built for one group can miss another completely.
- Believing needs never change: needs drift with seasons, life stages, and trends, so a business that stops listening sells yesterday's answer to today's problem.
- Building first and asking later: a business that guesses needs guesses wrong often, and the honest move is to observe, ask, and read feedback before committing money.

## Compare / Contrast

- **Need vs. Want:** A need is the essential, a problem to solve or a desire to satisfy that a customer will trade value for; a want is the specific form that need takes, shaped by taste, culture, and circumstance. The same need can be satisfied by many wants.
- **Stated need vs. Real need:** A stated need is what the customer explicitly asks for; the real need is what the request actually means. OpenStax warns that responding only to the stated need may not satisfy the customer.
- **Finding needs vs. Guessing needs:** Finding needs uses observation, conversation, and feedback; guessing needs relies on assumption. OpenStax notes that customers often cannot articulate their needs, so guessing misses the real problem, and a business that guesses needs guesses wrong often.

## Key Takeaway

Marketing starts with a need, not a product. A need is a problem to solve or a desire to satisfy, functional, emotional, or social, and it changes across people and over time. Find it by observing, asking, and listening to feedback, because guessing needs is how shelves end up full of things nobody wanted.

## Practice Question Bank

1. **At its core, a customer need is:**
   - A problem to solve or a desire to satisfy that a customer is willing to trade value for
   - Any product that a business wants to sell
   - The same thing as a want, just stated more formally
   - A complaint a customer makes after a purchase
   **Answer:** A problem to solve or a desire to satisfy that a customer is willing to trade value for
2. **Which pair shows a need followed by the want that expresses it?**
   - A parking spot, then the ride to work it enables
   - Getting to work on time, then a monthly bus pass
   - A bus pass, then the fare it covers
   - A bus route, then the driver who runs it
   **Answer:** Getting to work on time, then a monthly bus pass
3. **Maya buys the same running shoes as her training group because she wants to feel like part of the group on Saturday runs. Which category of need is she mostly satisfying?**
   - Functional
   - Emotional
   - Social
   - Stated
   **Answer:** Social
4. **A gym adds more treadmills because the owner assumes members "need" more machines. Membership stays flat. Conversations reveal that members skip workouts because the gym feels unwelcoming. What does this situation show?**
   - The gym correctly identified a stated need
   - Marketing can create needs where none existed
   - Needs never change, so the machines were the right call
   - Guessing at needs missed the real problem members had
   **Answer:** Guessing at needs missed the real problem members had
5. **Harbor Books wants to learn what its lunchtime customers need. Which action is an example of finding needs through observation?**
   - Watching which shelves shoppers linger at before deciding what to stock
   - Asking the distributor to ship a wider variety of cookbooks
   - Lowering the price of coffee to draw more foot traffic
   - Running a social media contest for a free book bundle
   **Answer:** Watching which shelves shoppers linger at before deciding what to stock
