# Market Research

## In 30 Seconds

Market research is gathering and analyzing information about customers, competitors, and the market before you build or sell. It comes in two flavors: primary research, new data you collect yourself, and secondary research, existing data you use from others. The honest truth: people do not always say what they do, and research informs decisions without making them. Think of it as a flashlight, not a crystal ball — it lights the next step without guaranteeing the future.

## Why This Matters

Why ask before building? Because guessing is expensive. A food cart owner who skips research can order three hundred pounds of a filling nobody buys, while a rival who spent an afternoon asking commuters what they actually eat at lunch makes the same bet with evidence. Research is how small, cheap questions get answered before big, expensive ones get asked. It also makes you a sharper customer: when you know how surveys and focus groups work, you can spot the difference between a real signal and a stacked deck.

## The College Version

### What market research is

The American Marketing Association defines marketing research as the function that links the consumer, customer, and public to the marketer through information — information used to identify and define opportunities and problems, generate, refine, and evaluate actions, monitor performance, and improve understanding of the process. In plainer words, market research is gathering and analyzing information about customers, competitors, and the market to support decisions. Investopedia describes it as a process that gathers and analyzes data for a company's product strategy, and CFI frames it as the way a company determines whether a new product or service is viable. The SBA's Business Guide adds the practical purpose: research blends consumer behavior and economic trends to confirm and improve a business idea, and it lets a business reduce risk while the idea is still taking shape. Notice what the definition does not say: research is not reading minds, and it is not a promise that a product will sell. It is organized curiosity — turning questions into information you can act on.

### Primary versus secondary research

Research splits into two families. Primary research is new data you collect yourself — or pay a firm to collect for you — directly from the source. Secondary research is existing data someone else already gathered, which you reuse. The SBA puts it plainly: you can do market research using existing sources, or you can do the research yourself and go direct to consumers. An original example: a climbing gym that wants to add a bouldering wall could run a primary survey of its 400 members asking which weekday evenings they would train, and pair that with secondary data such as neighborhood population counts from the Census Bureau. The trade-offs matter. Existing sources are fast and often free, but as the SBA warns, the information might not be as specific to your audience as you would like. Collecting your own data is tailored and current, but it takes time, money, and care. CFI adds a useful split inside primary research: exploratory research asks open questions to surface issues and problems, and specific research digs into the issues that exploratory work revealed.

### The methods: surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation

Four methods do most of the work, and each answers a different kind of question. A survey asks a fixed set of questions to many people at once — for example, a bike shop emailing 2,000 riders to rate how likely they are to buy a $900 commuter model. An interview is a one-on-one conversation that goes deep — a founder spending twenty minutes with each of eight former customers to learn exactly why they stopped ordering. A focus group gathers a small group, typically six to ten people, to react to an idea together, like a bakery testing three cake flavors with a dozen birthday-party planners and listening to them argue about the frosting. Observation watches what people actually do instead of asking — a coffee shop counting how many customers linger past 4 p.m. before deciding whether to add an evening menu. Surveys reach many people cheaply, interviews and focus groups reach few people deeply, and observation catches behavior that words hide.

### The process, and its honest limits

The research process in this lesson runs in four steps: define the question, gather data, analyze, and decide. The AMA's definition describes the professional version of the same arc — it specifies the information required, designs the method for collecting it, manages and implements the collection, analyzes the results, and communicates the findings and their implications. Step one is the one most often skipped, and skipping it is costly: a question like 'would people buy this?' is too vague to research, while 'would at least 300 downtown office workers buy lunch from a cart at this corner on Tuesdays?' is answerable. Two honest limits keep research humble. First, people do not always say what they do: respondents tend to answer in ways that look good to others, so stated interest can run ahead of real purchases. Second, data informs, but it does not decide. A survey can show that 60 percent of students want longer library hours; it cannot tell the library what to do about its budget. Research is a flashlight, not a crystal ball: it lights up the next step of the path, but the walking — and the judgment — is still yours.

## Key Vocabulary

- **Market research** — Gathering and analyzing information about customers, competitors, and the market to support business decisions.
- **Primary research** — New data collected directly by you or by a firm you hire, such as running your own survey.
- **Secondary research** — Existing data that someone else already gathered, such as census statistics or industry reports, which you reuse.
- **Survey** — A fixed set of questions given to many people at once to collect their answers in a consistent way.
- **Interview** — A one-on-one conversation in which a researcher asks open questions and follows up on the answers.
- **Focus group** — A small group, typically six to ten people, brought together to react to a product, idea, or message.
- **Observation research** — A method that watches what people actually do rather than asking them what they say they do.
- **Response bias** — The tendency of people to answer survey questions in ways that look good to others rather than truthfully.

## Eli-10

Market research is asking before building. Instead of guessing what customers want, you gather information about them, about rivals, and about the market, then use it to make a smarter choice. The work comes in two forms: primary research, where you collect brand-new data yourself, and secondary research, where you use data other people already collected. Four methods do most of the asking: surveys, interviews, focus groups, and watching what people actually do. The process has four steps — define the question, gather data, analyze, decide. And it has honest limits: people do not always say what they do, and research helps you decide without deciding for you.

Imagine walking into a dark room. Research is a flashlight. It does not move the furniture or choose your seat; it just shows you where the chairs are, where the rug ends, and where the table leg sticks out. You still decide where to sit — but you no longer have to trip over things to find out.

The flashlight analogy has limits. A room stays still while you shine a light in it; markets keep moving. People change their habits, rivals change their offers, and last month's data can be stale by next quarter. A flashlight also cannot see around corners: research describes what people say and do now, but it cannot guarantee what they will do tomorrow. That is why the honest phrase is "flashlight, not crystal ball."
